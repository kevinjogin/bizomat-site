This is an illustration. On Tue 3 Aug 2027, the Minister's office sent the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO) a bundle of letters from councils and freight operators about bridges on council roads that were closed or carried load limits. The last harvest season had also brought media stories about grain trucks and livestock carriers taking long detours. The office asked NRIO for advice on "a fund to fix regional bridges".

NRIO is a fictional non-corporate Commonwealth entity, the same agency as in the other books in this series. The request went to Grace Mitchell, Policy Manager (EL1) in the Strategy and Policy Branch, in NRIO's Office of the CEO. Her team is Tom Becker (APS 6), Senior Policy Officer for evidence, data and economic analysis, and Amara Osei (APS 5), Policy Officer for consultation, drafting and Cabinet material. Zoe Fraser (APS 6), a Finance Business Partner in the CFO area, joins when the work needs a costing.

The quickest reply would have been a design for a fund. Grace's first brief to the Minister, ten days later, treated a fund as one possible answer and set out to find the question. This chapter follows that work, from the request to a problem statement and objectives.

## A solution dressed as a problem

Most policy work starts with a request, and most requests arrive as solutions. They name an instrument, a target and an action, and say little about what is wrong, for whom, or why.

This is an illustration. "A fund to fix regional bridges" names an instrument (a fund), a target (regional bridges) and an action (fixing). It assumes that the bridges are in poor condition, that money for works is what is missing, and that the Commonwealth should supply it. Each could be true, but none had been tested. If any one is false, a well-run fund could spend all its money and leave the problem where it was.

Three questions expose a solution in disguise. Take the instrument out: is anything left to describe? Does the request say who is worse off, and by how much? Could a different instrument get the same result at lower cost?

Reframing a request is not refusing it. Section 13(j) of the [Australian Public Service Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) (the Directions) says that upholding the APS Value of Committed to Service requires "being responsive to Ministers, including being knowledgeable about the Government's policies and understanding the relevant issues and options". Responsiveness includes understanding the options, not only carrying out the first one named. Section 17A(a) adds, for Stewardship, "having proper regard to known and reasonably foreseeable implications of advice, decisions, and other actions". A works fund chosen before anyone knows which bridges need work has a foreseeable implication: some of the money goes to the wrong bridges.

So Grace treated the request as evidence: something real lay behind the letters, and her job was to find out what.

## Symptoms, causes and effects

This is an illustration. The letters described symptoms: load limit signs on bridges trucks had used the season before, long detours, and a closure on a road a small community depends on. A symptom is what people see, a cause is why it happens, and an effect is what it costs, and whom. Policy aimed at symptoms tends to move them around.

Grace's team drew a problem tree. From the letters and from what NRIO already knew about its program area, they described the chain like this. Regional councils own most of the bridges and major culverts on local roads, and many have no current engineering assessment of the load a bridge can carry. A council unsure of a bridge has two choices that look safe: close it, or put a precautionary load limit on it. Heavy vehicles then take long detours. And a council that has not assessed its bridges may leave open one that is in fact unsafe, because nobody has looked.

That chain changes the question. If councils do not know the safe load of their bridges, the first need may be knowledge, not concrete and steel. Some limited bridges may be sound once assessed, others may need strengthening, and a few may need closing. A works fund spent first cannot tell them apart.


Two cautions apply. Causes have causes, so stop where government action could make a difference and evidence can reach. And keep money apart from safety: a detour costs money, an unsafe bridge could cost lives, and they call for different measures and urgency.

## Who is affected, and by how much

A problem statement should say who bears the problem and roughly how big it is.

This is an illustration. In August 2027 Grace had groups but no numbers, so she listed each group, how it was affected and how NRIO would size it.

| Group | How it is affected | How NRIO would size it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy vehicle operators | Extra distance and time on detours | Detour lengths, operators' figures, an estimate of yearly cost |
| Councils that own the bridges | Safety and liability risk, and assessments they find hard to buy | A survey of every council |
| Farm businesses and communities | Freight costs passed on; less reliable access | Through operators' and councils' evidence |
| Other road users on detour routes | More heavy vehicles on their roads | Recorded as not yet measured |
| State and territory road agencies | Their own roles on the wider network | A roundtable, in general terms |

Size has three parts: how many (bridges, councils, trips), how much (dollars, hours, risk) and who carries it, since a cost spread thinly across many people differs from the same cost falling on a few. Write down what you do not know as plainly as what you do. "We do not yet know how many bridges carry precautionary limits" is a finding.

Do not fill the gaps with numbers from the letters. A council that writes "half our bridges are unsafe" may mean that half have not been assessed. That is a different claim, and the difference is the whole problem.

## Why government should act at all

A problem does not by itself justify government action. A brief should say why the problem will persist without new action, and why the Commonwealth should act. There are three common reasons, and one honest alternative.

This is an illustration. Here, councils own the bridges and state and territory road agencies have their own roles.

Market failure means that decisions left to each party give the community a worse result than they could. Two forms fit. The first is missing information: those who would gain from knowing a bridge's safe load are mostly not those who would pay for the assessment. The second is costs that fall on others. A council bears the safety and liability risk, so a precautionary limit is sensible for it, while the detour costs land on operators who have no say.

Equity is about fairness. A small council may own many bridges, and an assessment takes a far larger share of its budget than of a city council's.

Risk is about harm that people cannot see or manage for themselves. An unsafe bridge left open is a risk to everyone who crosses it, and nobody can judge it from the driver's seat.

The alternative is that none of these holds up: the problem is small, councils are already fixing it, or another level of government is better placed. Then the right advice is no new Commonwealth action, which is a real option (Option A, the base case, in Chapter 3). Government action has costs too. A reason to act is a reason to look at options, not a promise that any will be worth its cost; Chapter 4 tests that.

This is an illustration. Grace's brief judged market failure the strongest reason, and said that equity and risk were plausible but unmeasured.

## Writing the problem statement

A problem statement is one or two sentences that describe a condition, its main cause and its effect on identified people. It names no instrument. Five tests help:

1. It describes a condition in the world, not a missing program. "There is no fund" is never a problem.
2. It names a cause that evidence could confirm or refute.
3. It names who is affected, and how.
4. It can be measured, so you will know whether things improve.
5. At least two genuinely different options could address it, including no new action.

This is an illustration. Grace's working statement in the brief read: "Many council bridges in the program area appear to carry precautionary load limits, or to be closed, because councils do not know their safe load. The limits impose detour costs on heavy vehicles, and some unsafe bridges may remain open. We do not yet know how many bridges are affected, why assessments are not done, or what the limits cost." The last sentence is unusual, and deliberate: it tells the Minister why the next step is evidence rather than a design.

It passes the five tests: a condition, a testable cause, named people, things that can be counted, and room for assessments, standards, works or no new action. A fund is still possible; it is simply no longer assumed. The hedges, "appear to" and "may", are accuracy about the evidence, not weakness. Chapter 2 shows how the statement changed once the evidence came in.

## Objectives that leave the answer open

Objectives say what would be different if the problem were solved. Like the problem statement, they name no instrument. "Establish a bridge fund" is an output, something government would do. "Councils know the safe load of their priority bridges" is an objective, a change in the world.

This is an illustration. Grace's brief proposed three objectives, to be tested with the evidence:

1. Councils know the safe load of the bridges that matter most for freight and access.
2. Precautionary limits and closures that are not needed are lifted or eased, and limits that are needed stay.
3. Unsafe bridges are found and acted on.

Each traces back to the problem statement, each could be met in more than one way, and each can be measured: the share of priority bridges assessed, the share of precautionary limits lifted or eased, and unsafe bridges found and acted on. Chapter 10 returns to these measures.

The second objective deliberately cuts both ways. The goal is not to lift limits but to get them right. An objective that counted only lifted limits would reward the wrong behaviour.

There are no targets yet. A target such as "assess so many bridges by a given year", set before anyone knows the numbers, the costs or the supply of engineers, would be a guess presented as a commitment. Targets come with the options in Chapter 3.

## The brief of 13 August

This is an illustration. Grace's two-page brief went to the Minister on Fri 13 Aug 2027, cleared by the Director, Strategy and Policy. She sent it quickly rather than wait for a full paper, because the office needed to know within days what NRIO proposed to do.

Its purpose was to propose how NRIO would develop advice on bridge load limits on council roads. Its recommendation asked the Minister to agree that NRIO define the problem and test the evidence before advising on funding options, and to note that an options paper would arrive by the end of October.

The key points did four things. They acknowledged that the letters described a real problem. They reframed the request without dismissing it: "A fund may be part of the answer. Before money is committed, we need to know which bridges are affected, what is wrong with them, and whether money for works is what is missing." They set out the working problem statement, the three objectives and the reasons the Commonwealth might act, each marked as a hypothesis. And they gave the work plan and its dates.

## When it goes wrong: "not enough grant money"

This is an illustration. In March 2025, another NRIO team sent the Minister's office a proposal to double a small grant program that helped councils reseal and light remote airstrips, from A$4.0 million to A$8.0 million a year for four years. That is an extra A$4.0 million a year, or 4 × A$4.0 million = A$16.0 million in all. The problem statement was one line: "Councils do not have enough grant money to keep remote airstrips safe."

Its evidence was demand. The latest round had received 61 applications seeking A$12.2 million against A$4.0 million available: A$12.2 million ÷ A$4.0 million = 3.05 times oversubscribed.

The office sent it back with three questions. How many earlier projects were finished? Why were the rest not? And what would another A$16.0 million buy that the first A$4.0 million a year had not?

NRIO's own grant records held the answers, and they did not help. Of the 18 projects funded in the 2023 round, 7 were unfinished two years later: 7 ÷ 18 = 38.9 per cent. Councils had handed back A$0.9 million unspent. The reasons on file were few contractors willing to work at remote sites and councils with no staff to manage projects. More money would have bought more unfinished projects.

The proposal went no further. The cost was a lost year for the airstrips' real problem, weeks of discarded work, and an office that read NRIO's next proposal with more doubt.

Three controls would have caught it. First, a cause test: "not enough money" is almost never a cause, but the price of a solution someone has already chosen. Keep asking why until the answer is something money, rules or information could change. Second, read your own records first: oversubscription measures demand for money on offer, not need. Third, a reader from outside the team who asks for the evidence behind the cause.

Grace's team now writes a one-page problem definition for every new piece of policy work: the condition, the cause and its evidence, who is affected, what is not known, and at least two options it allows. Someone outside the team reads it before options work starts.

## Exercise: rewrite three problem statements

This is an illustration. The bundle the Minister's office sent on Tue 3 Aug 2027 held three more letters, each with its own diagnosis.

| Letter | Statement |
| --- | --- |
| A, from a freight industry body | "Councils put load limits on bridges for no good reason. The Commonwealth should stop them." |
| B, from a council association | "Councils need more money to employ bridge engineers." |
| C, from a regional council | "Small councils need a grant to buy asset management software." |

For each, identify the solution it contains and the cause it assumes. Rewrite it as a problem statement that passes the five tests. Then name a measure of the problem's size and at least two different options your rewrite leaves open.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

**A.** The solution is regulation: the Commonwealth overriding councils' limits. The assumed cause is that councils act without reason. But a council that does not know a bridge's safe load has good reason for caution, because it bears the risk if the bridge fails. Rewrite: "Councils place precautionary load limits on bridges whose safe load they do not know. Heavy vehicle operators bear the detour costs of limits that an assessment might show are not needed." Measure: the number of precautionary limits and the detour costs they impose. Options: assessments that let councils lift or keep limits on evidence, a shared data standard, or no new action. An override would move the risk without reducing it.

**B.** The solution is money for staff, and the assumed cause is that councils cannot afford engineers. If engineers are scarce, money alone will not produce more of them, and councils bidding against each other could simply push up the price. Rewrite: "Councils cannot get their bridges assessed, because assessments are costly and bridge engineers are scarce in regional areas." Measure: the share of bridges with no load assessment in the last 10 years, and the reasons councils give. Options: grants for councils to buy assessments, a central service that buys them at scale, training and guidance, or no new action.

**C.** The solution is software, and the assumed cause is a missing tool. But software holds data; it does not create it. A council that has never assessed its bridges will have an empty field in a new system. Rewrite: "Some councils cannot say which bridges they own, or when each was last assessed, so they cannot plan assessments or works." Measure: the share of councils that can give the date of the last assessment for each structure. Options: a data standard and guidance, assessments that produce the missing data, grants for systems, or no new action.

In each rewrite the instrument came out, a testable cause went in, the people affected were named, and at least two options remain. Together, the letters describe one problem from three sides: assessments that are costly and hard to get, records too thin to plan with, and the limits that follow when nobody knows a bridge's safe load. That is Grace's working statement. Each writer saw one part and attached a solution to it, which is a fair concern and a weak basis for policy.

Chapter 2 turns to the evidence that tested these causes: a survey of 142 councils and three roundtables.
