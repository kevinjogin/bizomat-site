## About this book

Most manufacturers meet the building code only a few times in their working lives: when they lease a bigger shed, add a mezzanine, carve an office out of a workshop or buy a building that was designed for someone else. Each time, decisions that look like operations choices turn out to be building code questions. How big can the mezzanine be? Is the roller door an exit? How many people does the code assume will work in the machine shop? Why does the certifier want a wall where the estimator drew a partition?

This book works through those questions in the order a project meets them. It is written for owners, operations managers and project leads, not for certifiers or fire engineers. The aim is to let you read a set of drawings, run the main checks yourself, and arrive at the certifier's office with sensible questions instead of surprises.

You need no previous building experience. You do need to be comfortable with areas, volumes and simple geometry, and willing to read a code clause slowly. Each chapter ends with an exercise and a worked answer. Read the chapters in order the first time; later, use them as checklists.

## The running case

Every chapter follows the same fictional project. This is an illustration. A precision-engineering business has bought a single-storey steel portal-frame building on a regional New South Wales industrial estate. The building is 60 m long and 30 m wide, a floor area of 1,800 m². The walls are 6.0 m high at the eaves and the roof rises to 7.5 m at the ridge. The owner wants a 300 m² office at the front, a 1,500 m² workshop containing a machine shop and a fabrication and assembly area, and a storage mezzanine inside the workshop. The first sketch shows that mezzanine at 240 m².

The case is invented. Its numbers were chosen to land near real code limits so that the checks matter. It is not a BIZOMAT project, and none of its outcomes describe a real building, certifier or council.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

All code values in this book come from the National Construction Code 2022, Volume One, read on NCC Online in October 2026. NSW is due to adopt NCC 2025 from 1 May 2027, so a NSW project assessed in 2026 would normally still use NCC 2022. Other states and territories may already be on NCC 2025, and every jurisdiction has its own variations and additions. Values are paraphrased with their clause numbers so you can check them against the edition that applies to you.

The supplied BCA 2004 volumes were used only to explain how the performance-based code developed. Referenced Australian Standards are named but not quoted.

This book is educational. It does not replace the applicable NCC edition, state legislation, a registered building certifier, a fire safety engineer or a structural engineer. Building approval decisions are made by the appropriate authority for your project, and the calculations here are teaching checks, not a design.
