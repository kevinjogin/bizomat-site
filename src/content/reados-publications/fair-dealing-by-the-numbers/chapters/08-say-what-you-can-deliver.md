The last test of fair dealing on the tank job is a sentence spoken on the phone. The customer's operations manager, worried about the old tank, asks whether the new one could be ready in two weeks instead of three. The owner, wanting to help and to keep a good customer, says: "Two weeks, no problem." It is the kind of thing said a hundred times a day in businesses everywhere, and it is the subject of this chapter.

Aquinas holds that every lie is wrong, because words are naturally signs of what is in the mind, and to signify what is not in one's mind misuses them (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.110 a.3). He distinguishes three kinds of lie: the officious lie, told to help or to spare someone; the jocose lie, told in fun; and the mischievous lie, told to harm (II-II q.110 a.2). The mischievous lie is the gravest, but the officious lie, the helpful one, is still a lie. He also draws a distinction that every business needs: it is lawful, he says, "to hide the truth prudently", by keeping it back, but never to assert what is false (II-II q.110 a.3, reply to objection 4). This chapter applies both points to the delivery promise.

## Was the promise true?

A promise about the future is not a lie merely because it later turns out wrong. Plans fail. But a promise is a statement about the speaker's present belief and intention: "I believe I can and I intend to." If the speaker has no grounds for the belief, the statement signifies something that is not in the speaker's mind, which is Aquinas's definition of a lie. Australian law takes a similar view in commercial settings: a representation about a future matter, such as a delivery date, can be misleading if the person making it did not have reasonable grounds for it.

So the question is whether "two weeks" had grounds. The owner had not checked. The workshop's capacity had just been allocated in Chapter 6, with the second welder committed to the quarry's guard and the dealer's frames.

The BIZOMAT [schedule risk check](/tools/schedule-risk/) compares remaining work with productive capacity before a deadline. It multiplies the people available by their productive hours a day and a planning efficiency, then by the working days remaining, and reports capacity as a percentage of the work, with the working days the work needs. It rates coverage of 115% or more as low delivery risk, 100% to 115% as tight and under 100% as high delivery risk.

This is an illustration. The tank needs 62 hours of work. One fabricator is assigned to it, with 6.5 productive hours a day available for the tank including the overtime agreed in Chapter 6, and the planner uses an efficiency of 80%, so 5.2 productive hours a day go to the tank.

| Promise | Working days | Capacity | Coverage | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Two weeks | 10 | 52 h | 84% | High delivery risk; needs 11.9 days |
| Two and a half weeks | 12 | 62.4 h | 101% | Tight; 0.4 hours of buffer |
| Three weeks | 15 | 78 h | 126% | Low delivery risk; 16 hours of buffer |
| Two weeks with two fabricators | 10 | 104 h | 168% | Low risk, but the second fabricator is committed elsewhere |

The promise of two weeks had no grounds. With the capacity actually available, the tank needs about 12 working days, and a date with any buffer at all is three weeks. The only way to make two weeks work would be to take the second fabricator off the quarry's safety guard or the dealer's now-confirmed frames, which would break the allocation Chapter 6 made on a fair basis. Making one customer's promise true by quietly making another's false is not a solution.

## A failure: the helpful promise

The owner's "no problem" was an officious lie in Aquinas's sense: not malicious, not even consciously false, but said to please and to spare the customer worry, without the grounds that would make it a sincere statement of belief. Its effects would not have been kind. The customer would have planned its production around a date the workshop could not meet, and would have found out on day 10, when it was too late to make other arrangements.

The error was detected within the hour, because the workshop's practice is that every date given to a customer is entered in the production plan, and the planner, entering this one, saw the conflict. The correction was to call the customer back the same afternoon. The owner said that he had answered too quickly, that the work needed about 12 working days with the people available, and that the workshop could commit to three weeks from the approval of the drawings, with a buffer for the polishing. He offered to keep the customer informed weekly, and to tell them at once if anything threatened the date. He confirmed it in writing the same day.

The customer was disappointed and grateful in about equal measure. A disappointing date given on the day of the question is something a customer can plan around. A comfortable date that fails without warning is not.

## A rule for dates

The workshop now has a simple rule: no delivery date is given to a customer until it has been checked against the production plan. When a customer asks on the phone, the answer is "I'll check the plan and confirm within the hour", and the hour is kept. Customers almost never object. Most have been given too many unchecked dates by other suppliers to mind waiting an hour for one that holds.

## Keep the promise true as things change

A promise that had grounds when it was made can lose them. Materials arrive late, a subcontractor slips, a test fails. Truthfulness does not end when the date is given. It continues in what the workshop says, and when, as the job progresses.

The workshop's weekly update to the customer gives the planned date, the hours remaining and any risk to the date. It also commits to telling the customer at once if a risk appears between updates. That commitment was tested at the end of the job. The day before delivery, the crane hire company told the workshop that it had double-booked the truck. The owner spent the evening trying to find another crane, and called the customer only the next morning, when the customer's installer was already on the road. The installer charged a call-out fee for the wasted visit, which the workshop reimbursed, as Chapter 7 described.

The workshop's mistake was not the double-booking, which was the crane company's, but the delay in saying so. A call the evening before, even without a solution, would have let the customer stand the installer down. The owner's reluctance to call until he had an answer is common and understandable. It turns a supplier's problem into the customer's cost. The workshop's update rule now says that a known risk to a date is reported the same day it becomes known, with or without a solution.

## Keeping back is not the same as saying false

When the owner explained why two weeks was not possible, he did not say that the second fabricator was working on a safety guard at a particular quarry, or that a friend's trailer frames had been confirmed that morning. Those facts belong to other customers, and the workshop keeps them confidential. He said that the workshop had other committed work that week and could not reassign people from it.

That is Aquinas's prudent reticence: keeping the truth back without asserting anything false. The customer was told everything it needed to make its own decisions, which was the date and the reason it could not be earlier, without being told things that were not its business. The same principle lets the workshop keep its costs and margin confidential, as Chapter 3 noted. Reticence becomes deception only when what is kept back makes what is said misleading.

## What else counts as a promise

Delivery dates are the most common promises a workshop makes, but not the only ones. Quotes, websites and conversations make many statements that customers rely on: lead times, capacities, materials, finishes, warranties, certifications, "we've done many of these". Each is a statement about what is in the business's mind, and each should be true.

The tank quote now includes four statements that the workshop has checked it can stand behind:

1. **The date.** Three weeks from drawing approval, based on a capacity check with 16 hours of buffer.
2. **The description.** Grade 316, 3,002 litres working capacity at the customer's freeboard, internal welds at Ra 0.8 micrometres or better, as established in Chapter 4.
3. **The design basis.** Legs and base plates checked for a product density of 1,200 kg per cubic metre, as established in Chapter 5.
4. **The records.** A data book with mill certificates, test results and any repairs, as Chapter 5 described.

None of these is marketing. Each is a promise with grounds, and each can be checked.

The same discipline applies to the general claims a business makes about itself. "We have built many tanks like this" should be true in the sense a customer would understand it: tanks of this kind, in this material, to this finish, not just vessels of any sort. A list of past customers should include only customers who would agree to be listed. A capability statement should describe what the workshop does now, with the people it has now, rather than what it did once or hopes to do. Each of these is a statement about what is in the business's mind and records, and a customer is entitled to rely on it. A workshop that keeps its general claims modest and exact finds that customers believe its specific ones more readily, which is a commercial benefit that costs nothing but restraint.

## What fair dealing did on this job

Looking back over the job, fair dealing did not make the workshop timid or unprofitable. It made it precise. The tank was priced at a margin the workshop could explain, with a rush premium tied to real costs. It was described truly, after three errors in the first draft were found and fixed. A weld repair, a scratch and a changed design load were disclosed in time for the customer to act. Scarce capacity was shared on a stated basis, and a subcontractor was chosen on criteria fixed in advance. An overcharge was refunded within two days, unasked. And a careless promise was corrected within an hour.

Each of those acts cost something: an hour here, a few hundred dollars there, an awkward phone call. Each also removed a risk that would have cost more later, in a dispute, a lost customer or a reputation for being hard to trust. Aquinas did not argue for justice on commercial grounds, and this book does not either. But it is worth noticing, for a small business that depends on repeat work and word of mouth, how often the just course and the prudent one turned out to be the same.

## Exercise: a partial delivery

The customer asks whether the workshop could deliver the tank in two weeks without the internal polishing, for the customer's own contractor to polish on site. Polishing and the final leak test account for 17 of the 62 hours. Check whether 45 hours fits in 10 working days at 5.2 productive hours a day, and say what the workshop must say about the tank if it agrees.

**Worked answer.** Capacity over 10 days is 52 hours, which is about 116% of 45 hours, so the schedule check rates the partial job as low delivery risk, with about 7 hours of buffer; the work needs about 8.7 working days. The workshop could therefore commit to two weeks for an unpolished tank. If it agrees, it must describe what it is supplying truly. The tank would not meet the specification's surface finish, would not have passed the final leak test after polishing, and should not be represented as ready for food contact. The delivery documents should say so, the data book should record which tests remain to be done and by whom, and the price should be reduced for the work not done. The customer's choice would then be an informed one, which is the whole point.
