Even a strong company can be a poor investment if its shares are purchased at an unrealistic price. Business quality and valuation are different questions, and both matter.

## The idea of durable earning power

A company that maintains useful margins, generates cash, earns attractive returns on capital and avoids excessive debt may have greater capacity to compound value over time. But future earnings can still decline because of competition, management decisions, regulation or technological change.

## Price-to-earnings ratio

**P/E ratio = Share price ÷ Earnings per share**

If a share costs $30 and annual earnings per share are $2, the P/E ratio is **15**. This is a comparison of market price with current accounting earnings. It is not a complete measure of value. Earnings may be cyclical, temporarily inflated, depressed or influenced by accounting choices.

## Free-cash-flow yield

A complementary approach compares available annual free cash flow with the company's market value. If a business has an equity market value of $100 million and an appropriately measured $5 million in annual free cash flow attributable to equity holders, its illustrative equity free-cash-flow yield is 5%.

Be careful: the common operating-cash-flow-minus-capex calculation is not always equivalent to cash flow available to shareholders. Debt financing, lease obligations, working capital and other adjustments matter.

## Why assumptions dominate valuation

Any projection of future cash depends on growth, margins, reinvestment, financing, risk and the discount rate. Small changes in assumptions can make a large difference to estimated value, especially when most expected cash arrives many years in the future.

A useful valuation process therefore uses scenarios:

- **Conservative:** Slower sales growth, weaker margins, higher reinvestment.
- **Base case:** Plausible performance informed by history and current conditions.
- **Optimistic:** Strong execution and a sustained economic advantage.

Look at the range of estimated outcomes rather than treating one precise number as certain.

### Key takeaway

**A sound business analysis does not tell you what to pay until you also examine the uncertainty of its future cash flows.**

### Quick reference

- Is current profit representative of normal conditions?
- What price is being paid relative to earnings and cash generation?
- What growth and reinvestment assumptions are already priced in?
- What happens if the competitive advantage weakens?
