A business model is a statement about how value reaches a customer and how the business retains enough value to remain viable. It must be specific enough to be tested. 'Sell more' is not a model; 'provide a defined product to a reachable group at a price that covers delivery costs' is a useful start.

## Create a one-page operating brief
Write one sentence for each: customer group, problem or desire, offer, delivery method, acquisition channel, revenue mechanism, principal costs, operational constraint and near-term goal. Treat unclear parts as hypotheses, not facts. A new business may have limited evidence; acknowledging that is better than manufacturing certainty.

Separate **buyer**, **user** and **payer** where relevant. An engineering supplier may satisfy a technical user while purchasing approval belongs to another role. A customer may say they like a concept but have no budget or urgency. The useful signal is observable behaviour: paid orders, repeat requests, authorised trials and referrals.

## The economics of an offer
For each offer record the selling price excluding any applicable GST, direct costs of delivering one sale, time required, expected returns and other costs that change with volume. Subtract variable cost from revenue to obtain contribution. Contribution must support fixed expenses, the owner's required compensation where relevant, reinvestment and a margin for uncertainty.

Do not hide the owner's labour. Sole-trader drawings are not ordinarily a wage expense in the same way as employee wages, but the owner's time still has an economic cost. A model that 'profits' only because the owner works unpaid may not be scalable or attractive to a purchaser.

## Customer evidence ladder
At the lowest level is an opinion. More useful is a detailed interview about a past purchase and its constraints. Stronger evidence is a request for quotation, a trial with clear evaluation criteria, a paid pilot, repeat purchases and reliable contribution margins. No single indicator removes all uncertainty.

Before expanding into stock, software subscriptions or staff, write down the claim the expenditure depends on. Example: 'At least eight organisations will buy this service at $X within Y days.' Decide in advance what result would mean continue, adjust or stop.

## Offer design worksheet
Record **Who | Problem | Current alternative | Offer | Price | Direct cost | Delivery time | Evidence | Next experiment**. For the next experiment, select one narrow audience and one response metric rather than running a broad campaign.

## A realistic seven-day test
Day 1: interview existing customers about actual needs. Day 2: draft one limited offer. Day 3: define cost and fulfilment. Day 4: invite a small eligible group. Day 5: observe objections and purchase intent. Day 6: follow up without pressure. Day 7: count responses, paid conversions and costs; decide whether more testing is justified. Seek consent and respect marketing and privacy requirements.

## Interpretation
A poor result is information, not a verdict on the founder. Distinguish demand problems from distribution, pricing, credibility and timing problems. Avoid changing all elements of an offer at once, because the cause of an improvement will be impossible to understand.

## Extended study: applying the method

### Segment by buying situation rather than vague demographics

A useful customer segment shares a recognisable reason to purchase, a procurement process and a willingness or ability to pay. Two customers who look similar may buy for different reasons: one needs fast turnaround for an urgent repair, another needs predictable documentation for an annual contract. Interview them separately. Capture the trigger that starts a purchase, the alternative they currently use, the person authorised to decide, the delay that causes the most cost and the evidence they need to trust a new supplier. Resist the temptation to describe everyone as a potential customer. A narrow segment enables a clearer test, simpler fulfilment and measurable communication.

### Define the complete unit of value

Before costing an offer, specify precisely what the customer receives. Is it a physical product, installation, support period, report or access licence? Include revision limits, delivery timeframe, acceptance criteria, exclusions, warranty handling and responsibility for customer-supplied information. Ambiguous scope turns quoted contribution into unpaid rework. Write a sample order confirmation and imagine how the customer might reasonably interpret every sentence. If a promised result depends on customer approvals or data, show that dependency. Unit economics mean little if the unit itself changes between sales. Once the deliverable is consistent, costs, price and satisfaction can be compared between orders.

### Customer conversations without leading the witness

Ask about the customer's last relevant purchase, not what they hypothetically might do in an ideal future. Useful prompts are 'What prompted you to look for a solution?', 'Which options did you compare?', 'What delayed the decision?', 'What happened when the solution failed?' and 'Who signed off?' Avoid asking whether your new concept sounds exciting; politeness often inflates positive responses. Record observations separately from interpretation. A statement such as 'delivery was delayed by two weeks' is evidence. A conclusion such as 'all customers value speed above price' is a hypothesis requiring more data. Seek permission before recording conversations and store notes safely.

### Design experiments around falsifiable claims

An experiment should be capable of disappointing you. Write the hypothesis, sample, intervention, observation, minimum threshold and decision rule before starting. Example: 'At least three of twelve eligible prospects will request a priced trial within fourteen days.' The threshold is illustrative, not an industry benchmark. Pre-registering the rule protects against retrofitting a positive story to weak results. Track the denominator: three enquiries out of twelve approached means something different from three out of four hundred impressions. If a trial fails, check whether the audience saw a credible offer, whether objections were consistent and whether another controlled test is justified.

### Connect price to delivery economics

A high quoted price does not ensure attractive contribution if the work is custom, scope changes frequently or payment is late. Cost direct materials, labour time valued appropriately, subcontractors, packaging, freight, payment fees and expected rework. Add a capacity charge where scarce equipment or expert attention limits throughput. Then compare customer willingness to pay with the economics required to keep delivering safely. Do not copy a competitor's price without knowing differences in quality, inclusions, scale or sales channels. Price is both a market proposition and an operating constraint, and the two views must reconcile.

### A disciplined opportunity decision

Use a small decision register with columns for customer evidence, margin estimate, upfront investment, operational complexity, payback uncertainty and next experiment. Rate risk qualitatively if numerical evidence is weak rather than inventing precision. A promising opportunity might justify a prototype but not a lease. A request from a single vocal customer may justify a paid custom order but not a permanent product line. State which assumptions must be proved before each irreversible commitment. Continue only when evidence improves the expected outcome; pause when critical conditions remain unknown; stop when the offer repeatedly fails economic or customer tests.

### Workbook and scenario

Imagine a service provider offering a $1,500 fixed-scope package. Delivery uses $350 in subcontractor expenses and 12 hours of specialist work. If those hours are worth $60 each for economic evaluation, the direct economic cost is $1,070 before overhead and rework. Apparent contribution of $1,150 when only subcontractor costs are counted is misleading: economic contribution is $430 before overhead. Now consider a typical two-hour unpaid revision: another $120 in time reduces it to $310. The exercise is to write clear inclusions and revision boundaries, then identify the customer evidence that could justify the price.
