Most people value shares by comparing the price with some estimate of what the business is "really worth". *Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements* describes a different way Buffett has framed the question, which the authors call the **equity bond**.

It is a simple idea with large consequences.

## Shares as a bond with a rising coupon

A bond pays a fixed amount of interest every year. If you pay $100 for a bond that pays $7 a year, your yield is 7%, and it stays 7% for the life of the bond.

Now think of a share in a business the same way. The company's **earnings per share** play the role of the interest payment. Divide them by the price you pay and you get an initial yield. Buy a share for $20 when the business earns $2 per share and your earnings yield is 10%.

The difference from a bond is the crucial part. In a business with a durable competitive advantage, earnings tend to **grow year after year**. So the "interest payment" on your purchase price keeps rising. If earnings grow at 10% a year, the yield on your original price is 10% in the first year, about 16% after five years and about 26% after ten.

Nothing about your original price changes. The business simply earns more on it every year.

## Why this suits durable businesses in particular

The equity bond only works if earnings are both predictable and rising. That is why the idea is tied so closely to durable competitive advantage. A business with erratic earnings has no reliable "coupon", so the analogy breaks down. A business whose earnings rise steadily for decades behaves like a bond whose payments keep increasing.

The book contrasts this with the approach of Buffett's teacher, Benjamin Graham, who looked for shares trading well below a conservative estimate of their value and sold once the gap closed. The equity-bond view is not about a gap that closes once. It is about a return that keeps compounding for as long as the advantage lasts.

## Comparing it with other options

The equity bond also gives a natural benchmark. If safe government bonds pay, say, 4%, then an equity bond with an initial earnings yield of 7% that is expected to grow is a straightforward comparison: a higher starting yield, plus growth, in exchange for business risk.

The book notes that Buffett has often looked at **pre-tax** earnings when making this comparison, since bond interest is quoted before tax. Whichever basis is used, the point is to compare like with like.

## The price still matters enormously

The most important lesson of the equity bond is that **the price you pay sets your starting yield, and you keep it forever**.

Take the same excellent business in two scenarios. One investor buys at a price that gives a 10% initial earnings yield. Another buys at three times that price, for a yield of about 3.3%. Both own a great business with the same growth ahead. But the first investor's yield on cost will always be three times the second's. A wonderful business bought at an extreme price can produce a mediocre return for a very long time.

That is why, in the book's account, Buffett has been willing to pay a fair price for an outstanding business, but not any price.

## Limits worth remembering

The equity bond is a way of thinking, not a formula:

- **Growth is an estimate.** Past growth does not guarantee future growth, especially if the advantage weakens.
- **Earnings are not cash.** Earnings that must be reinvested just to stand still are not truly available to owners.
- **Interest rates move.** The attractiveness of any yield depends on what safer alternatives pay.

## What this means for building a business

For founders, the equity bond offers a useful way to describe what makes a business valuable: not just the profit it earns today, but the likelihood that its profit keeps rising on the same capital base for many years. Designing for that, through lasting customer need, low reinvestment requirements and pricing power, is designing for a rising coupon.
