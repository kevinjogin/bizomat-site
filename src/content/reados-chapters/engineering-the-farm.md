For most of history, most people worked the land, and most farm work was done by human and animal muscle. Ploughing, sowing, harvesting, threshing and carting consumed enormous amounts of labour, and a poor harvest could mean hunger. Over roughly two centuries, machines transformed that work. Seed drills, reapers, steel ploughs, threshers, tractors and combine harvesters allowed far fewer people to farm far larger areas, freed labour for factories and cities, and made food cheaper and more reliable.

Australia played a distinctive part in this story. Its farmers faced conditions unlike those of Europe: vast distances, low and unreliable rainfall, light soils, scarce labour and land cleared from scrub that left stumps and roots in the ground. Australian inventors responded with machines designed for those conditions, and some of the businesses that built them became major manufacturers. One of them was at the centre of a court decision that shaped Australian wages for most of the twentieth century.

This article traces, in general terms, how farming was mechanised, Australia's contributions, the business models that made farm machinery firms successful and the lessons for product developers and manufacturers today. It is a general historical overview.

## Sowing in rows

In the early eighteenth century, the English farmer Jethro Tull developed a horse-drawn **seed drill** that sowed seed in rows at a controlled depth, rather than scattering it by hand. Drilling used less seed, improved germination and allowed weeding between rows. Seed drills were adopted slowly, because they cost money and needed suitable land, but they established a principle that still matters: placing inputs precisely improves results and reduces waste.

## Cutting the harvest

Harvesting grain was traditionally done with sickles and scythes, and it had to be done quickly before weather or shattering losses ruined the crop. Labour shortages at harvest limited how much a farm could grow. In the United States, Cyrus McCormick demonstrated a horse-drawn **mechanical reaper** in 1831 and built a large business around it. Reapers cut far more grain per day than a team of labourers.

McCormick's success depended on more than the machine. His company sold on credit, allowing farmers to pay from the proceeds of future harvests, offered guarantees, trained agents to demonstrate and service machines and supplied spare parts. These practices, of financing, dealer networks, service and parts, became the business model of the farm machinery industry.

## Turning the soil

Cast-iron ploughs clogged in the sticky soils of the American prairies. In 1837, the blacksmith John Deere made a plough with a polished steel share that scoured clean as it cut through the soil. His business grew into one of the world's largest machinery manufacturers. As with the reaper, the machine and the business grew together: standard designs, manufacturing at scale and a reputation for reliability.

## Threshing with steam

After cutting, grain had to be threshed from the straw and cleaned of chaff, work traditionally done with flails on a barn floor over the winter. Threshing machines, driven first by horses walking in a circle and later by portable steam engines, did the job far faster. In many districts, contractors travelled from farm to farm with a steam engine and threshing machine at harvest, an early example of sharing expensive equipment across many users. Threshing machines also displaced winter work that labourers had relied on, and in England in 1830 the "Swing" disturbances saw farm workers break threshing machines in protest at unemployment and low wages.

## Water on dry land

In much of Australia, the limiting factor for farming is water rather than labour. Windpumps lifting groundwater from bores allowed stock to be run across dry country, and dams and channels stored and distributed surface water. From the late 1880s, irrigation settlements were developed along the Murray River, including at Mildura, where the Canadian-born Chaffey brothers laid out an irrigation colony, and governments later built larger storages and channel networks for irrigation districts. Irrigation made intensive horticulture and dairying possible in regions that could otherwise support only grazing, but it also brought problems of salinity, river health and competition for water that remain contested.

## Danger in the paddock

Mechanisation brought new hazards. Tractors can roll over on slopes and banks, power take-off shafts can catch clothing, augers and other moving parts can cause severe injuries, and quad bikes have been involved in many deaths. Agriculture remains one of Australia's most dangerous industries for work-related fatalities. Rollover protective structures, guards on shafts and moving parts, design changes and operator training have reduced risks, and safety regulators publish specific guidance for farms. As in other industries, the machines that remove hard labour introduce hazards that must be designed out or controlled.

## Australian conditions, Australian machines

### The stripper

In South Australia in the early 1840s, labour was scarce and wheat fields were ripening. A machine known as the **stripper**, associated with John Ridley and developed from ideas by several colonists including John Wrathall Bull, used a comb to strip the heads from standing wheat and beaters to thresh the grain from them. The stripper suited dry Australian conditions, where grain was hard and ripe on the stalk, and it greatly reduced the labour needed at harvest.

### The stump-jump plough

Much farmland in South Australia's mallee country was cleared from scrub whose stumps and roots remained in the ground. Ordinary ploughs broke on them. In 1876, Richard Bowyer Smith developed the **stump-jump plough**, whose shares were mounted on hinged arms that rode up and over obstructions and were pressed back into the soil by weights or springs. Farmers could cultivate land that had not been fully cleared, saving enormous labour and cost. It is a classic example of a design that solves the problem in front of the user rather than copying a design from elsewhere.

### The Sunshine harvester

In 1885, Hugh Victor McKay, a young farmer in Victoria, built a machine that combined stripping, threshing and cleaning in one pass, delivering clean grain into bags in the paddock. His **stripper-harvester** removed the separate winnowing step, and McKay built a business manufacturing it. His Sunshine Harvester Works, near Melbourne, became one of the largest manufacturing enterprises in Australia, exporting machines to other countries. Later developments, including a header design associated with Headlie Taylor, contributed to the evolution of the modern combine harvester.

### The Harvester judgment

In 1907, McKay's company sought an exemption from excise duty under a law that required employers to pay "fair and reasonable" wages. In the **Harvester judgment**, Justice Henry Bournes Higgins of the Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration considered what such a wage meant and set it with reference to the needs of an unskilled worker supporting a family. The decision was later overturned on constitutional grounds as it applied to the excise, but the principle of a basic wage based on living needs shaped Australian wage-setting for decades. A farm machinery business thus became part of the history of Australian industrial relations.

## Tractors replace horses

Steam traction engines worked some large farms in the late nineteenth century, but they were heavy, expensive and needed skilled operators. Internal combustion tractors, smaller and more versatile, appeared in the early twentieth century. Henry Ford's company mass-produced the light, affordable **Fordson** tractor from 1917, and tractors steadily replaced horses through the following decades. The shift freed the land that had grown feed for working horses and changed the economics of farming. Power take-off shafts and hydraulic linkages allowed tractors to drive and lift implements, turning the tractor into a mobile power source for many tasks.

## Moving produce to distant markets

Mechanised production was only valuable if produce could reach markets. Railways carried grain and wool to ports, and ships carried it overseas. Refrigeration opened another market: in 1879 and 1880, a ship carrying frozen meat from Australia reached London in good condition, and frozen and chilled meat exports grew into a major trade. Machinery on the farm and infrastructure beyond it worked together.

## Precision agriculture

In recent decades, farm machinery has become a platform for data and automation. Satellite guidance allows tractors and sprayers to follow exact paths, reducing overlaps and missed strips. Yield monitors map harvest results across a paddock. Variable-rate equipment applies seed and fertiliser according to soil and yield maps, and sensors and drones monitor crops. Many Australian grain growers have adopted **controlled traffic farming** and **no-till** systems, keeping machinery on permanent wheel tracks and leaving crop residue on the surface to conserve moisture and soil. The [satellites](/reados/collections/engineering-history-modern-era/satellites-engineering-in-orbit/) article explains the positioning systems that make guidance possible.

## Regional manufacturers

Australia still has a cluster of specialist farm machinery makers, particularly in broadacre seeding, spraying, tillage and handling equipment. Many are based in regional towns close to the farmers who use their machines, which lets designers see how equipment performs in local soils and seasons and respond quickly to problems. They compete with global manufacturers by specialising: designing for Australian conditions, offering responsive service and building equipment that suits the scale and practices of Australian farms. The model echoes the stump-jump plough: closeness to the user's real problem is itself a competitive advantage.

## Lessons for product developers and manufacturers

### Design for the user's real conditions

The stump-jump plough and the stripper worked because they suited Australian soils, crops and labour. Products succeed when they solve the problems users actually face.

### Combine steps to remove work

McKay's harvester combined operations that had been separate. Looking for steps that can be combined or eliminated is often more valuable than speeding up each step.

### The business model matters as much as the machine

McCormick's credit, demonstrations, agents and spare parts made his reaper successful. Service, parts, financing and support often decide whether a technical product succeeds.

### Products depend on infrastructure beyond them

Farm machinery created value only with railways, ports and refrigerated ships. Many products depend on complementary infrastructure that someone must provide.

### Growth brings social responsibilities

The Harvester judgment shows how a growing manufacturer's employment practices became a matter of national policy. Employers' decisions about pay and conditions shape communities.

### Precision reduces waste

From Tull's drill to satellite guidance, placing inputs exactly where they are needed has saved seed, fertiliser, fuel and time.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The farm and figures are hypothetical, and costs vary widely.

A grain grower crops 2,000 hectares a year and spends about $250 per hectare on seed, fertiliser and crop protection. Without guidance, overlaps between passes average about 8%, meaning that some areas receive inputs twice. A satellite auto-steer system with section control on the sprayer is quoted at about $25,000 installed.

The grower estimates that guidance and section control would reduce overlaps to about 2%, a reduction of 6 percentage points. That would save about 0.06 × $250 × 2,000, or $30,000 a year in inputs, before counting reduced fuel use, less operator fatigue and the ability to work at night. The system would pay for itself in the first year. The grower also checks practical issues: the accuracy of the correction service in the district, compatibility with existing machinery and the local dealer's ability to support the system at sowing time.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Study users in their real conditions** before designing a product.
- **Look for process steps to combine or eliminate.**
- **Plan service, parts, training and financing** as part of the product.
- **Check the infrastructure your product depends on**, such as connectivity, transport or correction signals.
- **Use precision to reduce waste** in inputs, energy and materials.
- **Treat employment practices as part of the business's reputation.**

## Common mistakes

- **Importing a design that does not suit local conditions.**
- **Selling a product without support and parts**, leaving customers stranded.
- **Speeding up steps that could be removed altogether.**
- **Overlooking the infrastructure customers need** to use the product.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a stump-jump plough?** A plough whose shares are mounted on hinged arms that ride over stumps and roots and are pressed back into the soil, allowing partly cleared land to be cultivated.

**Why does the Harvester judgment matter outside farming?** It established the idea that a basic wage should meet the living needs of a worker and family, which shaped Australian wage-setting for much of the twentieth century.

**What made McCormick's reaper business succeed?** As well as a working machine, the company offered credit, demonstrations, agents in farming districts, guarantees and spare parts. Those services reduced the risk to farmers of buying an expensive new machine.

## Questions to ask

- Have we designed our products for our customers' real conditions?
- Which steps in our customers' work could our product combine or remove?
- What service, parts and financing do customers need to succeed with our product?
- What infrastructure does our product depend on, and is it reliable?
- Where could greater precision reduce waste in our own operations?

## Bringing it together

Seed drills, reapers, steel ploughs, tractors and harvesters transformed farming, and Australian inventors adapted machines to local conditions with the stripper, the stump-jump plough and the Sunshine harvester. McCormick's credit and dealer networks showed that the business model mattered as much as the machine, and the Harvester judgment tied a machinery maker to the history of Australian wages. Precision agriculture continues the theme of placing inputs exactly where they are needed. The [interchangeable parts and the rise of mass production](/reados/collections/engineering-history/interchangeable-parts-and-the-rise-of-mass-production/) article shows how manufacturing at scale made such machines affordable. For any product developer, the lessons are to design for real conditions, combine steps, build service into the product, plan for supporting infrastructure and treat employment as part of the enterprise.
