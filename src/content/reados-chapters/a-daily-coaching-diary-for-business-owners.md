Many business owners finish each year roughly where they started. They worked hard, often harder than anyone else in the business, yet the business did not move forward much. The reason is usually not effort. It is that their days were consumed by urgent tasks, interruptions and problems, while the few activities that would genuinely grow the business were squeezed out.

A **coaching diary** is a simple daily practice that counters this. Each month, you write a clear goal statement. Each morning, you spend an hour learning something directly related to that goal. Each day, you do the "main thing" before the "multiple things". Each evening, you review what went well, what went wrong and what could be improved, and note what you are grateful for.

It takes around 90 minutes a day. Initially it feels difficult. Once it becomes a habit, it builds focus that compounds over months and years. This article explains each element, why it works and how to start.

## Why small daily actions compound

Imagine being offered a choice: $1 million today, or one cent that doubles every day for 30 days. Most people take the million. But one cent doubled daily becomes about $5.4 million on day 30, and the total of all thirty days' amounts is about $10.7 million.

An old story makes the same point. A sage asked a king for a reward of grain: one grain on the first square of a chessboard, two on the second, four on the third, doubling on each of the 64 squares. The king agreed, thinking it trivial, until his advisers calculated that the total exceeded all the grain the world had ever produced.

Human effort does not double every day, of course. But skills, knowledge, relationships and reputation do accumulate, and their effects build on each other. The early results of consistent effort are so small they often go unnoticed, which is why many people give up too soon. The opposite is also true: small harmful habits seem harmless at first, and their consequences appear only much later.

### The first 90 days

Whatever you start, whether a new habit, a new role or a new business initiative, the first 90 days matter most. They build the mindset and routines. The following months build the skills and results. Commit to the coaching diary for 90 days before judging whether it works.

## Element 1: A monthly goal statement

Once a month, write a goal statement in the form **"I will achieve what, by when."** Without a goal statement, you cannot tell which activities help you and which distract you. A simple rule follows from it: **accept anything that contributes to your goal statement, and reject anything that does not.**

To write it, answer three questions:

1. **Current reality**: where am I right now? Use numbers: revenue, customers, margin, capacity, team.
2. **Desired outcome**: where do I need to be by the end of the month?
3. **Broad strategy**: how will I get there?

Then ask:

- What is the one thing I could achieve this month that would create the most growth?
- What specific goal would expand the existing business?
- Do I need to hire, open a new location, win a particular customer or arrange funding?

Be specific. "Grow sales" is not a goal statement. "Sign two new maintenance contracts worth at least $40,000 a year combined by 30 November" is.

## Element 2: An hour of focused learning each morning

Each morning, ideally before the day's demands begin, spend 60 minutes learning in solitude. Some trainers call this a "ritual of sixty-minute solitude". Solitude means no phone, no email, no meetings and no interruptions.

### Learn only what serves the goal

The crucial discipline is to learn **only what relates to your goal statement.** Watching random videos or reading whatever crosses your path may feel productive, but it scatters attention. If your goal is to win maintenance contracts, study service agreement pricing, read about customers' maintenance problems, or learn how other businesses structure service offerings.

### Four ways to learn

- **Audio**: podcasts and recorded talks.
- **Video**: courses, lectures and demonstrations.
- **Books** and in-depth articles.
- **Mentors**: conversations with people who have done what you are trying to do.

### Record what you learn

At the end of the hour, note how long you spent, how focused the session was and five things you learned. Over weeks, these notes become a valuable personal knowledge base, and you will notice new ideas emerging that move you towards your goal.

## Element 3: The main thing before the multiple things

Divide your working day into two parts:

- **The main thing**: the activities that directly advance your goal statement.
- **The multiple things**: the operational tasks, interruptions and problems that fill every day, often called firefighting.

Firefighting is endless. If you let it, it will consume every hour. Focusing on the main thing moves you towards your goal. Focusing only on multiple things leaves you where you were last year.

### Learn from the firefighters

Fire services offer a useful lesson. Firefighters once spent most of their effort responding to fires. Over time, fire services in many countries shifted emphasis towards prevention: building codes, fire safety standards, inspections, smoke alarms and public education. Fires became less frequent, and response capacity could be used better.

Business owners can do the same. Many of the "fires" in a business, such as rework, late deliveries, missing information and repeated customer complaints, recur because their root causes are never fixed. Some of your main-thing time should go into prevention: fixing systems so fires stop starting. The article on [small-business improvements needing repeatable systems](/reados/collections/business-systems-and-operating-rhythm/small-business-improvements-need-repeatable-systems/) explains how.

### Protect the main-thing block

- Schedule the main thing first, at your most productive time.
- Turn off notifications and close email.
- Let your team know when you are unavailable, and who handles urgent issues meanwhile.
- If you can only protect one hour, protect that one hour.

At the end of the day, take a few minutes to write down your main thing and rate the day's productivity from 1 to 10.

## Element 4: A ten-minute daily review

Before finishing work, spend ten minutes reviewing the day using three questions:

1. **What went well?**
2. **What went wrong?**
3. **What could be improved?**

Then plan tomorrow: what is tomorrow's main thing, and what will you do differently based on today's lessons?

This short review turns each day into a learning cycle. Over a month, you accumulate twenty or more small lessons and adjustments, which compound into substantial improvement.

## Element 5: Gratitude

Before sleeping, spend ten minutes writing what you are grateful for: a person, a situation, a customer, a team member, a family member or something that went well.

Gratitude may seem soft for a business routine, but it serves practical purposes. Owners under pressure tend to focus on problems. Deliberately noticing what is going well restores perspective, supports wellbeing and often prompts you to thank the people who helped you, which strengthens relationships.

## The people around you

Your environment shapes your progress. The motivational speaker Jim Rohn popularised the idea that you become the average of the five people you spend the most time with. It is an exaggeration, but it captures something real: the attitudes, standards and ambitions of the people around you influence your own.

Identify the five people, or sources, that influence you most. They may be colleagues, employees, customers, suppliers, investors, friends, family or even the media you consume. Ask:

- Do these influences support my goal statement or pull me away from it?
- Who could I spend more time with, or learn from, to support my goals?
- Where do I need to reduce negative influences?

Industry associations, peer advisory groups, mentors and business networks are practical ways to spend time with people who are working towards similar goals.

## The diary format

A simple diary layout:

| Page | When | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly goal | Once a month | Current reality, desired outcome, strategy, "I will achieve what by when" | 30 to 60 minutes |
| Learning log | Every morning | Topic, time spent, quality, five things learned | 60 minutes |
| Main thing | Every day | Today's main thing, productivity score out of 10 | 5 to 10 minutes |
| Review | Every evening | What went well, what went wrong, what could be improved, tomorrow's plan | 10 minutes |
| Gratitude | Every night | Things and people you are grateful for | 10 minutes |

A paper notebook works well because it keeps you away from screens. A digital document or notes app works too, if you can avoid distractions.

## Measuring whether it works

Give the diary a fair trial and judge it by evidence rather than feelings. At the end of each month, look back and ask:

- **Did I achieve the goal statement**, or how close did I come?
- **How many days did I complete each element?** Track a simple tally of learning sessions, main-thing blocks and reviews.
- **What was my average productivity score**, and is it trending up?
- **How many hours a week did firefighting take?** If prevention work is succeeding, this should fall over time.
- **Which lessons from the learning log did I actually apply?**

Most owners find that the habit of writing a goal and protecting the main thing alone changes their month noticeably. If one element is not helping, adjust it rather than abandoning the whole practice.

## Extending the practice to your team

Once the habit works for you, consider introducing a simplified version to your managers: a clear monthly goal, a protected block for the main thing and a short end-of-week review using the same three questions. Shared language, such as "what's your main thing this week?", helps teams focus. ## Common objections

**"I don't have 90 minutes a day."** Most owners lose far more than 90 minutes a day to unproductive firefighting, unfocused browsing and avoidable interruptions. Start with 30 minutes of learning and five minutes of review if necessary, and build up.

**"I'm not a morning person."** Choose any consistent time when you can be undisturbed. The principle matters more than the hour.

**"I missed a day."** Make it up the next day and recommit. Consistency over months matters more than perfection on any single day.

**"I've tried routines before and stopped."** Start smaller. Track your streak. Tell someone what you are doing, so they can ask you about it.

## A worked example

The owner of a commercial cleaning business with 25 staff feels trapped in daily operations. Every day is consumed by staff absences, customer requests and quotes.

Her monthly goal statement: "I will win three new office-cleaning contracts worth at least $60,000 a year in total by 31 March."

Each morning from 6:00 to 7:00, she studies how successful cleaning businesses win commercial contracts: tender writing, quality reporting and pricing. She notes five lessons each day.

Each working day, she spends 9:00 to 11:00 on the main thing: researching target buildings, contacting facility managers and preparing proposals. Her operations supervisor handles routine issues during that time. She also spends part of her main-thing time on prevention: creating a roster backup system so staff absences no longer require her personal involvement.

Each evening, she reviews the day. She notices that proposals including a sample quality report get more positive responses, and adds one to every proposal.

By the end of March, she has won two contracts and is negotiating a third. The roster backup system has cut her firefighting time by about an hour a day. She sets April's goal statement and continues.

## Summary

A coaching diary turns good intentions into daily practice. Write a monthly goal statement, "I will achieve what by when", and accept only activities that contribute to it. Spend an hour each morning learning in solitude, only about topics related to your goal, and record what you learn. Do the main thing before the multiple things, and invest some main-thing time in preventing recurring fires. Review each day with three questions, and finish with gratitude. Choose your influences deliberately. Commit for 90 days, because small consistent efforts compound into results that are invisible at first and substantial over time.
