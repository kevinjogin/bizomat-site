A Cabinet submission is often the last piece of advice a proposal gets before a decision. Its readers are ministers from other portfolios, busy, not specialists in the subject and reading it among many others. Whatever the earlier work found but the submission does not say clearly is, for that decision, lost.

This is an illustration. On Thu 11 Nov 2027 the Minister agreed that the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO) develop option C, the central assessment service, as a new policy proposal for the 2028–29 Budget. Amara Osei drafted NRIO's Cabinet material, Grace Mitchell reviewed it, and Zoe Fraser's costing, final on Fri 10 Dec 2027, sat behind it. The material was lodged through the portfolio department on Fri 21 Jan 2028.

This chapter follows that drafting: what Cabinet is for, how a submission is built, recommendations that can be agreed as written, other agencies' views, and how the law protects Cabinet material and its drafts.

## What Cabinet is for

Cabinet rests on convention. The Constitution provides for a Federal Executive Council to advise the Governor-General (s 62) and for Ministers of State to administer departments (s 64), but the words "Cabinet" and "Prime Minister" do not appear in its text. Cabinet is the meeting of senior ministers where the government makes its most important decisions together: those that cross portfolios, commit significant money or bind the government publicly.

Two conventions shape how it works. Under collective responsibility, ministers argue in the room and then all support the decision in public, whatever view each took. That depends on the second, confidentiality: ministers can argue freely only if what is said, and what each argued, stays private. The law backs this up.

For the writer this has three consequences. The submission needs to make sense to a minister who did not commission the work. It needs to show what the proposal means for other portfolios and for the Budget as a whole. And everything in it, including every draft, is handled as confidential from the first page.

## The shape of a submission

The rules for Cabinet material are in the *Cabinet Handbook*, published by the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, and your department's Cabinet liaison area applies them. This book does not describe them. Ask the liaison area for the current template and rules before you write a word, and again before you lodge.

Whatever the template, a submission that asks for a decision has to answer the same questions, roughly in this order: what decision is sought; why now; what options were considered and why this one; what it costs, in which years, and how it is funded; who was consulted and what they think; how it will be delivered; what could go wrong; and how anyone will know whether it worked.

This is an illustration. Amara built the submission from the options paper of Fri 29 Oct 2027 but did not paste it in. The paper was written for one minister, who had asked for it; the submission was for ministers who had not. She kept the evidence a non-specialist needs and cut the rest.

| Question | Where the bridge submission answered it |
| --- | --- |
| The problem | 96 of 142 councils responded; of their 4,410 structures, 1,540 had no load assessment in 10 years, 410 carried precautionary limits and 37 were closed |
| What it costs the community | Detours of about A$9 million a year for heavy vehicle operators, Tom Becker's estimate with its assumptions stated |
| The options | Five, a paragraph each, including the strengthening fund (D) and why it was premature |
| The value | C's benefits worth 1.63 times its costs over 10 years at NRIO's 7 per cent real discount rate, and 1.14 if benefits were 30 per cent lower |
| The cost | A$14.5 million over four years, by year, from Zoe's costing |

Ministers will not read "P/A factor" or "net present value" without stopping. Amara wrote "benefits worth about 1.6 times the costs over ten years, in today's dollars" and left the method to an attachment. Every number in the body was checked against the costing and attachments; numbers that disagree lose a reader's trust at once.

## Recommendations that can be agreed as written

Assume ministers will read the recommendations first, and perhaps little else. Write them so that, if ministers agree to them word for word, the officials who must act know what to do, how much they may spend and by when.

A few rules make that possible:

- **One decision per recommendation.** "Agree" asks for a decision. "Note" asks for none, so never put a decision behind "note".
- **Name the thing.** What will be done, by whom, for whom, how much of it and over what period.
- **State the money exactly.** The total, each year, and the split between program funding and running costs. Practitioners call these "administered" and "departmental"; they are working terms, and your CFO area will tell you how they apply.
- **Show costs beyond the forward years.** If something will cost money after the last year shown, say so and say how much.
- **Avoid words that leave the decision open.** "In principle", "support" and "consider" decide nothing.
- **Make them stand alone.** No "as set out at paragraph 12".

```figure
type: checklist
title: Is the recommendation decision-ready?
caption: Read each recommendation as if it were the only page ministers saw. If an official could not act on it alone, rewrite it.
- It starts with agree or note | And every decision is an agree
- It names what, who, for whom and how many | So nobody has to read the body to find the scope
- It gives the money by year and by type | The same figures as the costing, to the same decimal place
- It shows costs that continue after the forward years | Even when they are small
- It says what ministers are not deciding | Later stages, other options, future reviews

```

This is an illustration. Amara's first draft of the recommendations, on Wed 15 Dec 2027, had four. Grace read them with one question: could Zoe set up a program on 1 July from these alone? The answer was no, and the exercise at the end of this chapter shows why.

## Other agencies' views

Cabinet decides for the whole government, so agencies whose responsibilities a proposal touches normally see a draft and comment before it is lodged. Which agencies, when, and how their views appear in the submission are matters for the *Cabinet Handbook* and the department that coordinates your portfolio's Cabinet business. Ask your Cabinet liaison area early, because coordination takes longer than drafting.

Send a near-final draft with time to read it, and say what you want checked. Settle what you can at officer level and record it. Where disagreement remains, state it plainly in the submission, with your reason for keeping the recommendation. Ministers should never discover in the room a disagreement that the submission hid.

This is an illustration. In early January 2028 the portfolio department circulated Amara's draft to the agencies it judged had an interest. One, which runs grant programs for local government, preferred grants to councils, option B. Grace and Amara met its officers. They agreed on the size of the problem but not on the instrument. The submission recorded that view in two sentences, with NRIO's reason for C: bridge engineers are scarce, and a panel can plan their work across the area instead of having up to 142 councils compete for the same firms. Zoe's costing had been settled with the CFO area in December, and its handling with the Department of Finance followed the Budget process (Chapters 5 and 6).

Consultation outside the Commonwealth is different. Councils, operators and state and territory road agencies gave evidence on the problem (Chapter 2). None of them saw the submission.

## Confidentiality and the law

Section 13(6) of the [Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) says an APS employee "must maintain appropriate confidentiality about dealings that the employee has with any Minister or Minister's member of staff". Treat Cabinet material prepared for a minister as part of those dealings.

The [Freedom of Information Act 1982](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text) (FOI Act) exempts Cabinet documents in section 34, and for that Act "Cabinet includes a committee of the Cabinet" (s 4(1)). Under s 34(1), a document is exempt if:

- (a) it has been submitted to the Cabinet, or a Minister proposes or proposed to submit it, **and** it was "brought into existence for the dominant purpose of submission for consideration by the Cabinet";
- (b) it is an official record of the Cabinet;
- (c) it was brought into existence for the dominant purpose of briefing a Minister on a document covered by (a); or
- (d) it is a draft of a document covered by (a), (b) or (c).

Copies and extracts are covered too (s 34(2)). So is information that "would reveal a Cabinet deliberation or decision, unless the existence of the deliberation or decision has been officially disclosed" (s 34(3)).

Three exceptions limit the exemption:

- **Attachments.** A document is "not an exempt document only because it is attached" to a Cabinet document (s 34(4)). The Act's note adds that the attachment may be exempt in its own right.
- **Official publication.** "A document by which a decision of the Cabinet is officially published is not an exempt document" (s 34(5)).
- **Facts.** "Purely factual material" is not exempt under s 34, unless releasing it would reveal a deliberation or decision whose existence has not been officially disclosed (s 34(6)).

Section 34 is an exemption, not a conditional exemption, so a document within it does not face a public interest test. Contrast deliberative material, such as advice and opinion, which is only conditionally exempt (s 47C) and must be released unless access would, on balance, be contrary to the public interest (s 11A(5)).

The dominant purpose test is what decides. Writing "Cabinet" on the front of a document does not make it a Cabinet document.

The [Archives Act 1983](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02796/latest/text) decides when the records become public. For a Commonwealth record created in any year after 2000, the open access period starts on "1 January in the year that is 21 years after the creation year" (s 3(7), table item 13). Cabinet notebooks, the notes taken of ministers' discussions in the meeting, have their own table: for those made in any year after 1990, it is 1 January in the year 31 years after the creation year (s 22A(1), item 13), and "Subsection 3(7) does not apply in relation to Cabinet notebooks" (s 22A(2)). Cabinet notebooks are not "documents" for the FOI Act at all (s 4(1)). The Archives must make records in the open access period available to the public, except those that are exempt records (s 31).

This is an illustration. Between lodgement and the Budget, councils rang Amara to ask what had happened to the bridge work. Her answer was the same each time: NRIO had given the Government advice, and any decision would be announced by the Government. She did not say what the advice was or where it had gone.

## Handling drafts

Drafts are where most leaks start, because they are made early, change often and pass through many hands. The exemption for drafts in s 34(1)(d) helps only when someone asks for a draft under the FOI Act. It cannot recall a copy that has already been sent.

This is an illustration. Amara kept NRIO's drafts under a few simple rules:

- one master copy in a restricted folder of NRIO's records system, with a version number in the file name and header;
- a distribution list kept with the master, showing who had which version and when;
- comments made on the master, not on copies sent around by email;
- markings and storage as the portfolio department's Cabinet liaison area and NRIO's security rules required;
- no copies in personal drives, inboxes or devices, and nothing deleted without asking the records area;
- when a figure in Zoe's costing changed, every place it appeared changed in the same edit.

Anyone outside the list who asked for a copy got a polite no, and Amara told Grace. That included colleagues in NRIO.

## When it goes wrong: a draft sent for a sense-check

This is an illustration. In January 2027, before the bridge work began, another NRIO team was drafting Cabinet material for a proposal to fund safety upgrades at level crossings where council roads cross freight rail lines in the program area. The draft proposed 50 upgrades at about A$1.6 million each: 50 × A$1.6 million = A$80 million.

An officer wanted to test the unit cost. He emailed the draft costing and the draft submission to a contact at a regional councils' association, "for a quick sense-check", with "confidential" in the subject line. The association shared them with its board. Ten days later a regional newspaper reported that the Government was considering a A$80 million level crossing plan.

Ministers had not seen the proposal. The Minister's office spent a week answering questions about a plan Cabinet had not considered. NRIO had to tell the portfolio department's Cabinet liaison area and its own security area. It also had to consider whether the officer had breached the Code of Conduct, including the duty of confidentiality in s 13(6). The Minister held the proposal back from that year's Budget. And councils that had read "50" would treat any smaller number as a cut.

Three controls would have caught it:

1. **Ask the question, not the document.** The officer could have asked the association what recent level crossing upgrades had cost its members, and which crossings worried them most, without showing anyone the draft.
2. **Set the rules before the first draft.** These include a distribution list, a single master, and the rule that Cabinet material leaves the agency only with the Cabinet liaison area's approval, which you should assume will be no.
3. **Report a slip at once.** Tell your manager and the liaison area the same day. The cost of a leak grows with every day nobody knows about it.

## Exercise: rewrite the recommendations

This is an illustration. Amara's first draft of the recommendations, Wed 15 Dec 2027, read:

1. Note the problem of precautionary load limits on council bridges.
2. Agree in principle to support councils to assess their bridges.
3. Note that funding of around A$15 million will be required.
4. Agree that NRIO consult further with stakeholders.

Using the case facts (1,200 priority bridges; A$9,500 per assessment; 300, 500 and 400 bridges from 2028–29 to 2030–31; Zoe's costing), say what is wrong with each, then write a decision-ready set.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. What is wrong:

1. Noting a problem decides nothing. The problem belongs in the body.
2. "In principle" and "support" commit nobody to anything. It does not say which option, who delivers it, or how many bridges.
3. "Around A$15 million" is not the costing, which is A$14.50 million. "Will be required" asks ministers to note money they are being asked to provide, so the real decision is hidden behind "note".
4. The consultation in Chapter 2 is done. What remains is councils nominating bridges, which is implementation, not a decision.

First, check the numbers:

- Bridges: 300 + 500 + 400 = 1,200.
- Assessments: 1,200 × A$9,500 = A$11,400,000, or A$11.40 million. By year: 300 × A$9,500 = A$2.85 million; 500 × A$9,500 = A$4.75 million; 400 × A$9,500 = A$3.80 million. Check: 2.85 + 4.75 + 3.80 = 11.40.
- Running costs: 1.25 + 0.75 + 0.70 + 0.40 = A$3.10 million.
- Yearly totals: 2.85 + 1.25 = 4.10; 4.75 + 0.75 = 5.50; 3.80 + 0.70 = 4.50; 0 + 0.40 = 0.40.
- Total: 4.10 + 5.50 + 4.50 + 0.40 = 14.50, and 11.40 + 3.10 = 14.50.

A decision-ready set, in the form the case used (your Cabinet liaison area will give you the current form of words):

That the Committee:

1. **agree** that NRIO establish a central bridge assessment service, procuring a panel of engineering firms to assess the safe load of 1,200 priority bridges nominated by councils in NRIO's program area, to one data standard, from 2028–29 to 2030–31;
2. **agree** to provide NRIO with A$14.50 million over four years from 2028–29 (A$4.10 million in 2028–29, A$5.50 million in 2029–30, A$4.50 million in 2030–31 and A$0.40 million in 2031–32), made up of A$11.40 million in administered funding for assessments and A$3.10 million in departmental funding for staff, a shared bridge data register and evaluation;
3. **note** that the assessment cost of A$9,500 is an average of indicative rates and recent invoices, and that a higher average would mean fewer bridges assessed within the funding;
4. **note** that keeping the data register after 2031–32 would cost about A$0.15 million a year, which this proposal does not fund;
5. **note** that NRIO will evaluate the service against the 2027 council survey, with a final evaluation in 2031–32, and that a strengthening fund is not proposed now.

Each decision is an "agree", and each "note" tells ministers something they would otherwise find later. Recommendation 4 is the one most often left out. The impact analysis assumed A$0.15 million a year of register upkeep over ten years, but the costing funds it only to 2031–32. Saying so now prevents a surprise later. Recommendation 5 states what ministers are not deciding, so nobody reads the agreement as a fund in waiting. If the proposal were to be funded by savings elsewhere, a further recommendation would say so, in the form the portfolio department advises.

Recommendations are the end of a long line of advice. Chapter 8 goes back along that line, to briefing the Minister and keeping advice frank when the office preferred another answer.
