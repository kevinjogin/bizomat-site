## About this book

A process that has never run at full scale is a promise backed by a pilot. It may well be better than the equipment it would replace, and its makers usually have good evidence that it works. What they cannot yet have is evidence that it works at ten times the size, for thousands of hours, on someone else's feed, with someone else's operators. The first full-scale plant of a technology is where that evidence is produced, and the owner of the first plant pays for it: in capital cost that grows during the project, in a longer schedule, and above all in a start-up that takes months or years to reach the output the business case assumed. A plant built to a design that has run many times before carries much less of that risk, because others have already paid for the learning.

This book works through that difference with numbers. It starts with first-of-a-kind and nth-of-a-kind plants and what the published evidence says about them, then rates technology readiness element by element, explains from research on pioneer process plants where cost growth and performance shortfalls come from, and places suppliers on their experience curves. It goes on to scale-up from pilot to production, qualification tests sized to show reliability and yield on the owner's own feed, ramp-up and reliability growth curves, and cost and schedule allowances that grow with novelty. It ends with the ways of buying the risk down, qualifying first, guarantees and separable modules, valued against each other, and with the choice and what to watch after it. Each chapter follows one running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for owners, managers, process engineers and finance staff who must choose between proven equipment and a new process, or approve the first full-scale plant of a technology. It assumes spreadsheet arithmetic, logarithms and net present value at the level of a business case; readiness scales, reliability bounds and simulation are introduced where they are needed.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional decision. This is an illustration. A family-owned plastics recycler in Perth's southern industrial area, with about 55 staff, collects, sorts and bales post-consumer rigid HDPE and PP packaging and sells the bales to processors. In March 2024 its board must choose a **washing-and-sorting line** to turn **18,000 t a year** of bales into washed, sorted flake. **Option A** is a proven line from an established supplier that has built 41 similar lines, guaranteed at **72%** yield, with a capital cost of **A$16.0 million**. **Option B** is a novel process from a small Australian technology company, claiming **84%** yield from a hydrocyclone wash, a closed-loop water system and a hyperspectral fines sorter, for **A$17.5 million**, and proven so far on a pilot one-tenth of the size.

On paper the novel process is far better: about **A$2.4 million a year** more contribution for A$1.5 million more capital. The chapters test that claim one element at a time, through readiness, scale-up, qualification, ramp-up and contingency, and follow the board to its decision and the first months of operation.

The business, its suppliers, its plant, its people and every number in the case are invented. None describes BIZOMAT work, a real business, a real supplier or a real technology. All amounts are Australian dollars excluding GST.

## How to use the book

Read Chapter 1 first, because it sets out the two options and the paper comparison that the rest of the book corrects. Chapters 2 to 4 measure novelty and experience, Chapters 5 to 7 follow the technology from pilot to steady operation, and Chapters 8 to 10 turn the findings into allowances, risk reduction and a decision. The corrected yield found in Chapter 6 is used in every later chapter. Every calculation was checked by running it, and the BIZOMAT tools linked in the chapters reproduce the learning-curve, contingency and schedule arithmetic.

The book is one of a series of BIZOMAT Library books on how projects are planned and delivered. *Reference Class Forecasting by the Numbers* supplies the outside view and the percentile and simulation methods that Chapter 8 relies on. *Front-End Planning by the Numbers* covers project definition and estimate classes, one of the drivers of cost growth in Chapter 3. *Stage Gates and Stopping Rules by the Numbers* develops the value of information and real options behind Chapter 9, and *Project Teams and Contracts by the Numbers* covers the contracts through which guarantees and risk are allocated.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The methods are established and the sources are listed with what each was used for. Where the book quotes published results, they are attributed and concern the plants and programmes that research studied, mostly large North American process plants and US defence programmes; their relevance to a small Australian plant is discussed where they are used. The readiness ratings, cost-growth factors, ramp-up curves and probabilities in the case are the book's own teaching values, not published rates, and a real decision needs its own evidence, quotes and independent review. Recycling plant is hazardous plant, and its design, installation and operation are governed by work health and safety laws, environmental and trade-waste approvals, and the competent people responsible for them. This book is general information about technology and investment decisions. It is not engineering, environmental, legal or financial advice for any real plant.
