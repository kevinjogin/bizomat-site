A performance rating should contain no news. By the time a supervisor writes it, the employee should already know what was expected, how they were tracking and what the supervisor thought, because the two of them talked about it during the year. When a rating surprises someone, the failure happened months earlier, in an agreement nobody could measure or a conversation nobody had.

This is an illustration. Hannah Lee started at NRIO on Monday 3 August 2026, two months before the end of the Client Services Section's performance cycle. The agreements she inherited mostly described what people should be, such as "responsive" or "a team player", not what they should do or how anyone would know. She had eight weeks to write better ones.

This chapter follows the cycle from the agreement to the record. Chapter 6 takes up what happens when the conversations show a real problem.

## What the rules ask of agencies, supervisors and employees

The Employment Principles in section 10A of the [Public Service Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) describe an APS that "requires effective performance from each employee" (s 10A(1)(d)). Part 5, Division 2 of the Commissioner's Directions 2022 divides that work three ways.

Section 49 covers the agency. The Agency Head upholds the principle by ensuring, among other things, that the agency's performance policies and processes are "fair, open and effective" and clearly communicated. The Agency Head also ensures that each employee is given "a clear statement of the performance and behaviour expected" and opportunities to discuss performance. The agency requires supervisors to manage performance effectively, and its policies "are used to guide salary movement" (s 49(1)(g)). Your agency's enterprise agreement says how.

Section 50 of the [Commissioner's Directions](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) covers you. Its list includes ensuring the employee "has a performance agreement" consistent with the agency's corporate plan and the work level standards for their classification. It also includes "clear, honest and timely feedback" and career conversations at least once a year. Section 51 covers the employee's side, including "engaging constructively with their supervisor to clarify work expectations".

Read the wording closely. Section 50 does not say "must". It describes how a supervisor upholds a principle, and your agency's performance policy turns that into the steps you are required to take. Read both, and ask your HR area for the work level standards for each classification in your team.

## NRIO's cycle and where Hannah started

This is an illustration. NRIO's performance procedure, which is fictional, runs a 12-month cycle from 1 October to 30 September. It fixes four points:

- agreements in place by the end of the first month of the cycle;
- a one-to-one at least monthly, with a short dated note in NRIO's performance system;
- a mid-point review in April, recorded by the end of the month, with no rating;
- a final assessment in October, rating each expectation and the overall result as met, partly met or not met.

Any rating below met needs the People Branch's advice before it is final. Hannah holds the agreements for everyone in the section. Marcus and Leila check their teams' work each day, give feedback and supply evidence. The Director holds Hannah's agreement and moderates ratings across the branch. The cycle began on Thursday 1 October 2026. Daniel was leaving on promotion later that month and Mei was on parental leave, so six people needed agreements, including Ruby.

## From the business plan to each person's agreement

A performance agreement should show how a person's work serves the agency's purpose. Section 50(c) links it to the corporate plan, and in practice the line runs through the business plan.

This is an illustration. NRIO's purpose is that regional communities have safe, working public facilities. The Corporate and Client Services Branch business plan for 2026–27 commits the Client Services Section to four service standards, all NRIO's own:

- 80 per cent of calls answered within 60 seconds;
- emails answered within 2 business days;
- complaints acknowledged within 2 business days;
- letters and complaints answered within 15 business days of receipt.

Hannah's agreement carries all four, plus her people-management duties. Marcus's carries the rostering and daily checks behind the phone and email standards, and Leila's the complaint and letter work. Each officer's agreement carries the part of the work that officer controls.

That last phrase matters. The call standard is a section result that depends on demand, rostering and call length (Chapter 2). No single APS 4 controls it, so it is not an APS 4's personal target. What the officer does control belongs in the agreement instead: being on the phones when rostered, giving accurate answers and recording each call properly.

## Writing expectations someone can meet

An expectation is specific when two people reading it would agree on whether it was met. A useful test has four parts:

- **What:** the task or behaviour, in words the person would use.
- **How well:** a count, a time or something you can observe.
- **How it is checked:** the report, sample or observation, and how often.
- **What is not counted:** leave, agreed priorities and work outside the person's control.

This is an illustration. Leila's 2025–26 agreement said "Manage complaints effectively." For 2026–27 she and Hannah agreed: "Acknowledge every complaint within 2 business days of receipt. Send a response within 15 business days, or write to the complainant before then with the reason for the delay and a new date. Checked monthly from the complaints register." Leila can see each day whether she is on track, and so can Hannah.

Two cautions apply. First, an easy count can crowd out the work that matters. Average handling time falls when officers rush callers and rises when someone takes the hard calls on purpose, so use it to start a conversation, not as a target.

Second, put behaviour in the agreement as well as output. Section 13(3) of the Act says an APS employee "must treat everyone with respect and courtesy, and without harassment". Describe observable behaviour, such as how a hard call was handled, not a trait such as "a positive attitude".

Write the agreement with the person, not for them.

This is an illustration. Hannah tested each draft with its officer: is this the work you do, can you see the measure, is anything outside your control? Several drafts changed, and the people who changed them understood them better.

## Conversations and feedback through the year

Conversations during the year make the agreement work. Section 50(d) asks for feedback that is clear, honest and timely, and each word is a test:

- **Clear:** name the work and the standard. "Two of the four calls I listened to gave out-of-date opening hours" is clear. "Watch your accuracy" is not.
- **Honest:** say what you see, including what is good. "Your reply to the council answered all three of its questions" tells a person what to repeat.
- **Timely:** within days, while the work is fresh. A concern saved for the mid-point review could have been fixed months earlier.

This is an illustration. Hannah holds a short weekly one-to-one with each person, and Marcus and Leila give day-to-day feedback on the calls and letters they check. Once a month the one-to-one covers progress against each expectation, using reports the person also sees, what is getting in the way and what support is needed. Hannah writes a four-line note in NRIO's performance system that day: the date, what was discussed, what was agreed and the next step. The employee can add a comment.

Career conversations are a different meeting. Section 50(b) describes them as covering the employee's "performance, potential, aspirations, organisational fit and future opportunities". Hold each one separately, so this month's numbers do not crowd it out. Chapter 10 returns to development.

## The mid-point review

This is an illustration. NRIO's mid-point review falls in April 2027 and carries no rating. Its job is to check each expectation against the evidence, adjust what has changed and leave nothing unsaid before the final assessment.

Hannah prepares from the October to March reports the person has seen, her one-to-one notes (to check each concern was raised when it arose) and a list of what has changed.

By April, a lot has. Mei returned from parental leave on Monday 4 January 2027, and her agreement reflects the working arrangement settled after her request (Chapter 9). Tessa Morgan, the new APS 5, started on Monday 15 February 2027, so her mid-point covers about two months, not six. Ruby's agreement sits alongside her probation plan (Chapter 4), and the two should not send her different messages.

Change an expectation at the mid-point when the work has changed, and record why. Do not change it because someone is behind; that calls for support and, if the gap is real and lasting, Chapter 6. End each review with a written summary the person has seen. If anything in it would surprise them, the problem lies in the months before.

## Records that would stand up to review

A performance record serves the employee, a future supervisor and, now and then, a reviewer. Each needs what was expected, what happened and what was said, in order and dated: the agreement and any changes with reasons, the monthly notes, the evidence relied on, the employee's comments, the mid-point summary and the final assessment. Keep it in the agency's system, not a notebook. It is personal information, so ask your HR area how long it is kept and who may see it.

Records matter because decisions about people can be reviewed. Section 33(1) of the Act entitles an APS employee to review "of any APS action that relates to his or her APS employment", with exceptions in the Public Service Regulations 2023, which generally set a 60-day limit for applying to the Agency Head (s 37(5)). Ask your HR area how review applies to ratings, and whether your enterprise agreement adds its own process.

## Fair and consistent across the team

Fairness in performance management is mostly consistency: the same work, the same measure and the same chance to improve. Section 10A(1)(f) of the Act describes workplaces "free from discrimination, patronage and favouritism", and uneven standards let favouritism in even when nobody intends it.

This is an illustration. Hannah applies four checks:

- **Same work, same measure.** Marcus listens to four calls a month for each phone officer, across different days and times. Leila checks every letter before it is sent.
- **Context before judgement.** Before a number becomes evidence, ask what drives it: the mix of calls, a system outage, a new template or leave.
- **Fair adjustments.** Expectations written as counts are scaled to the hours a person works and their months in the role.
- **Side-by-side review.** Hannah reads all the draft ratings together with their evidence before the Director moderates across the branch. It exposes common distortions: the confident talker rated above the quiet officer for the same results, or one strong skill colouring every rating.

## When it goes wrong: the rating Sophie did not see coming

This is an illustration. In October 2026 Hannah completed the final assessments for the 2025–26 cycle, which ended on Wednesday 30 September. She had supervised the team for its last two months. For the other ten she had her predecessor's notes, the April 2026 mid-point records and a one-page handover note.

Sophie Grant's agreement said "Handle calls efficiently." The phone system's annual report showed she had taken 6,000 calls at an average handling time of 8.0 minutes, against the section's average of about 6. The handover note said "Sophie slow on calls?" Hannah rated that expectation not met and Sophie's overall result partly met, and took the draft to Sophie's final conversation on Tuesday 13 October 2026.

Sophie was shocked. Her April mid-point record said "on track", and nobody had shown her the report. She also had an explanation. Since February 2026 Marcus had routed calls from councils to her, because she knew the facility booking system best, and those calls took longer.

Hannah checked. Marcus confirmed the routing, and the phone report, split by type of call, showed:

- council calls: 2,000 × 12 minutes = 24,000 minutes;
- other calls: 4,000 × 6 minutes = 24,000 minutes;
- all calls: 48,000 minutes ÷ 6,000 calls = 8.0 minutes on average.

On ordinary calls, Sophie was at the section's average. Her higher figure was the cost of taking the hardest calls, which the team had asked her to do. Hannah changed both ratings to met, added a comment recognising the council calls and apologised. The assessment became final on Tuesday 27 October 2026.

It cost two weeks of Hannah's and Marcus's time, a distressing meeting for Sophie and some of the trust a new manager needs. Had the rating stood, it could have affected Sophie's salary movement.

Three controls would have caught it. An agreed measure would have left no gap for an undiscussed number to fill. Timely feedback would have raised handling time in February, when Marcus could have explained the routing in a minute. And the second question in the checklist above would have stopped the draft, because Sophie had never seen the evidence. Now every phone officer sees their own report each month, split by type of call.

## Exercise: rewrite five vague expectations

This is an illustration. Before the 2026–27 cycle, Hannah collected expectations from the 2025–26 agreements. Rewrite each of these five so that two people could agree on whether it was met, using the section's standards and checking methods. Assume a phone and email officer answers about 200 emails a month and a correspondence officer drafts about 45 letters a month.

| | Expectation in the 2025–26 agreement | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | "Provide excellent customer service on the phones." | APS 4 phone and email officer |
| B | "Respond to emails in a timely manner." | APS 4 phone and email officer |
| C | "Produce high-quality correspondence." | APS 5 correspondence officer |
| D | "Ensure the team meets the service standard." | APS 6 Team Leader, phones and email |
| E | "Be a team player." | Everyone |

### Worked answer

**A: split it into what the officer controls.** "Be logged in and available for calls during your rostered phone hours, apart from agreed breaks, leave and tasks your team leader has approved, as the weekly phone report shows. Give accurate, current information and follow NRIO's call quality checklist on at least 11 of the 12 calls Marcus checks each quarter." Marcus checks 4 calls a month, so 4 × 3 = 12 a quarter, and 11 of 12 is 91.7 per cent (11 ÷ 12). Do not write "95 per cent" for a sample this small: 11 of 12 falls short of it, so that target really demands 12 of 12. Write the count. Handling time is left out on purpose, because it reflects the mix of calls.

**B: name the time limit and the report.** "Answer each email assigned to you within 2 business days of receipt, or send a holding reply within that time saying when a full answer will come. At least 95 per cent on time each month, from the email system's report." An email received on Thursday 22 October 2026 is due by the end of Monday 26 October: Friday 23 October is the first business day and Monday the second. At about 200 emails a month, 200 × 0.95 = 190, so no more than 10 may be late.

**C: define "quality" by what the check looks for.** "At least 9 in 10 of your letters approved at Leila's check without rework each month, from her check log. Each letter answers every question the writer raised, uses current facility and program information and follows NRIO's template. Drafts are ready for checking within 10 business days of receipt, so letters can go within the 15-business-day standard." At about 45 letters a month, 45 × 0.9 = 40.5, so at least 41 must pass first time and no more than 4 may need rework. Forty-one passes is 91.1 per cent (41 ÷ 45) and meets the standard; 40 is 88.9 per cent (40 ÷ 45) and does not. Chapter 6 uses this standard.

**D: turn a team result into the team leader's actions.** The call standard depends on demand and staffing (Chapter 2), so it cannot be Marcus's personal target. "Publish the phone roster at least a week ahead, at the staffing level Hannah approves for each half hour. Check the queue report each morning. If the share of calls answered within 60 seconds falls below 80 per cent on two days in a row, tell Hannah that day, with the likely cause and a proposed fix. Report the monthly result, with reasons, at your next one-to-one." The section's result stays in Hannah's agreement, where the staffing decisions sit.

**E: describe behaviour you can observe.** "When your team leader asks you to join the phones to cover a peak, log on within 10 minutes, unless you are on a task they have agreed comes first. When you find an answer in the team's knowledge base missing or out of date, add or correct it. Treat callers and colleagues with respect and courtesy, including on difficult calls, as the Code of Conduct requires (s 13(3))." Check it with the phone report and the team leaders' observations, and discuss it at one-to-ones with examples, not impressions.

Each rewrite now says what is expected, how well and how it is checked. A and D also move a section result away from people who cannot control it.
