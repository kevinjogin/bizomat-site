The bank balance is a snapshot. The cash forecast is a calendar of what must happen next. A profitable business can still struggle when customers pay later than suppliers, employees or tax authorities require payment.

## Use weekly time buckets
Create thirteen columns for the next thirteen weeks. Start with the reconciled opening bank balance. Add expected customer receipts in the week likely to be paid, not merely the invoice due date. Subtract payroll, suppliers, rent, tax, debt payments, equipment and other cash outflows in their expected weeks.

**Closing cash = opening cash + actual or forecast cash received − cash paid.**

The next week's opening cash equals the preceding week's closing cash. Maintain a separate line for restricted or reserved funds so the report does not overstate spendable cash.

## Three classifications for each line
**Confirmed:** banked cash, approved payment or reliable contractual due date. **Expected:** credible but uncertain payment based on customer history. **Possible:** opportunity not yet ordered or likely to slip. A prudent model keeps possible receipts out of the survival baseline and shows them separately.

## Worked cash illustration
Suppose opening cash is $14,000. In week one the business receives $4,000 and pays $11,000: closing cash is **$7,000**. Week two receives $2,000 and pays $9,000: closing cash is **$0**. If $3,000 of week-two payments must occur before the $2,000 receipt lands, the account can run short before the weekly closing balance suggests. For tight cash, forecast daily within high-risk weeks.

## Weekly operating rhythm
Every Monday: reconcile the bank; mark actual receipts and payments; update overdue invoices; confirm payroll and statutory obligations; review the minimum forecast balance; agree action owners. Every Friday: compare forecast against actual, investigate surprises and move the thirteen-week window forward.

## Response hierarchy when the forecast goes negative
First verify that the dates and balances are correct. Then contact customers about legitimate outstanding amounts, discuss supplier terms before the due date, remove discretionary commitments, accelerate approved invoicing or adjust delivery timing. Financing is a separate decision with costs and risks; do not assume it is the default answer.

Never defer legally required employee entitlements or taxes without obtaining suitable advice about permitted arrangements. Record payment commitments and communications.

## Minimum cash floor
Decide on an operational reserve that reflects the business's largest near-term commitments, volatility, refund risk and payment concentration. A reserve is useful only if the weekly model tests against it. Do not assume that a universal number of months fits every organisation.

## Extended study: professional practice

### Create a reliable opening position

A forecast begins from a reconciled bank balance, not a figure remembered from the banking app. Compare bank accounts with the bookkeeping ledger, investigate unpresented transactions and separate restricted or earmarked amounts such as tax provisions and customer money that has an associated obligation. If several bank accounts serve the business, consolidate them only after eliminating internal transfers. Record the opening date and distinguish cleared funds from money still in transit. The forecast is only as trustworthy as its starting position, so one unexplained difference should be investigated before making large payment decisions.

### Schedule receipts by expected collection

For each open customer invoice, record amount, due date, payment history, dispute status, person responsible and the most plausible cash date. An invoice due on Friday might arrive next Tuesday. If the customer has disputed quality, do not model it as certain. New sales prospects should remain in a separate scenario until sufficiently committed; optimistic expected revenue is not immediately available bank cash. Keep a visible distinction between invoiced, contractually committed, forecast and speculative receipts. When customers pay partial amounts or deductions, enter the actual amount and track the remaining claim separately.

### Forecast all payments, including irregular ones

Create groups for supplier payments, employee costs, superannuation, tax, insurance, finance commitments, licences, rent, asset purchases and refunds. Check supplier statements and contracts for dates that do not occur monthly. For instance, an annual insurance renewal can be missed by a forecast based only on recent average expenses. Payment timing may depend on payment method, cut-off date or weekends. Do not assume money will arrive on the same day a bill must leave. If a large obligation has no confirmed amount, record a conservative estimate with the reason and refresh it.

### Use forecast variance as evidence

After each week, compare actual cash receipts and payments with last week's forecast. Classify differences as timing, volume, price, missing item or recording error. If a $5,000 receipt moved from week one to week three, the total over thirteen weeks may be unchanged while short-term liquidity deteriorates. Preserve the prior forecast to assess accuracy instead of overwriting history without a trace. Track cumulative variance and repeated causes. A recurring late-paying customer should change future collection assumptions even when the contractual due date has not changed.

### Prepare a cash response playbook

When the projected balance falls below the agreed floor, first check the records and assumptions. Next prioritise legitimate collection of overdue invoices, identify avoidable discretionary spending, revise new purchases, communicate early about supplier arrangements and correct invoicing delays. For cash constraints affecting wages, tax, super or other legal obligations, obtain qualified advice immediately rather than quietly delaying payment. Record the actual decisions, approvals and revised forecast after each action. Last-minute decisions often carry higher cost, so the playbook should be rehearsed before a crisis.

### A three-week practical example

Assume week one opening cash of $21,000, receipts of $6,000 and payments of $11,000: closing $16,000. Week two receipts of $2,000 and payments of $13,000: closing $5,000. Week three receipts of $14,000 and payments of $9,000: closing $10,000. The lowest weekly closing position is $5,000, but daily payment timing might be worse. Now delay $8,000 of week-three receipts into week five: week-three closing cash becomes $2,000. The example illustrates why a forecast should contain timing assumptions and why a reserve floor triggers action before the balance reaches zero.

### Operational worksheet

Create a ledger with date, counterparty code, explanation, expected week, likelihood, amount, GST treatment, evidence and owner. Establish a weekly rule for marking each line confirmed, changed or unresolved. Run a thirteen-week base case and at least one delayed-receipts scenario. The weekly review should produce the lowest projected cash balance, the next three largest payments, the five largest expected receipts and named actions for every uncertainty.
