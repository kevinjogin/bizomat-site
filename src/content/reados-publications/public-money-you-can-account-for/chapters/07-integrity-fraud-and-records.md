Integrity problems rarely arrive with a label. They turn up as a remark before a panel meeting, a friendly email with an offer in it, or an invoice that looks oddly familiar. The business manager is often the first to see them, and what happens in the next day matters more than any policy on the intranet. The facts are written down or lost, the right people hear or do not, and the failed control is fixed or left for the next person.

Lena meets three of these moments, then finds a fourth that someone had already tidied away. Each has a rule behind it and a record that someone will read later. Amounts in the case include GST, as the Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) state their values.

## The framework behind the controls

[Section 10 of the PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) says the accountable authority "must take all reasonable measures to prevent, detect and respond to fraud and corruption relating to the entity". That includes regular risk assessments, control plans, periodic reviews of whether controls work, making every official aware of what fraud and corruption are, and processes for officials and others to report suspicions. The rule is one part of the Commonwealth Fraud and Corruption Control Framework 2024, which took effect on 1 July 2024 and replaced the 2017 framework. The other parts are a policy that binds non-corporate Commonwealth entities, and guidance.

The duty belongs to the accountable authority, but most controls run at branch level: separation of duties, matching invoices to orders and receipts, panel declarations, a gifts register. A business manager who knows which controls the agency's fraud and corruption control plan relies on can tell when one is failing. Find the plan and the reporting process before you need them.

The officials' duties in sections 25 to 29 of the PGPA Act apply to every official, and three matter most here. Section 27: do not improperly use your position to gain a benefit or advantage for yourself or another person, or to cause detriment to the entity, the Commonwealth or anyone else. Section 28: the same for information obtained as an official. Section 29: disclose details of a material personal interest that relates to the entity's affairs. For APS employees, the Code of Conduct in section 13 of the Public Service Act adds honesty and integrity (13(1)), reasonable steps to avoid any conflict of interest, real or apparent, and disclosure of material personal interests (13(7)), and no improper use of inside information or of duties, status, power or authority (13(10)).

## A panel member's relative works for a tenderer

This is an illustration. When the maintenance tender closed (Chapter 4), the tenderers' names were known for the first time. Before anyone opened a submission, every panel member signed a declaration of interests. One regional site coordinator declared that her brother is a supervisor at Coastline Facility Services.

That declaration is the system working. Section 16 of the PGPA Rule says officials disclose material personal interests in accordance with the accountable authority's instructions, and notes that the Public Service Act has "a similar, but separate, requirement in subsection 13(7)". The CPRs add that acting ethically includes "recognising and dealing with actual, potential and perceived conflicts of interest". A declaration admits nothing. The problem is an interest nobody declares.

Dealing with it is a decision for someone with authority. The panel chair took the declaration to the Branch Manager, who would approve the contract under NRIO's delegations. The options were to keep the coordinator on the panel with limits, move her to answering factual questions only, or replace her. A sibling working for one tenderer is hard to manage with limits, and the perception would remain whatever the panel concluded. The Branch Manager replaced her. Her knowledge of remote site access was still useful, so the panel sent her written questions and shared her written answers with every member.

The procurement file holds the declaration, options, decision, reasons, questions and answers. Coastline did not win, but had it won, that file would have been the evidence that the award was sound. Interests also change mid-process: a panel member whose partner takes a job with a tenderer in week three declares it in week three.

## A supplier offers tickets

This is an illustration. Later in the contract, while Tom was pricing a variation to add two sites to the Ridgeway Maintenance contract (Chapter 5), Ridgeway's account manager emailed him and Lena. The offer was two tickets each to a regional football final in a hospitality suite, "about A$450 a ticket". Four tickets at A$450 is A$1,800 of hospitality, offered to the two officials about to agree a price with the supplier.

The CPRs say officials must act ethically, including "not accepting inappropriate gifts or hospitality", and section 27 of the PGPA Act covers improperly using a position to gain a benefit. These rules state principles, not dollar limits. Your agency's gifts and benefits policy sets the limits, who may approve acceptance and what must be recorded. NRIO's policy, as fictional as the rest of the case, requires every offer above token value, accepted or declined, to go in its gifts register. It also says officials do not accept hospitality from a supplier while a procurement or contract negotiation with that supplier is open.

The test is not only the value but who offers, when and why. Hospitality worth A$1,800, from the contractor, to the negotiators, during a price negotiation, fails on all three. Lena declined for both of them in a short, polite reply, recorded the offer and reply in the register, and told the Branch Manager. Tom asked whether he could go if he paid for his own ticket. The answer was no: the suite was not open to the public, so the access itself was the benefit.

## Position and information

A business manager holds information other people would value: tender prices, the forecast position, a contractor's performance problems, the work coming to market next year. Section 28 of the PGPA Act and 13(10) of the Code forbid its improper use. A friend asks which firms bid. A relative with a business asks whether a tender is coming. A supplier offers a personal discount "because we work together". The answer is the same each time: do not disclose or use it, and tell the panel chair or your manager if the approach is repeated, because a repeated approach can be an early sign that someone is trying to influence a procurement.

## A duplicate invoice

This is an illustration. In September NRIO paid a fencing contractor A$13,750 for depot repairs. In October a second invoice for the same job arrived, with a new date and the same invoice number plus an "A". The finance system's duplicate check compared invoice numbers only, and the purchase order was still open because the receipt had been entered as partial. The system would have let payments reach A$27,500, twice the order. Arun noticed only because the job number and site were familiar.

He stopped the payment. Then he established the facts before deciding what they meant: he checked the order, the receipt and the first payment, and asked the supplier, without accusation, which invoice was current. The supplier explained that a new accounting system had re-issued invoices it showed as unpaid, and cancelled the duplicate in writing. Arun still reported it to Lena and to the finance area that owns the system: the control had failed, even though no money had gone.

An honest error and a deliberate one can look the same on the first day, which is why Arun started with facts and did not keep digging on his own. Had the explanation not held up, or had the supplier shown a pattern, the next step would have been a report through NRIO's fraud reporting process, leaving any investigation to people trained for it.

Lena then asked for a wider check, shown below. This is an illustration. Of the 3,200 invoices paid from 1 July to 31 October, Arun found 38 pairs with the same supplier and amount within 60 days. Regular fixed charges such as monthly cleaning explained 33. Of the other 5, 2 were genuine duplicates already paid, for A$2,640 and A$1,320: A$3,960 in all, recovered by credit notes.

The fix belonged to finance: a duplicate check on supplier, amount and date range as well as invoice number, and closing each order when its final receipt is entered. Lena reported the gap with the evidence and checked a month later that it had been fixed. Section 10 of the Rule asks for periodic reviews of whether controls work. Findings like Arun's are the evidence those reviews need, if someone passes them on.

## Records that explain the decision

Section 41 of the PGPA Act requires the accountable authority to cause accounts and records to be kept "that properly record and explain the entity's transactions and financial position". The important word is "explain". A payment record shows that money left. Only the records around it, such as the approval, the declaration and the reasons, show why the payment was proper.

Section 24 of the Archives Act 1983 says a person must not engage in conduct that results in the destruction or other disposal of a Commonwealth record, the transfer of its custody or ownership, or damage to or alteration of it, unless an exception applies. The exceptions include the permission of the Archives or a practice or procedure it has approved, what the law requires, and normal administrative practice. A breach is an offence (20 penalty units). Ask your records area what your agency's approved practices allow before you delete anything about the agency's business, email included.

Lena's team filed each of these records in NRIO's records system, not a personal drive or inbox. Write each one as if an auditor will read it in two years without you in the room: what happened, when, who decided and why.

## When it goes wrong: the quiet fix

This is an illustration. Reviewing open orders in November, Lena found a purchase order for A$22,000 for switchboard work at a community building, dated three weeks before the work. The emails told a different story. A site coordinator had engaged a local electrician directly, calling the work urgent, with no order and no approval. When the invoice arrived, the coordinator raised an order with an earlier date "so the system's happy", and Lena approved it in a batch of routine orders without noticing the date. The work was good, the price was reasonable and the invoice was paid. Nothing looked wrong.

Everything was wrong in the record. Section 18 of the PGPA Rule requires an official who approves a commitment to record the approval in writing as soon as practicable after giving it. The order made it look as if approval came before the commitment, when none was given until the invoice arrived. The real order date was hidden, so any AusTender report would be wrong: at A$22,000 the arrangement was above the A$10,000 reporting threshold, and the CPRs require such contracts to be reported within 42 days of entering into them. The gap that let a coordinator commit A$22,000 with no delegation stayed open. Had the coordinator any link to the electrician, the quiet fix would have hidden that too.

What should have happened is the same four steps as every case in this chapter.

```figure
type: steps
title: When something looks wrong
caption: The same four steps apply to a conflict, a gift, a duplicate invoice or a purchase that skipped process.
- Establish the facts | What happened, who was involved, when, and what the records show. Do not accuse or investigate beyond this.
- Report to the delegate and the right area | The delegate for the money; finance or procurement for the control; the fraud area if anything suggests improper conduct.
- Fix the control | Change the step that failed, and check later that the change was made.
- Keep the records | The true dates, the explanations, the decisions and the reasons, filed in the records system.
```

Lena asked the coordinator what had happened, why it was urgent and whether there was any connection to the electrician. There was none. The amount was within her A$250,000 delegation, so she recorded her approval properly, with the true dates and circumstances. She reported it to finance under NRIO's instructions on commitments made without prior approval (check your own Accountable Authority Instructions), and to central procurement so the AusTender report carried the real date. The backdated order was annotated, not deleted. Every coordinator was reminded that they hold no financial delegation. With the Facilities Director, who manages the coordinators, Lena told the coordinator privately that fixing a missed step never means making a record say something untrue. She also told the Branch Manager that she had approved the order without reading the date.

## Exercise: five integrity calls

For each situation, say what Lena's team should do, which rule applies and what record should exist. This is an illustration.

1. Grace is about to join the panel for a A$90,000 security services request. Over coffee she mentions that she worked for one of the likely bidders until last year.
2. A supplier sends the team a box of chocolates in December.
3. A friend of Jas asks, "just out of interest", which firms bid for the cleaning request that closed last week.
4. Tom receives an invoice from an electrical contractor for an after-hours repair at a remote site: 9.5 hours at A$120 an hour, A$1,140. The access log shows 4 hours on site. The contract pays by the hour and allows up to 2 hours of travel each way to that site, at the same rate.
5. A manager in another branch asks Lena to delete an email chain about a contractor dispute because "it reads badly".

### Worked answer

1. Grace declares the past employment in writing, as NRIO's instructions require, before she sees any submission. The panel chair and the delegate decide whether she stays, perhaps with limits, or steps aside; a recent employment link can create a perceived conflict. Record the declaration, decision and reasons in the procurement file. Rules: section 29 of the PGPA Act, section 16 of the PGPA Rule, 13(7) of the Code and the CPR ethics requirement.
2. Check NRIO's gifts and benefits policy, which decides whether a token gift can be accepted and shared and whether it must be recorded. If the supplier is in an open procurement or negotiation with the branch, declining may be right. Rules: the CPR ethics requirement and the Code's honesty and conflict requirements.
3. Jas declines to say. Who bid is information obtained as an official: section 28 of the PGPA Act and 13(10) of the Code. If the friend keeps asking, Jas tells Lena, who tells the panel chair. A short note of the approach is the record.
4. Establish the facts first:
   - Invoiced: 9.5 hours × A$120 = A$1,140.
   - Allowed: 4 hours on site + 2 × 2 hours of travel = 8 hours; 8 × A$120 = A$960.
   - In question: 9.5 − 8 = 1.5 hours; 1.5 × A$120 = A$180 (check: A$1,140 − A$960 = A$180).

   Tom asks the contractor, without accusation, to explain the extra 1.5 hours or correct the invoice, and asks finance how NRIO handles a disputed invoice within its payment terms. One overcharge of A$180 is most likely a mistake. If the same gap appears on many call-outs, the pattern goes to the fraud area through NRIO's process and Tom stops asking questions himself. Record the invoice, access log, query and reply in the contract file.
5. Lena refuses and explains why. The chain records the agency's business, so she treats it as a Commonwealth record. Section 24 of the Archives Act forbids destroying or altering a Commonwealth record except as allowed, and none of the exceptions is about how a record reads. She keeps the chain in the records system and, if the request is repeated, tells the Branch Manager.

Every control in this chapter depends on people with the time, skills and support to run it. Chapter 8 turns to the team.
