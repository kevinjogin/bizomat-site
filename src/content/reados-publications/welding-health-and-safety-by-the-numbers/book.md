## About this book

Welding is one of the most useful trades in industry and one of the most hazardous. A welder works with an electric arc or flame hot enough to melt steel, intense ultraviolet and visible light, fume containing metals and gases that harm the lungs and other organs, electrical circuits that can kill in wet or cramped places, compressed and flammable gases, sparks and spatter that start fires, noise, heat and awkward positions held for hours. Most of these hazards are invisible or delayed in their effects. Arc eye appears hours after exposure, lung disease and cancer years later, hearing loss so slowly that it is noticed only when it is permanent.

This book is for fabrication managers, supervisors, safety coordinators, welders and instructors who manage those hazards. It explains, with numbers, how each hazard arises and how big it is: how much fume a process makes and how exposure is measured, how much air an extraction hood needs, what a respirator does, how radiation falls with distance, how much current a body carries at a given voltage, how fast a gas leak empties the oxygen from a confined space, and how noise doses and injury rates are calculated. Numbers make the hazards real and the controls measurable.

## Early manuals as a starting point

The welding manuals of a century ago took the welder's eyes seriously. The Davis-Bournonville course of 1919 gave a whole lecture to the care of the eyes, requiring coloured spectacles or goggles for welders, cutters and their helpers, warning that glasses darker than needed handicapped the welder, and offering a rough test of whether a pair was dark enough. P. F. Willis, in 1917, warned that glasses too dark strained the eyes as badly as none. E. Wanamaker and H. R. Pennington, in 1921, explained that the arc's ultraviolet and infrared rays were dangerous even though invisible, and that a helmet must cover the whole face because the arc blistered exposed skin.

The fume, the electrical and the long-term health hazards were less understood then. Each chapter takes up a hazard, begins where the early manuals were right or silent, and works it through with modern knowledge and measurement.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A fabrication shop of about thirty people makes structural steel, trailers and general fabrication, with gas metal arc, flux-cored and manual metal arc welding, plasma and oxy-fuel cutting, and some stainless and galvanised work. Its new health and safety coordinator, a former welder, wants to replace a folder of generic procedures with a programme built on the shop's own hazards and measurements, and to bring down a run of eye injuries, burns and strain injuries.

The shop, its people, measurements, incidents and results are invented for teaching. They are not KEVOS® work. Real workplaces must assess their own risks, measure their own exposures and follow the work health and safety laws of their jurisdiction.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 covers the hazards and the risk management process. Chapter 2 explains welding fume, Chapter 3 how exposure is measured, Chapter 4 extraction and ventilation, and Chapter 5 respiratory protection. Chapter 6 deals with eyes, skin and radiation, Chapter 7 with electrical safety, and Chapter 8 with fire, gases and confined spaces. Chapter 9 covers noise, heat and ergonomics, and Chapter 10 health monitoring, training and safety records. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational and does not replace the work health and safety laws, regulations, codes of practice and standards that apply, or the advice of competent occupational hygienists, engineers and medical practitioners. It quotes no exposure standards, protection factors or shade numbers; those come from the current editions of the regulator's publications and the standards named. Its figures are illustrations for teaching, labelled as such.
