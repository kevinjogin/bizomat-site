For thousands of years, ships were built of timber and driven by wind or oars. Then, within roughly a century, from the 1820s to the 1920s, almost everything about them changed. Hulls moved from timber to iron and then to steel. Paddle wheels gave way to screw propellers. Sails gave way to steam engines, then to steam turbines and diesel engines. Ships grew from a few hundred tonnes to tens of thousands. A voyage from Britain to Australia that once took months under sail could be made in weeks.

The transformation was neither sudden nor simple. Sail and steam competed for decades, and many early steamships carried sails as well. New materials and machines brought new problems, from fouled hulls and confused compasses to brittle fractures in welded steel. Disasters prompted rules that shaped every ship that followed. Underlying it all was a powerful economic force: larger ships carry cargo more cheaply per tonne, which pushed shipowners towards ever-bigger vessels until ports, canals and risk set limits.

This article traces, in general terms, how ships changed from timber to steel and from sail to engine, the economics and rules that shaped them and Australia's place in the story, and draws out lessons about technology transitions, scale, materials and safety for engineers and businesses. Containerisation, which transformed cargo handling later in the twentieth century, is discussed in a separate article. This is a general historical overview.

## The limits of timber and sail

Wooden sailing ships reached remarkable sophistication. They carried trade, migrants and navies across the world's oceans for centuries. But timber imposed limits. Very long wooden hulls tended to flex, leak and work loose in heavy seas, which constrained their practical size. Suitable shipbuilding timber, especially large oaks for frames, became scarce in parts of Europe, and navies worried about supply. Wooden hulls were attacked by shipworm in warm waters, which led shipbuilders to sheathe hulls below the waterline with copper from the late eighteenth century.

Sail also had limits. Ships depended on the wind, so passages were unpredictable, and calms or contrary winds could delay voyages by weeks.

## Sail at its peak

Sailing ships did not disappear quietly. In the mid-nineteenth century, fast **clipper ships**, with sharp hulls and huge sail areas, carried high-value cargoes such as tea and wool and passengers on long ocean routes. Australian wool was carried to Britain by fast sailing ships for decades. The *Cutty Sark*, launched in 1869 for the tea trade, later carried wool from Australia and made some of the fastest passages of its time on that route.

Sail remained competitive on long routes, such as those to Australia, well after steam had taken over shorter ones, because early steamships consumed so much coal that long voyages were barely economic. The old technology often reaches its peak just as the new one is taking over.

## Steam at sea

Steam-powered vessels appeared in the early nineteenth century, first as paddle steamers on rivers, lakes and coastal routes, where their independence from the wind was most valuable and coal could be loaded frequently. Steamships began crossing the Atlantic in the 1830s, and in 1838 Isambard Kingdom Brunel's *Great Western* entered regular transatlantic service.

The main obstacle to long-distance steam was **fuel consumption**. Early marine engines were inefficient, so coal took up space that could otherwise carry cargo. Improvements in engine efficiency changed the balance:

- **Compound engines**, which expanded steam in two stages, became common in the mid-nineteenth century and cut coal consumption substantially.
- **Triple-expansion engines**, from the 1880s, expanded steam in three stages at higher pressures and improved efficiency further.
- **Coaling stations** around the world allowed ships to refuel along their routes.
- **The Suez Canal**, opened in 1869, shortened the route from Europe to Asia and Australia and favoured steamships, because sailing ships struggled with the winds of the Red Sea.

By the end of the nineteenth century, steam dominated most ocean routes, although sailing ships continued to carry bulk cargoes such as grain and wool on some long routes into the twentieth century.

## The screw propeller

Early steamships used paddle wheels, which were exposed to damage, lost efficiency as a ship rolled or changed draught and took up space on the sides of the hull. In the 1830s, several inventors, including Francis Pettit Smith in Britain and John Ericsson, developed practical **screw propellers**, which work submerged at the stern.

The Royal Navy famously compared the two in 1845, when the screw-driven *Rattler* and the paddle-driven *Alecto*, ships of similar size and power, were tied stern to stern in a tug of war. *Rattler* pulled *Alecto* backwards. Demonstrations like this helped persuade sceptical decision-makers, and the screw propeller became standard for ocean-going ships.

## Iron hulls

Iron began to be used for hulls in the early nineteenth century, first in small vessels and river craft. Iron offered several advantages over timber:

- **Strength and length.** Iron hulls could be built much longer without excessive flexing, allowing larger ships.
- **Lighter structure** for a given size, leaving more capacity for cargo.
- **Watertight bulkheads**, internal walls that divided the hull into compartments so that flooding one did not sink the ship.
- **Independence from timber supply.**

Brunel's *Great Britain*, launched in 1843, combined an iron hull with a screw propeller, making it one of the first ships to bring together the key features of the modern ocean-going ship. After an early career on the Atlantic, it spent many years carrying emigrants and passengers between Britain and Australia.

Iron brought new problems that had to be solved:

- **Fouling.** Weed and barnacles grew quickly on iron hulls, slowing ships and increasing fuel use. Copper sheathing could not simply be fixed to iron, because the two metals corrode each other in seawater. Anti-fouling paints and regular dry-docking became necessary.
- **Compass errors.** An iron hull disturbs a magnetic compass. Scientists and navigators developed methods of correcting compasses with carefully placed magnets and soft iron, an early example of a new material creating a navigation problem that required scientific study.
- **Corrosion.** Iron and later steel hulls needed protection from seawater, through paint and later cathodic protection.

## Steel

The Bessemer and open-hearth processes made steel cheap enough for shipbuilding in the second half of the nineteenth century. Steel is stronger than wrought iron, so a steel hull could be lighter for the same strength, leaving more capacity for cargo. By the 1880s and 1890s, steel had largely replaced iron in new ships. The [how materials changed industry](/reados/collections/engineering-history/how-materials-changed-industry/) article explains how cheap steel transformed many industries.

## Turbines and diesel engines

Two new forms of propulsion arrived around the turn of the twentieth century. In 1897, Charles Parsons demonstrated the steam turbine at sea with his small vessel *Turbinia*, which reached speeds far beyond those of conventional ships at a naval review. Turbines ran smoothly at high power and soon drove fast passenger liners and warships.

Ocean-going motor ships appeared before the First World War, and diesels steadily replaced steam in merchant ships through the twentieth century because of their much lower fuel consumption. Today, most large cargo ships are driven by large, slow-speed diesel engines. ## The economics of size

One of the strongest forces in shipping is the advantage of size. As a ship gets larger, its cargo capacity grows faster than the costs of building and running it.

A simple way to see this is the relationship between volume and surface area. If every dimension of a hull is doubled, its volume, and therefore roughly its cargo capacity, increases about eight times, while its wetted surface area increases only about four times. At the speeds of most cargo ships, much of the resistance to motion comes from friction over that wetted surface. So the larger ship needs roughly half as much propulsive effort per tonne of cargo. Crew numbers grow far more slowly than size, and many other costs, such as navigation equipment, grow even less.

This is why ships have grown dramatically over time, and why bulk carriers, tankers and container ships today are enormous. But size has limits:

- **Ports and channels** must be deep and wide enough to receive larger ships.
- **Canals and locks** set maximum dimensions on many routes.
- **Handling time** increases, and larger ships must fill their capacity to be economic.
- **Concentration of risk** grows, because a single incident involves more cargo.
- **Flexibility falls**, because large ships can serve fewer ports.

Each increase in size requires investment elsewhere in the system, in ports, cranes, channels and logistics.

## Rules written after disasters

Many of the rules that govern ships were written after disasters.

**Overloading** was once a serious problem. Some owners sent heavily loaded and poorly maintained ships to sea, and losses were frequent. A campaign led by the British politician Samuel Plimsoll led to legislation in the 1870s requiring a **load line**, a mark on the hull showing the maximum safe loading depth. The "Plimsoll line" became a standard feature of ships worldwide.

**Classification societies** developed to assess the condition and construction of ships. Lloyd's Register, which traces its origins to 1760, began by recording the condition of ships for merchants and insurers and later set rules for ship construction.

**The loss of the *Titanic*** in 1912, in which more than 1,500 people died, exposed gaps in safety rules, including lifeboat capacity that fell far short of the number of people on board. It led to the first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea in 1914, which required, among other things, sufficient lifeboat places for everyone on board. The convention has been updated many times since.

## Welded ships and brittle fracture

During the Second World War, shipyards in the United States built more than 2,700 **Liberty ships**, standardised cargo ships produced using prefabrication and welding at a pace never seen before. Welding replaced riveting, which was slower and more labour-intensive.

Some of these welded ships developed serious cracks, and a few broke in two, particularly in cold water. Investigations found that the steel used was prone to **brittle fracture** at low temperatures, that square corners at hatch openings concentrated stress and that welding defects and continuous welded structures allowed cracks to run through the hull rather than stopping at a riveted joint. The lessons led to improved steels with specified toughness, better detailing, stricter welding quality control and the development of **fracture mechanics** as an engineering discipline. The [what engineering failures teach any business](/reados/collections/engineering-design-practice/what-engineering-failures-teach-a-business/) article explores how failure investigations change practice.

## An Australian chapter

Australia has always depended on ships for trade and migration. Shipbuilding and ship repair grew with the colonies. Cockatoo Island in Sydney Harbour operated as a major dockyard from the nineteenth century until 1991, building and repairing naval and commercial vessels. BHP's shipyard at Whyalla in South Australia built many steel ships from the 1940s to the 1970s. In more recent decades, Australian builders in Tasmania and Western Australia have become known internationally for large, fast aluminium ferries, showing how a specialised niche can sustain a manufacturing industry.

## Lessons for engineers and businesses

### Transitions take longer than they seem

Sail and steam coexisted for decades, and ships carried both. New technologies usually win first where their advantages are greatest, such as short routes with frequent refuelling, and spread as improvements and supporting infrastructure make them competitive elsewhere. Businesses can plan for long transitions and hybrid periods rather than sudden replacement.

### The old technology often peaks late

Clipper ships reached their peak as steam was taking over. A mature technology can look strong right up to the point where its successor overtakes it. Watching the trend of the new technology matters more than admiring the current performance of the old one.

### Infrastructure decides adoption

Coaling stations, the Suez Canal, deeper ports and dry docks enabled the shift to steam and larger ships. A new product or technology often depends on supporting infrastructure that someone must build.

### Scale has economics and limits

Larger ships carry cargo more cheaply, but they depend on ports, handling systems and full loads, and they concentrate risk. In business, larger batches, vehicles, machines and facilities often reduce unit costs, but they reduce flexibility and require the rest of the system to keep up.

### New materials bring new failure modes

Iron hulls fouled and disturbed compasses; welded steel ships cracked in cold water. Each new material or process solves old problems and creates new ones, which must be anticipated, tested and understood.

### Demonstrations persuade

The *Rattler* tug of war and *Turbinia*'s demonstration convinced sceptics faster than reports. A clear demonstration of a new approach can be more persuasive than any argument.

### Rules often follow disasters

Load lines and lifeboat rules came after losses. Businesses that anticipate the rules their industries will eventually need, rather than waiting for a disaster, protect both people and their own reputations.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The business and figures are hypothetical.

A regional distributor runs two 8-tonne trucks on its delivery routes. Each costs about $1.60 per kilometre to operate, including fuel, driver, maintenance and depreciation. The owner considers replacing them with one larger truck carrying 16 tonnes at about $2.40 per kilometre.

The economics of size look attractive. Fully loaded, the small trucks cost $1.60 ÷ 8, or $0.20 per tonne-kilometre, while the larger truck costs $2.40 ÷ 16, or $0.15 per tonne-kilometre, a saving of 25%.

Like a shipowner considering a larger vessel, however, the owner checks the limits. About a fifth of customers are on narrow streets or have loading docks that cannot take the larger vehicle. Loads are full only on some days, and on lighter days the larger truck's advantage shrinks or disappears. With only one truck, a breakdown would stop all deliveries.

The business captures much of the saving of scale while keeping flexibility and a backup, much as shipping lines use large ships on main routes and smaller vessels to serve smaller ports.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Plan for gradual transitions**, with old and new technologies running side by side.
- **Watch the trend** of emerging technologies, not just their current performance.
- **Identify the infrastructure** a change depends on, and who will provide it.
- **Test scale decisions** against access, utilisation, flexibility and risk.
- **Anticipate the failure modes** of new materials and processes.
- **Use demonstrations** to build support for change.
- **Get ahead of likely regulation** rather than waiting for an incident.

## Questions worth considering

- Which of our technologies are at their peak, and what might replace them?
- What infrastructure would we need to adopt a new technology, and does it exist yet?
- Where would larger scale reduce our costs, and what would it cost us in flexibility and risk?
- What new failure modes could a change of material, process or supplier introduce?
- Which safety or quality rules are likely to apply to our industry in future, and are we already meeting them?

## Bringing it together

Between the 1820s and the 1920s, ships changed from timber and sail to iron and steel, from paddle wheels to screw propellers and from sail to steam, turbines and diesel. The transition was gradual: sail peaked as steam rose, and many ships carried both. Infrastructure such as coaling stations and canals decided where new technology could compete. The economics of size pushed ships ever larger, limited by ports, canals and risk. New materials brought new failures, from fouling and compass errors to brittle fracture, and disasters produced rules that still govern shipping. For engineers and businesses, ships teach that transitions take time, infrastructure decides adoption, scale has both economics and limits, new materials bring new failure modes and the best time to adopt good rules is before they are forced.
