## About this book

A capital project spends very little while it is being defined and a great deal while it is being delivered. A week of planning costs a project engineer, a consultant's study and some meetings; a week of delivery costs contractors, equipment, site overheads and money committed that cannot be recovered. Problems found while the project is still on paper are cheap to fix, and the same problems found on site are fixed with rework, variations and lost weeks. The case for **planning slowly and delivering quickly** follows directly: spend the time where it is cheap, so that the expensive part is short, well understood and exposed to as little as possible.

This book works through that idea with numbers. It starts with where a project's money and risk are exposed, then organises the front end into stages that end at gates, scores how well a project is defined with a weighted checklist in the manner of the Construction Industry Institute's Project Definition Rating Index, and reads estimates by their class and range. It goes on to the hidden scope a front end exists to find, how much front-end work a project is worth, the exposure window of delivery, and the cost of missing a fixed deadline, then covers locking the plan in at approval, reviewing the outturn and scaling the method to smaller projects. Each chapter carries one running case, includes a plausible error and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for owners, managers, project managers, estimators and finance staff in small and medium Australian businesses. It assumes spreadsheet arithmetic. Probability is introduced where it is needed, through one simple schedule simulation.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional decision. This is an illustration. **Northbank Fruit Packers**, a family business in northern Victoria, packs apples and pears from its own orchards and from local growers: about **18,000 bins** a season of around 400 kg each. Its six cold rooms hold 6,000 bins, so a large share of each harvest goes to hired cold storage, and its packing line is old and slow. The business plans an upgrade: **four new cold rooms** holding 4,000 bins, a **new refrigeration plant** serving the old rooms and the new, an **optical grader** for the packing line, and the electrical, civil and fire services work they need. The upgrade must be ready by **31 January 2028**, one week before the first fruit of the 2028 harvest arrives, because the packing line can be changed only between November and January and the rooms must be at temperature before intake.

In November 2026 the concept estimate is **A$5.41 million** excluding GST, and the managing director proposes approving a budget of **A$5.95 million** at once and starting work, to give the project as much time as possible. The book follows the other path: 22 weeks and **A$248,000** of front-end planning, which raises the estimate to **A$6.43 million**, finds the scope the concept missed, orders the long-lead equipment at approval, and delivers in 36 planned weeks. The chapters compare the two paths on cost, time and the chance of missing the harvest.

The business, its people, its projects and every number in the case are invented. None describes KEVOS® work, a real business or a real project. All amounts are Australian dollars excluding GST.

## How to use the book

Read Chapter 1 first: it sets out the two paths and the numbers that later chapters build on. Chapters 2 to 5 cover the front end itself: stages and gates, scoring definition, estimate classes and the scope a front end finds. Chapter 6 asks how much front-end work is enough. Chapters 7 and 8 cover delivery, its exposure window and the deadline, and Chapters 9 and 10 cover approval, delivery control and the review afterwards. Every calculation in the book was checked by running it, and the schedule simulation uses a fixed random seed so that its results can be reproduced.

The book is one of a series of KEVOS® READOS books on planning and delivering projects, written to be read together. *Reference Class Forecasting by the Numbers* adds the outside view: how projects like this one have actually turned out, and how to set a budget and contingency from that record. *Stage Gates and Stopping Rules by the Numbers* covers the decisions made at each gate, including when to stop, where this book covers what must be defined before a gate. *Modular Projects by the Numbers* applies short exposure windows to repeated installations on working sites, and *Project Teams and Contracts by the Numbers* covers the people and contract forms that carry a plan into delivery.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The method draws on published research, industry practice and government guidance, and the sources are listed with what each was used for. Published results are cited only for what they found and for the projects they studied, which were mostly larger industrial and building projects than the case. The case's scoring index, estimate ranges, durations and costs are teaching values, not benchmarks, and a real project needs its own definition, quotes, approvals and risk assessment. Building, electrical, refrigeration and fire services work is regulated, and its design, approval, installation and commissioning are matters for the competent and licensed people responsible for them under the applicable laws, codes and standards. This book is general information about planning and approving projects. It is not engineering, legal, financial or investment advice.
