Every business makes mistakes in its invoices. What distinguishes fair dealing from its opposite is not the absence of errors but what happens when one is found. A business that corrects an overcharge promptly and completely, without being asked, builds a kind of trust that no marketing can buy. A business that waits to see whether the customer notices teaches its customers to check everything, and charges them, in effect, for the checking.

Aquinas treats this under restitution, which he calls an act of commutative justice: the restoring of equality when one party holds what belongs to another, whether with or without the other's consent (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.62 a.1). He answers several practical questions with unusual precision. How much must be restored? How soon? What if the loss was caused without anything being taken? This chapter applies those answers to an overcharge on the tank job.

## The overcharge

This is an illustration. After the tank order was placed, the customer asked for an extra outlet nozzle with a valve. The workshop agreed to supply it as a variation, charged at the loaded labour rate plus materials. The welder estimated 8 hours.

When the variation was invoiced, the labour line showed 14.5 hours. The customer, busy with the new tank's installation, paid the invoice. Two weeks later, during the job close-out described in Chapter 3, the owner reconciled the welders' timesheets against attendance and found that the quarry guard repairs showed 6.5 fewer hours than the crew had actually spent on them. The missing hours had been coded to the tank variation by mistake. The variation had taken 8 hours, as estimated.

```figure
type: formula
title: The amount to restore
formula: Restitution = hours overcharged × rate, plus the GST charged on them
caption: The correction is exactly the amount wrongly taken: no less because nobody noticed, and no more as a penalty.
- Hours invoiced | 14.5
- Hours worked | 8.0
- Rate | A$105 per hour, excluding GST
step: Overcharge excluding GST = (14.5 − 8.0) × 105 = 6.5 × 105 = A$682.50
step: GST on the overcharge = 682.50 × 10% = A$68.25
step: Total to restore = 682.50 + 68.25 = A$750.75
```

The customer paid A$1,522.50 plus GST for 14.5 hours. It should have paid A$840.00 plus GST for 8 hours. The difference, A$750.75 including GST, belongs to the customer.

## Restore exactly the amount taken

Aquinas's first answer is that returning the exact value taken is enough to restore equality (II-II q.62 a.3). Any further payment is a penalty, which he says belongs to a judge to impose after a wrong has been established, not to the person making restitution as a matter of justice.

That answer rules out two common responses. The first is restoring less: refunding the A$682.50 but not the GST on it, or rounding down, or deducting an "administration fee" for processing the correction. Each leaves the customer short of what they paid for nothing. The second is confusing restitution with generosity. The workshop may choose to add something as a goodwill gesture, perhaps a free inspection when the tank is next cleaned, and there is nothing wrong with that. But it should not describe the gesture as part of the correction, because doing so blurs the line between what the customer is owed and what the workshop chooses to give.

The other customer affected by the coding error is the quarry. Its repairs were quoted at a fixed price, so the 6.5 hours missing from its timesheet do not change what it owes. They change only the workshop's own record of what the job cost. That record is corrected, because the quarry job's real margin was lower than the books showed, and the realised-margin review in Chapter 3 depends on accurate hours.

## Restore it promptly

Aquinas's second answer is that restitution should be made immediately where possible, and that if it cannot be made at once, the person who owes it should ask the owner for a postponement (II-II q.62 a.8). Holding another's property while delaying its return continues the wrong.

### A failure: the credit against the next order

When the owner explained the error, the bookkeeper suggested a convenient solution: "Let's just credit it against their next order. They'll probably want another tank eventually." It was meant kindly and would have saved a bank transfer.

It fails both of Aquinas's tests. It delays restitution indefinitely, possibly for years. And it makes the customer's recovery of its own money conditional on placing more business with the workshop. If the customer never orders again, it never gets the money back. A correction that depends on the wronged party doing something further for the person who wronged them is not a correction.

The error was detected when the owner asked what would happen if the customer did not order again, and the bookkeeper had no answer. The correction was to refund the A$750.75 within two working days of finding the error, with a short letter explaining what had happened. The letter offered the customer the alternative of a credit against a future order if it preferred, making clear that the choice was the customer's. The customer chose the refund.

## Restore it to the right party

Aquinas also asks to whom restitution must be made, and his general answer is the person from whom the thing was taken (II-II q.62 a.5). That sounds obvious until a business tries to do it. The person who noticed the problem, the person who approved the invoice and the entity that paid it may all be different.

Here, the variation was requested by the customer's operations manager, approved by its engineering lead and paid by its accounts department from the company's account. The refund goes to the company's account, because that is where the money came from, and the letter goes to the accounts department with a copy to the operations manager, so that the person who requested the work knows what happened. Paying the refund to an individual, or as a discount on some other company's order because the two businesses are related, would not restore the money to the party that lost it.

The same care applies when circumstances have changed. If the customer had sold its business between payment and discovery, the refund would belong to whichever entity actually paid, not automatically to the new owner. Questions like that can become legal ones, and a business should take advice before guessing.

## When the other party made the mistake

Restitution runs in both directions. Chapter 5 showed the workshop telling a supplier about an invoice that undercharged it. The same applies to customers' errors. Shortly after the variation refund, the customer's accounts department pays the main tank invoice of A$24,519 twice, once by bank transfer and once through its supplier portal.

The workshop's bookkeeper sees the duplicate the next morning and returns it the same day, with a short note. Nobody suggests holding it as a credit, and nobody suggests waiting to see whether the customer notices. The money was never the workshop's. Holding it, even with every intention of returning it eventually, would be using another's property without consent, which is exactly the inequality restitution exists to correct.

```figure
- *Restitution | Exactly what was wrongly taken, returned promptly | Owed; the workshop must act without being asked
- Compensation for loss caused | A real cost the other party bore because of the workshop | Owed in full for actual loss; reasonable for lost gains
- Penalty | An amount beyond what was taken | A court or the contract, not the workshop's own choice
- Goodwill | Something extra freely given | The workshop, and it should be called what it is
```

## Get the GST right

A correction to a taxable sale is also a correction to the GST on it. The Australian Taxation Office's guidance says that a supplier must issue an adjustment note when the price of a taxable sale changes, within 28 days of becoming aware of the adjustment or of the purchaser asking for one, unless the GST adjustment is A$75 or less. Here the GST on the overcharge is A$68.25, under the threshold, so a formal adjustment note is not required.

The workshop sends a clear credit document anyway, showing the original invoice number, the corrected hours, the amount excluding GST, the GST and the total, because the customer's bookkeeper will need to account for the change and should not have to reconstruct it. The workshop also records the decreasing adjustment in its own activity statement for the period. Tax rules change and their application depends on the facts, so a business in doubt should check the ATO's current guidance or ask its tax adviser. The principle is not in doubt: the tax on an amount wrongly charged is part of the amount wrongly charged.

## Losses caused without anything being taken

Aquinas's third answer extends restitution beyond things taken. A person who causes another's loss must make it good, even if they took nothing (II-II q.62 a.4). He draws a distinction that is still useful: where property is actually destroyed or a loss actually incurred, full compensation is owed; where what is lost is only an expected gain, some reasonable compensation is owed, not the whole of what might have been gained, because the gain was never certain.

Two claims on the tank job illustrate the difference.

| Claim | Kind | Fair response |
|---|---|---|
| The tank was delivered a day late because the crane was double-booked; the customer's installer charged a A$480 call-out fee for the wasted visit | A loss actually incurred, caused by the workshop | Reimburse the A$480; it is a direct cost the customer would not have borne but for the workshop's failure |
| The customer says the day's delay cost it A$6,000 of product it could have made | An expected gain that was blocked | Discuss; some reasonable compensation may be fair, but not necessarily the full amount, and the contract may govern |

The first is straightforward, and the workshop paid it with the same promptness as the refund. The second is harder. The customer's lost production is real in one sense, but whether it would have been earned that day, and how much of it was really lost rather than deferred, is uncertain. Aquinas's distinction suggests the workshop should not dismiss the claim, but neither is it obliged to treat a possible gain as a certain one. In practice, commercial contracts often limit liability for this kind of loss, and Australian law may affect how far those limits apply. A business facing such a claim should read its contract and, if the amount matters, take advice. What fairness requires is that the workshop engages with the claim honestly rather than hiding behind the contract before it has even looked at the facts.

## Make the error less likely next time

Restitution corrects one error. Prudence asks how to prevent the next. The coding error had a simple cause: the timesheet system offered every open job in one long list, and the welder picked the wrong line. The workshop now does two things. Job codes are restricted to the jobs a person is assigned to that day. And every variation invoice is checked against its estimate before it is sent; a variation that comes in more than 25% over estimate is reviewed by the owner before invoicing. Had that check existed, 14.5 hours against an 8-hour estimate, an 81% overrun, would have been queried before the customer ever saw it.

The workshop also asked a less comfortable question: how many errors like this had gone unnoticed in the past? It could not know for certain, but it could look. The owner reconciled timesheets against attendance for the previous three months, a few hours' work, and found one more misallocation, 2 hours coded to the wrong job, on a fixed-price job where the customer's invoice was unaffected. No other customer had been overcharged. Had another overcharge been found, it would have been refunded in the same way, without waiting to be asked, because the obligation to restore does not lapse because a mistake is old.

That search is the difference between correcting an error and correcting a process. A business that fixes only the errors customers point out will, on average, keep the ones customers miss. A business that looks for its own errors, and pays them back when it finds them, is the only kind whose invoices a customer can safely stop checking. For a small workshop that wants repeat work from industrial customers, that is a commercial advantage as well as a matter of fairness.

## Exercise: two later corrections

Months later, two more invoice errors are found. In the first, a customer was charged 3 hours too many at A$105 an hour. In the second, a different customer was charged 10 hours too many at the same rate. For each, calculate the amount to restore including GST, and say whether a formal adjustment note is required under the ATO guidance described in this chapter.

**Worked answer.** For the first, the overcharge is 3 × 105 = A$315.00 excluding GST, with GST of A$31.50, so A$346.50 is restored. The GST adjustment is under A$75, so a formal adjustment note is not required, though a clear credit document is still good practice. For the second, the overcharge is 10 × 105 = A$1,050.00, with GST of A$105.00, so A$1,155.00 is restored. The GST adjustment is more than A$75, so the workshop must issue an adjustment note within 28 days of becoming aware of the error. In both cases the refund should be made promptly and unconditionally, with the customer told before they find it themselves.
