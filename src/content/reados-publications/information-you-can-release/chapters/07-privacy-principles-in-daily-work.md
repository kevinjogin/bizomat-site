Most privacy decisions in an agency are small and made quickly. Does this form need a date of birth? May I send this list to another branch? What can I tell a caller about the information we hold on her? Each question has an answer in the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), and the answer is easier to give before the question becomes urgent.

This is an illustration. Alongside its FOI work, NRIO's Information Access and Privacy Team handles about 25 privacy enquiries and complaints a year. On Monday 2 August 2027 the secretary of a community group emails the team. She asks for all the information NRIO holds about her personally in its client records, the system where officers log their dealings with community groups and councils. Her email does not mention the FOI Act. Ruth Adeyemi is NRIO's Privacy Officer, but most of the principles that decide the answer are applied, or broken, at someone else's desk.

## Personal information is wider than most people think

The [Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text) defines personal information as "information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable" (s6(1)), whether or not it is true and whether or not it is recorded. An individual is a natural person, so facts about an incorporated group or a council are not personal information; the name and mobile number of its secretary are. An officer's opinion counts too: a note calling someone "difficult" is personal information about them, fair or not.

Some personal information is also sensitive information (s6(1)): information or an opinion about a person's racial or ethnic origin, political opinions or associations, religious or philosophical beliefs, professional, trade or union membership, sexual orientation or practices, or criminal record, together with health information and some genetic and biometric information. Health information includes information about a person's illness, disability or injury (s6FA). A note that a volunteer needs a ground-floor room because of a knee injury is sensitive information, with stricter rules for collecting and using it.

Section 15 says an APP entity "must not do an act, or engage in a practice, that breaches an Australian Privacy Principle". Every agency is an APP entity; the small business exemption concerns organisations, not agencies. A breach of an APP is an interference with the individual's privacy (s13(1)(a)), and the individual may complain to the Information Commissioner (s36(1)). The FOI Act uses the same definition of personal information (FOI Act s4(1)), which is why the two laws meet in s47F and in APP 12.

## APP 1: a policy that matches what the agency does

An APP entity must take reasonable steps to put in place practices, procedures and systems that will ensure it complies with the APPs and any registered APP code that binds it, and that let it deal with inquiries and complaints (APP 1.2). It must have "a clearly expressed and up-to-date policy" about how it manages personal information (APP 1.3), covering what it collects and holds, how and why, how to seek access and correction, how to complain, and whether information is likely to go overseas (APP 1.4). It must take reasonable steps to make the policy available free of charge and in an appropriate form (APP 1.5).

A policy goes out of date quietly. A branch starts a survey or sends data to a contractor, and nobody tells the policy's owner. Ask in every new project and every annual review whether the policy still describes what the agency actually does.

From 10 December 2026, APP 1 gains three subclauses on automated decisions, inserted by the [Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00128/latest/text) (Schedule 1, item 88). They apply where an entity has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing substantially and directly related to making one; the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual's rights or interests; and the program uses personal information about that individual (APP 1.7). The privacy policy must then describe the kinds of personal information used, the kinds of decisions made solely by such programs, and the kinds for which the programs do a thing substantially and directly related to the decision (APP 1.8). Refusing or failing to decide counts, and an effect may be beneficial or adverse (APP 1.9). The rules apply to decisions made after they start, even if the program or the data is older (item 89).

This is an illustration. Ruth's annual review of NRIO's policy now asks every branch three questions. Does a computer program make a decision about individuals, or do a substantial step towards one? Could the decision significantly affect their rights or interests? Does the program use their personal information? Three answers of "yes" put the program in the policy.

## Collect what you need, and say why: APPs 3 and 5

An agency must not collect personal information unless it is "reasonably necessary for, or directly related to, one or more of the entity's functions or activities" (APP 3.1). Sensitive information also needs the person's consent, unless an exception applies, such as collection required or authorised by an Australian law (APP 3.3 and 3.4). Collection must be "only by lawful and fair means" (APP 3.5), and from the person themselves unless they consent, a law requires or authorises otherwise, or asking them is unreasonable or impracticable (APP 3.6).

The test works field by field. A form that asks for a date of birth "in case we need it" fails. So does a call note that records a caller's family troubles when the call was about a hall booking. In a free-text field, write only what you would be content for the person to read, because under APP 12 they may.

At or before collection, or as soon as practicable after it, the entity must take reasonable steps to tell the person the matters in APP 5.2, or otherwise make sure they are aware of them (APP 5.1). They include who is collecting and how to make contact, the purposes, any law that requires or authorises the collection, the main consequences of not providing the information, the usual recipients, the fact that the privacy policy explains access and complaints, and any likely overseas disclosure. Put a notice on every form and a short script on every phone line, and describe the real flows: if information will go to a contractor or the Minister's office, say so. What people were told at collection is the best evidence of what they would reasonably expect later.

## Use it for the purpose it was collected for: APP 6

Information is collected for a primary purpose. An entity must not use or disclose it "for another purpose (the secondary purpose)" unless the person consents or an exception applies (APP 6.1). The exceptions met most often in agency work are:
- the person would reasonably expect it, and the secondary purpose is related to the primary purpose, or directly related if the information is sensitive (APP 6.2(a));
- it is required or authorised by or under an Australian law or a court or tribunal order (APP 6.2(b));
- a permitted general situation in s16A exists (APP 6.2(c)).

Most mistakes start with "we already have it". When you rely on an exception, write down which one and why, because the person may ask, and so may the Commissioner.

## Protect it, and dispose of it lawfully: APP 11

An entity holding personal information must take reasonable steps to protect it "(a) from misuse, interference and loss; and (b) from unauthorised access, modification or disclosure" (APP 11.1). Since 11 December 2024 the Act says those steps include "technical and organisational measures" (APP 11.3): access limited to those who need it and protected files, but also training, a second check before bulk personal information leaves the agency, and rules about which channel may carry what.

APP 11.2 requires an entity to destroy or de-identify personal information it no longer needs, but not if it is in a Commonwealth record. For an agency, almost everything is. Disposal then follows the Archives Act and an authority from the National Archives (Chapter 2). "Delete it to protect privacy" is not a safe instinct in an agency.

This is an illustration. The spreadsheet emailed to the wrong address on Wednesday 23 June 2027 was an APP 11 question before it was a data breach question. Chapter 8 follows both.

## Access and correction: APPs 12 and 13

An entity that holds personal information about an individual must give the individual access to it on request (APP 12.1). An agency is not required to give access to the extent the FOI Act, or another Commonwealth access law, requires or authorises it to refuse (APP 12.2). It must respond "within 30 days after the request is made" (APP 12.4(a)(i)), in the manner requested if reasonable and practicable (APP 12.4(b)), and "must not charge" (APP 12.7). If it refuses, it must take reasonable steps to give access another way that meets both sides' needs (APP 12.5) and give a written notice of the reasons and the ways to complain (APP 12.9).

Under APP 13, if the entity is satisfied that information is inaccurate, out of date, incomplete, irrelevant or misleading for the purpose for which it is held, or the person asks, it must take reasonable steps to correct it (APP 13.1). A refusal needs written reasons (APP 13.3), and on request the entity must take reasonable steps to attach the person's own statement to the information (APP 13.4). It must respond within 30 days and must not charge (APP 13.5). A complaint goes to the agency first: the Commissioner must not investigate one the person did not first make to the agency, unless that was not appropriate (s40(1A)).

Chapter 1 set out the routing rule. A request that states it is an FOI application is an FOI request; one that asks for the person's own information without invoking the FOI Act is an APP 12 request. Because APP 12.2 adopts the FOI Act's grounds for refusal, the result for a person's own information is often similar. The paths differ in form, timing, notices and review, so tell people both exist and let them choose.

## The APP Code: who does what inside the agency

The [Privacy (Australian Government Agencies — Governance) APP Code 2017](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2017L01396/latest/text), in force since 1 July 2018, binds every agency (s7) and sets out how agencies are to comply with APP 1.2 (s8). An agency must:
- have a privacy management plan with specific, measurable goals and targets, and measure and document its performance against the plan at least annually (s9);
- have, at all times, "a designated Privacy Officer" (s10(1)), the primary point of contact for privacy advice, and ensure the Privacy Officer functions are carried out: handling enquiries, complaints and access and correction requests, keeping a record of personal information holdings, helping with privacy impact assessments (PIAs), keeping the PIA register and measuring performance against the plan (s10(4) and (5));
- have, at all times, a designated Privacy Champion who is "a senior official" (s11(3)), and ensure the Champion's functions are carried out, including promoting a privacy culture, reviewing or approving the plan and reporting regularly to the executive (s11(4));
- conduct a PIA "for all high privacy risk projects" (s12(1)): projects it reasonably considers involve new or changed ways of handling personal information likely to have a significant impact on privacy (s12(2));
- keep a register of its PIAs and publish it, or a version of it, on its website (s15(1));
- include privacy training in staff induction, and take reasonable steps to train annually all staff with access to personal information (s16).

This is an illustration. At NRIO, Ruth is the Privacy Officer and the General Counsel, an SES Band 1 officer, is the Privacy Champion. The team keeps the PIA register and the record of holdings. A proposal to record calls to NRIO's enquiry line, for example, would come to Ruth before any contract was signed.

## The statutory tort, and where agencies stand

Since 10 June 2025, Schedule 2 of the Privacy Act has given individuals a cause of action in tort for serious invasions of privacy. The plaintiff must show an intrusion upon their seclusion or a misuse of information about them, a reasonable expectation of privacy, an invasion that was "intentional or reckless" and serious, and a public interest in their privacy that outweighs any countervailing public interest (cl 7(1)). Damage need not be proved (cl 7(2)).

The Schedule does not apply to an invasion of privacy by an agency, or by one of its staff, to the extent it is done in good faith in the performance or purported performance of the agency's functions, or the exercise or purported exercise of its powers (cl 16 and 16A). Intelligence agencies and law enforcement bodies have separate clauses (cl 16B and 17). The exemption depends on good faith and on a function or power: an officer who looks up a neighbour's file out of curiosity is not performing a function of the agency. And it concerns only the tort; the APPs and the Commissioner's complaint process still apply.

## When it goes wrong: the FOI register as a briefing note

This is an illustration. In March 2027 the manager of another NRIO branch was preparing to meet the president of a local historical society about a dispute over a heritage building. He emailed Ethan Brooks to ask whether the president had made FOI requests, "so we know what we're dealing with". Ethan searched the team's case register and replied that afternoon with three requests from the previous two years and their subjects. At the meeting the manager remarked that the president had "been busy with FOI". The president complained to NRIO in writing two days later.

The use fails the APP 6 check at every step. The register's details were collected to process FOI requests; briefing a manager before an unrelated meeting is another purpose. There was no consent. No applicant would reasonably expect it, and the FOI Act says a person's right of access is not affected by their reasons for seeking it (s11(2)). No law required or authorised it. NRIO had breached APP 6.1, an interference with the president's privacy (s13(1)(a)), and having complained to NRIO, the president could go to the Commissioner (s36(1)).

The cost was out of proportion to one email. Ruth spent most of a week establishing what had been said, apologising in writing and briefing the General Counsel. A meeting meant to settle a dispute made it worse, and one community leader learned that asking NRIO for documents gets noticed.

Ethan's instinct, to help a senior colleague quickly, was an ordinary one. The missing control was a stop between the question and the register. NRIO's rule now is that the FOI and privacy case registers are open only to the team, and any request to use their contents for another purpose goes to the Privacy Officer, who runs the APP 6 check and records the answer. The case is now part of the team's annual privacy training (APP Code s16(2)).

## Exercise: the secretary's request

This is an illustration. The secretary's email of Monday 2 August 2027 asks for "all information NRIO holds about me personally" in its client records, with copies by email. It does not mention the FOI Act. Ethan's search finds:
1. her contact record: name, role in the group, mobile number and email address;
2. notes of 9 phone calls and 2 meetings, and 23 emails she sent or received;
3. a 2025 call note, by an officer who has since left NRIO, calling her "combative", with no other detail;
4. in one meeting note, a sentence that another committee member, named, missed the meeting "because of his surgery";
5. an email from NRIO's legal area to a program manager, attached to her record, advising how to respond to a complaint she made in 2026.

Decide the path and due date, what she receives and at what cost, how to treat items 4 and 5 and what notice she must get, and what NRIO must do if she then asks for "combative" to be removed.

### Worked answer

**Path and date.** The email does not state that it is an application for the purposes of the FOI Act, so it is not a valid FOI request (s15(2)(aa)). It is an APP 12 request. The 30 days run from the day after it was made: 3 to 31 August is 29 days, so day 30 is Wednesday 1 September 2027 (APP 12.4(a)(i)). Ethan registers it with that date on the day it arrives. NRIO's practice adds three steps: acknowledge it promptly; confirm her identity before sending anything, because sending her information to someone posing as her would be an unauthorised disclosure (APP 11.1); and tell her she may apply under the FOI Act instead. Handling the request is a Privacy Officer function (APP Code s10(5)(a)).

**What she receives.** Everything about her in items 1 to 3, by email, because she asked for that manner and it is practicable (APP 12.4(b)). That includes the "combative" note, because an opinion is personal information whether or not it is true (s6(1)). NRIO must not charge (APP 12.7).

**Item 4.** The surgery detail is not information about her. It is health information about another man, so it is sensitive information (s6(1), s6FA). APP 12 reaches only information about her, so the detail is left out of her copy and the covering letter says that information about other people has been omitted. Under an FOI request the whole note would be in scope; the detail would be assessed under s47F(1), and her own information in the note could not be exempt only because it relates to her (s47F(3)).

**Item 5.** The email names her and discusses her complaint, so it holds her personal information. It is also legal advice. The FOI Act exempts a document that "would be privileged from production in legal proceedings on the ground of legal professional privilege" (s42(1)), unless privilege has been waived (s42(2)). NRIO is therefore authorised to refuse under the FOI Act, so APP 12.2 lets it refuse under APP 12. Authorised is not required: Ruth could release the advice. If she relies on the exemption, NRIO must take reasonable steps to meet the secretary's needs another way, such as a plain account of how her complaint was handled (APP 12.5), and give a written notice with the reasons and the ways to complain: to NRIO first, then the Commissioner (APP 12.9; s36(1), s40(1A)).

**Correction.** Her request engages APP 13. The client record exists to record NRIO's dealings with the group. Ruth finds that a bare label written two years earlier, with no facts behind it, is irrelevant and misleading for that purpose, so NRIO must take reasonable steps to correct it (APP 13.1). The note is a Commonwealth record, so the correction follows the Information Management Unit's advice: a dated annotation withdrawing the description, not a quiet deletion (Chapter 2). NRIO must respond within 30 days and must not charge (APP 13.5). Had Ruth refused, NRIO would have owed written reasons and the ways to complain (APP 13.3) and, on request, reasonable steps to attach the secretary's own statement to the note (APP 13.4).
