Assessment is where a grant round keeps or breaks the promise its guidelines made: that every applicant will be judged on the published criteria, in the same way as everyone else. Every applicant that misses out can ask why, and the only answer that holds is one the record can show: who scored the application, against what, for what reasons, and who checked.

This is an illustration. Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) closed at 2:00 pm, Canberra time, on Thursday 11 March 2027. It received 186 applications asking for A$41.3 million, more than four times the A$10.0 million available. Sam Reid has from 12 March to 23 April to turn them into a ranked list for the assessment panel, which meets on Wednesday 28 April. Ivy Chen runs the round day to day and Ben Walker coordinates the assessment.

## Check eligibility before anyone scores

Eligibility criteria are pass or fail. An application that fails one is not assessed, however good the project. Assessment criteria are scored, weighted and compared. The CGRPs describe an open competitive process as one in which applications are assessed against the selection criteria, meaning both eligibility and assessment criteria, and against the comparative merits of other applications (11.3). Checking eligibility first keeps assessors' time for applications that can be funded and stops merit leaking into a pass-or-fail question.

This is an illustration. Nadia Haddad checked every application against a checklist taken word for word from the eligibility table in the guidelines (Chapter 3), and Ivy checked every fail before it stood. Nine applications were ineligible:

- three arrived after 2:00 pm on 11 March, on the time recorded at lodgement, and the guidelines accept no late applications;
- four came from an ineligible type of applicant: two unincorporated groups, a company that operates for profit and a state government body;
- two proposed projects in towns outside NRIO's region.

That left 177 applications to assess. Record the criterion and the evidence for each fail, not just the word "ineligible". Keep ineligible applications away from assessors: one who has read a strong project that cannot be funded will compare others with it.

## Write the assessment plan before the round closes

Fairness is mostly settled before the first score. The CGRPs say it is important to document procedures before a grant opportunity starts (13.11). They call it better practice to assess all like applications through a common appraisal process, and say the reasons for any departure should be documented (13.9). And "Officials involved in assessing applications should be appropriately skilled and have access to procedural instructions and/or training before processing grant applications" (14.5). These are "should" statements, but in a round of 186 applications they are the only practical route to consistent results.

This is an illustration. The Director, Regional Programs, approved NRIO's assessment plan before the round closed. It covers who assesses and how they are trained and paired; how scores and reasons are recorded against the published anchors; moderation; conflicts; the probity adviser's role; the timetable; and what the record must hold.

The plan explains how the published criteria are applied. It should never add a criterion, change a weight or move the minimum. If the two disagree, the guidelines win.

## Score against the anchors, two assessors to an application

A scoring anchor is a written description of what an application at a given score looks like. Chapter 3 published anchors from 0 to 10 for each criterion, and showed how the weighted score is calculated and why the minimum of 5 applies before any ranking. Anchors turn "I think this is a 7" into "this matches the description for 7, for these reasons".

This is an illustration. Two assessors score every eligible application independently. Each reads the whole application and its attachments, scores the three criteria against the anchors, and writes a reason for every score that points to the evidence. Neither sees the other's scores until both are submitted. Ben varies the pairs, and nobody scores an application from a town they have declared a link to.

Score only what is in the application and its attachments, because that is all every applicant had the chance to provide, and write down anything else you rely on. The CGRPs say "grant assessors should document when referring to, or relying on, knowledge or documentation other than the application form" (13.9). This is an illustration. NRIO's own rule: outside knowledge is recorded but does not count towards a score.

## Plan the workload in hours

A round that runs short of assessors in week three is tempted to cut corners. Plan assessment like any other workload: count the units, time one unit, work out each person's productive hours in the period, then divide. The result is in full-time equivalents (FTE): the number of full-time people the work needs.

This is an illustration. With 177 applications and two assessors each, Round 1 needs 354 assessments. Ivy timed herself and Ben on three practice applications written for the training. A careful assessment took about 150 minutes: 90 to read the form, plan, budget, quotes and supporting material, 45 to score the criteria and write the reasons, and 15 to record it all. Individual scoring runs from Monday 22 March to Friday 16 April 2027, which is 18 working days after Good Friday and Easter Monday. NRIO plans on a paid day of 7.5 hours, and Sam asked the assessors not to book leave in the period. He assumed 80 per cent of paid hours would be productive, because training, a calibration session, weekly check-ins and questions all take time. These are illustrative planning figures, not standards.

```figure
type: formula
title: Assessors needed for Round 1
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's planning figures for 18 working days of individual scoring. The FTE is rounded only at the end.
formula: Workload hours = assessments × minutes per assessment ÷ 60
formula: Productive hours per person = paid hours × productive share
formula: FTE = workload hours ÷ productive hours per person
- Assessments | 177 applications × 2 assessors = 354
- Minutes per assessment | 150, from three timed practice runs
- Paid hours | 18 working days × 7.5 hours = 135 per person
- Productive share | 80%
step: 354 × 150 = 53,100 minutes; 53,100 ÷ 60 = 885 hours
step: 135 × 80% = 108 productive hours per person
step: 885 ÷ 108 = 8.19 FTE, so 9 assessors
```

The [Staffing Need](/tools/staffing-need/) tool runs the same calculation. It takes the units of work, minutes per unit, paid hours per person and an efficiency figure, and gives the FTE requirement and the number of whole people. Use it to check the arithmetic or test another assumption.

This is an illustration. Sam had 11 officials from Regional Programs and two other NRIO branches trained on Thursday 18 March: nine to assess and two in reserve, for illness and conflicts. Nine assessors give 9 × 108 = 972 productive hours against 885 hours of work, a margin of 87 hours. Ivy and Ben stay out of the pool so they can moderate with fresh eyes. Because 150 minutes came from only three practice runs, Ben records how long each assessment actually takes, and the plan is checked at the end of week two.

## Moderate differences with reasons, not averages

Moderation turns two assessors' scores into one, with a reason. It corrects misreadings and keeps the scale consistent; it does not split the difference.

This is an illustration. NRIO's plan sets one rule for each criterion:

- If the two scores are within 2 points and on the same side of the minimum of 5, the criterion score is their average.
- If they differ by 3 points or more, or one is below 5 and the other is 5 or above, Ivy or Ben moderates. The two assessors and the moderator go back to the application and the anchors, and agree a score with a written reason.
- If they cannot agree, a third assessor scores that criterion and the panel decides.

The second trigger exists because of the minimum. Scores of 4 and 6 are only 2 points apart, but their average of 5 would pass an application that one assessor found below the minimum, with no reason given. In the reconciliation week, 19 to 23 April, the rule applied to 38 of the 177 applications, about one in five.

Ben also compares each assessor's average scores with their partners' on the same applications; a large, steady gap means someone is reading the anchors differently. Keep both original scores beside the moderated one. Overwriting a score destroys the evidence that moderation happened.

## The panel and the probity adviser

This is an illustration. The Director, Regional Programs, chairs the assessment panel, with Sam and a director from another NRIO branch as members. On Wednesday 28 April 2027 it met with the moderated results. Of the 177 applications assessed, 61 scored at least 5 on every criterion. In rank order, the first 31 fitted within the A$10.0 million, asking for A$9.92 million between them.

```figure

title: Round 1, from applications received to recommendations
caption: This is an illustration. Of 61 applications that met the minimum, the A$10.0 million funds the first 31 in rank order.
- Applications received | 186 | A$41.3 million requested
- Eligible and assessed | 177 | 9 ineligible: 3 late, 4 applicant type, 2 outside the region
- Met the minimum of 5 on every criterion | 61

```

The panel checks the process and owns the ranked list: that every application was assessed the same way, that each moderated score has a reason, that conflicts were handled, and where the funding line falls. It is not a third round of scoring, and changes a score only where it records a reason tied to the anchors and the evidence.

This is an illustration. Hartley Probity Services, the probity adviser engaged for Round 1, wrote the probity plan, briefed the assessors, reviewed every conflict declaration, observed the panel and gave the Director a written probity report for the brief. A probity adviser advises; it does not make decisions or take over officials' responsibility for them.

## Declare conflicts early and act on them

The CGRPs describe a conflict of interest as arising when a decision or a power may be, or may be seen to be, influenced by "material personal interests (financial or non-financial) or material personal associations" (15.6). Conflicts can arise at any stage (15.7). Section 29 of the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) says an official who has "a material personal interest that relates to the affairs of the entity must disclose details of the interest". Paragraph 3.12 of the [CGRPs](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) confirms this duty applies to grants administration. How an agency finds and manages conflicts is better practice: accountable authorities should put mechanisms in place, which may include declaration procedures and a register of interests (15.9).

This is an illustration. NRIO's plan asks every assessor and panel member to declare interests in writing before any allocation, against the list of eligible applicants and their towns, and at once if a link appears later. Family ties, club memberships, past employment and close friendships count, not only money. A declaration is not an admission of wrongdoing. It is the start of a decision.

```figure
type: decision
title: After an assessor declares an interest
question: Could this interest, financial or not, influence the assessment, or be seen to?
- No | Record the declaration and the decision, then carry on | The reason goes in the round's conflicts register
- Yes, for one application | Move that application to another assessor | Set aside any scores already given; a reserve scores it afresh
- Yes, for a group of applications | Remove the assessor from every related application | Ask the probity adviser whether other scores need review
- *Unsure | Treat it as a conflict and ask the probity adviser | Decide, record it and tell the assessor in writing
caption: This is an illustration. Under NRIO's plan, Sam decides for assessors, the Director for panel members and the Branch Manager for the Director.
```

Record each decision in the conflicts register with its reason and date, and tell the person in writing what they may and may not do. A declaration that nobody decides on protects nobody.

## Keep a record of every score and its reason

Paragraph 3.15 of the CGRPs is direct: "Officials must retain appropriate records, consistent with their accountability obligations, and ensure the records are available on request." For an assessment, appropriate means a record from which someone who was not there could rebuild each score and see why it was given.

```figure
type: checklist
title: What the assessment record shows for each application
caption: Keep it in the grants system, not in assessors' spreadsheets or email, so that the brief, feedback and any later review all draw on one record.
- Eligibility result | who checked it, against which criterion, and the second check of any fail
- Assessors and declarations | both names, their declarations for this applicant and the dates
- Scores and reasons | each assessor's score on each criterion, with the evidence and anchor relied on
- Outside knowledge | anything relied on beyond the application, written down (13.9)
- Moderation | the trigger, the agreed score and its reason, or the panel's decision
- Weighted score and rank | calculated by the system and checked before the panel
- x Overwritten scores | add the moderated score beside the originals; never replace them
```

Test each reason by imagining it read aloud to the applicant. If it would embarrass the assessor, it is not yet a good reason.

## When it goes wrong: a brother on the club committee

This is an illustration. On Thursday 18 March, at the training, one of the nine assessors declared no conflicts against the list of eligible applicants. On Tuesday 6 April she scored the application from Brindlemere Football Netball Club Inc. for its A$310,000 change-room upgrade. On Tuesday 20 April, checking papers for the panel, Ben noticed the club's treasurer shared her uncommon surname. Asked, she confirmed he is her brother. She had not declared it because she had no money in the club and thought conflicts were about financial interests.

The CGRPs answer that point directly: a material personal association is enough (15.6). Her scores for the club were the higher of the pair on two criteria. With them, the club's weighted score was 7.90 and it ranked 10th.

Sam acted the same day, on the probity adviser's advice. Her scores were set aside, not deleted, and on Wednesday 21 April a reserve assessor scored the application afresh without seeing either earlier set. Combined with the other assessor's original scores under the moderation rule, the result was 7.60, and the club moved to 12th, still well inside the funding line. Hartley compared her other assessments with her partners' scores and found no pattern. The conflicts register, the probity report and the panel papers record what happened. Whether she breached a duty is a separate question for NRIO's people processes.

The cost was two days of rework for four people and a ranking that had been wrong for two weeks. Found after the Minister's decision, through a complaint or an audit, the same fact would have put a conflicted score inside an approved ranked list, and every other score in the round would have been doubted.

The control that would have caught it is cheap: declare against the people named in each application, such as office-bearers and contacts, not only against organisation names. NRIO now puts one question at the top of every score sheet, answered before the scoring fields open: "Do you, a family member or a close associate have any link to this applicant or the people it names?" The briefing now says plainly that family and personal associations count.

## Exercise: moderate one application and re-plan the workload

This is an illustration.

**Part 1.** Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. has applied for A$85,000 to fit out its workshop. Apply NRIO's moderation rule to its two assessors' scores, settle any criterion that needs moderation, and calculate the weighted score.

| Criterion (weight) | Assessor A | Assessor B | Reasons given |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Community need and benefit (40%) | 8 | 7 | Both: the town's only regular social program for older men, with two years of use data |
| 2. Capacity to deliver (30%) | 4 | 7 | A: "No quote for the A$18,000 dust extraction system; no risk plan for working while the shed stays open." B: "Clear timetable and budget; I know the shed delivered a similar fit-out in 2024." |
| 3. Value for money (30%) | 7 | 7 | Both: costs are reasonable for the use described |

At moderation, Ivy and the assessors find that attachment 4 is a one-page risk table covering works while the shed stays open. The dust extraction cost is a supplier's published price, not a quote. Nothing in the application mentions a 2024 project.

**Part 2.** Suppose that by Friday 2 April, after eight working days, the nine assessors have completed 130 of the 354 assessments. They are taking 175 minutes each on average, not 150, mostly because checking budgets against quotes takes longer than the practice runs suggested. Ten working days remain until Friday 16 April. Using the same 7.5-hour day and 80 per cent productive share, work out the FTE needed for the remaining assessments, and say what Sam should do.

### Worked answer

**Part 1.** On criterion 1, scores of 8 and 7 are within 2 points and both at least 5, so the score is their average, 7.5. On criterion 3 both gave 7, so the score is 7. On criterion 2, scores of 4 and 7 differ by 3 points and sit either side of the minimum, so they must be moderated.

The evidence closes most of the gap. A's second point is wrong: the risk table is in attachment 4. A's first point is partly right: a published price is weaker evidence than a quote for a major item, and A$18,000 is about 21 per cent of the grant (18,000 ÷ 85,000 = 0.21). B's 2024 project is outside knowledge. B was right to write it down, as 13.9 asks, but under NRIO's plan it does not count. Read against the anchors, the application has a sound plan, a managed risk and one significant cost on weaker evidence. The assessors agree on 6 and record that reason.

The weighted score is 0.4 × 7.5 + 0.3 × 6 + 0.3 × 7 = 3.0 + 1.8 + 2.1 = 6.90. Every criterion is at least 5, so the application meets the minimum. It ranked 28th, inside the funding line.

Moderation changed the outcome. Had A's 4 stood, the application would have failed the minimum. Had B's 7 stood, the score would have been 3.0 + 2.1 + 2.1 = 7.20, resting partly on knowledge no other applicant could match. Averaging 4 and 7 to 5.5 would have given 3.0 + 1.65 + 2.1 = 6.75, a number neither assessor holds and no reason explains.

**Part 2.** The remaining assessments are 354 − 130 = 224.

- Workload: 224 × 175 = 39,200 minutes; 39,200 ÷ 60 = 653.3 hours.
- Productive hours per person: 10 days × 7.5 hours = 75 paid hours; 75 × 80 per cent = 60 hours.
- FTE: 653.3 ÷ 60 = 10.89, so 11 people.

Nine assessors give 9 × 60 = 540 hours, 113.3 hours short: about 39 assessments at 175 minutes (113.3 × 60 ÷ 175 = 38.9). Both reserves make 11 assessors and 660 hours, a margin of only 6.7 hours, or about two assessments. The Staffing Need tool flags a margin that thin, here 0.11 FTE.

Sam should bring both reserves in from Monday 5 April, after a calibration hour with Ben, and hold Monday 19 April as a catch-up day. That day adds 11 × 7.5 × 80 per cent = 66 productive hours, enough for about 22 more assessments, and still leaves four days for moderation.

Sam should not ask the assessors to get back to 150 minutes. The extra time is the budget checking that criterion 2 requires, and cutting it for the applications scored last would mean like applications were no longer assessed alike. He records the timetable change, and its reason, in the assessment plan.
