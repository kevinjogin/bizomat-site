On her first morning at the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office, Lena Moss was given a folder and a request. The folder held the agency's financial delegations instrument, its Accountable Authority Instructions, the branch budget and the corporate plan. The request came by email from one of the regional site coordinators. A contractor was ready to repair a leaking depot roof, and the coordinator wanted the new business manager to sign the order that day.

In Lena's private-sector jobs, the person with the budget and the right title signed. A Commonwealth entity works differently. The authority to commit public money does not come with a job title or a seat at the management table. It comes from an Act of Parliament, through written instruments, to particular officials, with limits and conditions attached. The first skill in a business manager's job is knowing exactly where that authority starts and stops, for yourself and for everyone who asks you to sign.

## Six layers of rules

Public money in a non-corporate Commonwealth entity is governed in layers. Each layer is made under the one above it, or must fit inside it. A business manager works mostly in the bottom three, but every decision has to stand up against all six.

At the top is the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013, the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text). Its objects include requiring entities "to use and manage public resources properly". It sets the duties of the accountable authority and of every official, defines proper use, creates the power to commit money and allows that power to be delegated. Below it sits the PGPA Rule 2014, made under the Act, which fills in detail such as how approvals of spending are recorded.

The third layer holds the Commonwealth Procurement Rules, made under section 105B(1) of the PGPA Act, and other whole-of-government policies. Examples are the Commonwealth Risk Management Policy from the Department of Finance and the Commonwealth Fraud and Corruption Control Framework 2024 from the Attorney-General's Department. Officials of non-corporate Commonwealth entities must comply with Division 1 of the CPRs for every procurement, and with Division 2 as well when the value is at or above the relevant threshold and no exemption applies.

The bottom three layers belong to the agency. Its Accountable Authority Instructions are the accountable authority's directions on how the rules apply in this entity. They can add steps and tighten limits, but they cannot permit what a higher layer forbids. The delegations instrument gives particular powers to particular officials. The branch's own procedures, its forms, checklists and review steps, turn everything above into daily work. Lena owns some of these and can improve them, within the layers above.

When sources seem to disagree, the higher layer wins. When a branch procedure is silent, look one layer up, and ask the area that owns it: the CFO's area for the instructions and delegations, central procurement for buying.

## Proper use: the test behind every approval

Section 8 of the PGPA Act gives the test that runs through this book: "proper, when used in relation to the use or management of public resources, means efficient, effective, economical and ethical." Public resources means relevant money, relevant property or appropriations. Relevant money is money held by the Commonwealth or a corporate Commonwealth entity, or standing to the credit of one of their bank accounts.

In plain terms, each word asks a question you can put to any request:

- Efficient: does the process use no more time, effort or money than the job needs?
- Effective: will the spending achieve what it is for, and does that connect to the agency's purposes?
- Economical: is this the least costly way to get the result needed, over the whole life of the thing?
- Ethical: is it honest, fair and free of conflicts, and would it look that way to someone outside?

This is an illustration. Lena put the four questions to the morning's request. The order was for A$27,500 including GST. The roof was leaking over stored equipment, so the repair was needed and urgent. Grace, the procurement officer, confirmed that the coordinator had sought quotes in the way NRIO's procedures require at that value. The amount was within Lena's delegation. She approved the order that afternoon and recorded her approval in writing. The checks took a few hours, not a week, which is part of being efficient too.

## Two sets of duties

The PGPA Act places duties at two levels.

The accountable authority carries duties for the whole entity. Section 15 requires it to govern the entity in a way that promotes the proper use and management of the public resources it is responsible for, the achievement of the entity's purposes, and the entity's financial sustainability. Section 16 requires it to establish and maintain "an appropriate system of risk oversight and management" and "an appropriate system of internal control", including measures directed at ensuring officials comply with the finance law.

Those duties reach Lena through the systems built to meet them: the instructions, the delegations, the risk framework and the controls inside the finance and purchasing systems. When Lena decides who checks an invoice or who reviews the monthly report, she is building a small part of the system of internal control that section 16 requires.

Every official also carries general duties personally, at every level from the administration officer to the agency head:

- Section 25: exercise the care and diligence a reasonable person would exercise in the official's position and circumstances.
- Section 26: act honestly, in good faith and for a proper purpose.
- Section 27: do not improperly use your position to gain a benefit or advantage for yourself or another person, or to cause detriment to the entity, the Commonwealth or anyone else.
- Section 28: the same duty for information you obtain as an official.
- Section 29: disclose details of any material personal interest that relates to the affairs of the entity.

The words "position and circumstances" in section 25 matter. A reasonable business manager is expected to read a delegation before using it and to check the numbers before signing. The standard rises with the role.

## Delegation: power lent in writing

Section 23 gives the accountable authority of a non-corporate Commonwealth entity the power, on behalf of the Commonwealth, to enter into, vary and administer arrangements and to approve commitments of relevant money. "An arrangement includes a contract, agreement, deed or understanding." Section 110 allows the accountable authority to delegate powers like these to an official "by written instrument". Its exceptions include the accountable authority's own general duties, which stay with it.

So when Lena approves an order, she is not using authority of her own. She is exercising the accountable authority's power, lent to her in writing, on the terms the instrument sets. The guide to section 18 of the PGPA Rule says an official acting under a delegation must exercise the power consistently with the delegation, comply with the accountable authority's instructions, and comply with sections 25 to 29 of the Act.

This is an illustration. NRIO's instrument and limits are fictional and are not Commonwealth rules. Lena may approve commitments of relevant money up to A$250,000. The Facilities Director may approve up to A$500,000, and the Branch Manager up to A$2,000,000. NRIO's limits include GST, in the same way that the CPRs state their thresholds inclusive of GST. Site coordinators hold no delegation to approve commitments. NRIO's Accountable Authority Instructions add a condition: Lena cannot approve a procurement method above the A$125,000 procurement threshold without central procurement's sign-off.

The dollar figure is one line of the instrument, and the rest matters as much. An instrument may list powers separately, so approving a commitment and varying a contract may sit with different people. It may set conditions, such as procurement steps that must be finished first. Read your own delegation, in the version in force, before you use it.

## The APS Values and the Code of Conduct

As an APS employee, Lena is also bound by the Public Service Act 1999. Section 10 sets six APS Values: Committed to service, Ethical, Respectful, Accountable, Impartial and Stewardship. Accountable means being "open and accountable to the Australian community under the law and within the framework of Ministerial responsibility", which for a business manager means records that explain each decision to someone who was not in the room.

Stewardship was added by the Public Service Amendment Act 2024. It describes an APS that "builds its capability and institutional knowledge, and supports the public interest now and into the future, by understanding the long-term impacts of what it does". In business management, Stewardship is concrete: procedures someone else can follow, records that still make sense in five years, whole-of-life thinking about assets, and a team that is better trained when you leave than when you arrived.

The parts of the APS Code of Conduct in section 13 that a business manager uses most are: behave honestly and with integrity (13(1)); act with care and diligence (13(2)); comply with lawful and reasonable directions (13(5)); take reasonable steps to avoid any conflict of interest, real or apparent, and disclose material personal interests (13(7)); use Commonwealth resources in a proper manner and for a proper purpose (13(8)); and do not give false or misleading information in response to an official request (13(9)).

Notice the overlap. Section 16 of the PGPA Rule requires officials to disclose material personal interests in line with the accountable authority's instructions, and notes that the Public Service Act has "a similar, but separate, requirement in subsection 13(7)". Lena has to meet both, so she follows NRIO's instructions on how to disclose.

## Leading at Executive Level 1

The Australian Public Service Commission's Integrated Leadership System describes five EL1 capabilities: Shapes strategic thinking; Achieves results; Cultivates productive working relationships; Exemplifies personal drive and integrity; and Communicates with influence. The examples below are this book's own.

Shapes strategic thinking: connecting the budget and the buying to the corporate plan purpose, "Regional communities have safe, working public facilities". Achieves results: approvals turned around in days, and a monthly report that arrives on time and is right. Cultivates productive working relationships: steady contact with the program, central procurement and the CFO's area, so problems surface while there is time to act. Exemplifies personal drive and integrity: owning errors, declaring interests and refusing to sign what you have not checked. Communicates with influence: explaining why a rule applies in a way that wins cooperation rather than resentment.

## What the business manager decides, and what they do not

Everyone asks the business manager. Part of the job is knowing which questions are yours to answer, which you advise on, and which belong to someone else.

Most of the value sits in the middle column. A business manager who decides too much takes over choices that belong to others. One who decides too little becomes a post box. The test is the instrument and the instructions, not the organisation chart.

By the end of her first week, Lena had met her team: Arun in finance, Grace in procurement, Tom in contracts and Jas in administration. None of them held a delegation to approve commitments, and all of them knew who did.

## When it goes wrong: a title is not a delegation

This is an illustration. In Lena's second week, Arun brought her an invoice for A$18,500 including GST for emergency plumbing at a community building. A regional site coordinator had placed the order by phone and confirmed it by email, signing as "Regional Site Lead". No delegate had approved it. The coordinator had held a small spending delegation in an earlier role and assumed a similar title carried the same power. NRIO's instrument gives site coordinators no power to approve commitments.

Nobody had acted dishonestly. The repair was needed, the price was reasonable and the supplier had done the work in good faith. But a commitment of relevant money had been made by someone with no power to make it, and nobody who held the power had approved it in writing.

Lena's first instinct was the private-sector one: sign the order now and move on. She did not, because a signature on an order that reads as if she approved it last week would create a misleading record. She wrote down the facts: who ordered what, when, at what price and why. She reported it to the CFO's area, as NRIO's instructions require when a commitment is made without a delegation, and asked how the invoice should be handled. She left the payment decision to the people the instructions give it to.

Then she fixed the cause. She sent every coordinator a one-page table of who may approve what. She asked the finance area to make the purchasing system refuse an order without a delegate's approval. She set up an urgent-work route: the coordinator rings Lena, or the Facilities Director if Lena is away, and the delegate approves by phone and records the approval in writing as soon as practicable, as section 18 of the PGPA Rule requires.

What should have happened is simple. The coordinator should have asked who could approve the work before placing the order, and NRIO should have made the answer easy to find. A title describes a job. Only the instrument confers the power.

## Exercise: map your own authority

List five decisions you make, or expect to make, in the next month. For each one, write down the power you would use and the instrument and clause that give it to you; your limit, and whether it includes GST; any condition or earlier step, such as another area's sign-off; the record you must keep; and who decides if the answer is not you. Then ask your finance area to check the map.
