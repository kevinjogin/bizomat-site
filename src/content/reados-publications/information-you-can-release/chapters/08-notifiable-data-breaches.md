A data breach usually starts as a small mistake: an email to the wrong address, a lost laptop, a shared folder open to the wrong group. What happens in the first hours decides how much harm follows. What is written down in the following days decides whether the agency can show it met the law.

This is an illustration. On Wednesday 23 June 2027, while grant agreements are going out, an RCFP officer emails a spreadsheet to the wrong external address. It holds the 31 grantees' contact names, phone numbers and bank account details, and the recipient is a private individual whose email address resembles the intended one. Ruth Adeyemi leads NRIO's assessment under Part IIIC of the Privacy Act, the notifiable data breaches scheme. This chapter follows it step by step.

## What Part IIIC covers

Part IIIC of the [Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text) sets up a scheme for notifying eligible data breaches (s26WA). For an agency it applies to personal information the agency holds and must protect under APP 11.1 (s26WE(1)(a)), which for most agencies means nearly all of it. Three points shape everything that follows.

First, the scheme is about personal information and the individuals it describes. Information about an incorporated body or a council is not personal information, but the people named alongside it are individuals.

Second, it sits on top of APP 11, not in place of it. A breach that turns out not to be notifiable may still show that the agency's steps to protect information were not reasonable.

Third, its duties are enforceable. If an entity contravenes the duty to assess (s26WH(2)), to give the Commissioner a statement (s26WK(2)) or to notify individuals (s26WL(3)), the contravention is taken to be an interference with the privacy of an individual (s13(4A)).

## When a breach is eligible

Under s26WE(2)(a) there is an eligible data breach when there is unauthorised access to, or unauthorised disclosure of, the information, and a reasonable person would conclude that the access or disclosure "would be likely to result in serious harm to any of the individuals to whom the information relates". Loss counts too. Information lost in circumstances where unauthorised access or disclosure is likely is assessed as if that access or disclosure had happened (s26WE(2)(b)). An individual covered by the test is at risk from the eligible data breach (s26WE(2)(d)).

The test is objective. It asks what a reasonable person would conclude, not what the agency hopes, and it looks forward: harm need not have happened yet. It also leaves things out. How a notification would look, who might ask about it and whether the program is in the news are not part of it.

The Act defines neither "serious harm" nor "likely". Record how you read them in each assessment, and check the Information Commissioner's current guidance. Section 26WG lists the matters to have regard to:
- the kind or kinds of information, and its sensitivity;
- whether security measures protect it, and the likelihood that they could be overcome;
- the persons, or kinds of persons, who have obtained or could obtain it;
- where a technology such as encryption made it unintelligible, the likelihood that people intending harm could obtain what they need to get around it;
- the nature of the harm, and any other relevant matters.

Think about harm from the individuals' side: money lost to a scam, misuse of their identity, distress, damage to reputation or relationships, or a risk to their safety.

## Contain first: remedial action can change the answer

Containment is more than good practice, because it can change the legal result. Under s26WF(1), if an entity takes action in relation to an access or disclosure covered by s26WE(2)(a), does so before it results in serious harm to any of the individuals, and as a result a reasonable person would conclude that serious harm is not likely, the access or disclosure "is not, and is taken never to have been" an eligible data breach. If the action protects some individuals but not others, the entity need not notify those it protected (s26WF(2)). Similar rules cover lost information, such as a laptop recovered before anyone could open it (s26WF(3) to (5)).

Two conditions do the work. The action must come before serious harm, and it must actually change what a reasonable person would conclude. A request to delete that goes unanswered changes little. A prompt, specific confirmation from someone with no reason to misuse the information can change a lot. So containment needs evidence: what was done, when, by whom and what shows it worked.

This is an illustration. NRIO's first hours on Wednesday 23 June 2027 went like this:
- The officer meant to send the spreadsheet to NRIO's CFO area, which sets up grant payments. The email program completed a private address that differs by a few characters. At about 2:40 pm, half an hour after sending, the officer noticed and tried to recall the message. The recall failed: the email had left NRIO's system.
- The officer told Sam Reid, the RCFP manager, who rang Ruth at 3:10 pm.
- At 3:30 pm Ruth emailed the recipient. The message said the email had been sent in error and held other people's information, and asked the recipient not to forward or keep it, to delete it, including from deleted items, and to confirm in writing.
- The CFO area applied its call-back check to any request to change these grantees' bank details, and Ethan Brooks opened an entry in the breach register.
- Nobody deleted NRIO's own copy of the sent email. It is a Commonwealth record and the main evidence of what was sent (Chapter 2).

## The assessment and its 30 days

Section 26WH applies when an entity is aware that there are reasonable grounds to suspect there may have been an eligible data breach, but is not aware of reasonable grounds to believe there has been one (s26WH(1)). The entity must "carry out a reasonable and expeditious assessment" of whether there are reasonable grounds to believe, and take all reasonable steps to ensure the assessment is completed within 30 days after it became aware (s26WH(2)). If it already has reasonable grounds to believe, s26WK applies instead and the statement to the Commissioner is due as soon as practicable.

Read the words closely. "Expeditious" means the assessment moves quickly; 30 days is the outer limit, not the target. "All reasonable steps to ensure" describes effort rather than a guarantee, but a contravention of s26WH(2) is still an interference with privacy (s13(4A)).

The 30 days run from the day after the entity became aware. NRIO counts them as the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 counts a period that begins after a specified day, leaving that day out (s36(1), item 6). This is an illustration. NRIO became aware on Wednesday 23 June, when its officer realised the mistake. From 24 to 30 June is 7 days and from 1 to 23 July is 23 more, so day 30 is Friday 23 July 2027. Ruth counts from the officer's realisation, not from the call that reached her half an hour later, because NRIO's rule is that the agency knows what any of its officers knows.

That afternoon Ruth recorded her starting position in the register. The disclosure was plainly unauthorised. She had grounds to suspect an eligible data breach, but not yet to believe one: she did not know who held the file, whether it had been opened or what they might do with it. The assessment would turn on facts she could expect to have within days.

Part IIIC places the duties on the entity, not on a named officer. NRIO's own response plan names the Privacy Officer to lead the assessment and decide, and requires her to tell the Privacy Champion, the General Counsel, the same day. Ruth did.

## Notifying the Commissioner and the individuals

Once an entity is aware that there are reasonable grounds to believe there has been an eligible data breach, it must prepare a statement and give a copy to the Commissioner "as soon as practicable" (s26WK(2)). The statement must set out:
- the entity's identity and contact details;
- a description of the eligible data breach;
- the kind or kinds of information concerned; and
- recommendations about the steps individuals should take in response (s26WK(3)).

The entity must then notify the contents of the statement, taking reasonable steps. It may notify every individual to whom the information relates, or only those at risk from the breach, if notifying that group is practicable. If neither is practicable, it publishes the statement on its website and takes reasonable steps to publicise it (s26WL(2)). It must do this as soon as practicable after preparing the statement (s26WL(3)), and it may use the method it normally uses to communicate with each person (s26WL(4)).

The Commissioner has powers at both ends. The Commissioner may declare that the statement and notification sections do not apply to a breach, or extend the time to notify (s26WQ). The Commissioner may also direct an entity to notify a breach when the Commissioner is aware of reasonable grounds to believe it happened (s26WR).

## The breach register

This is an illustration. Ethan keeps NRIO's breach register. Part IIIC does not prescribe one; it is NRIO's own practice, and its value is as evidence. Every suspected breach gets an entry, including those found not to be eligible, because the s26WH assessment has to be done either way and the register is how NRIO shows it was. The entries also feed NRIO's annual measurement against its privacy management plan (APP Code s9(3)) and supply the examples used in training.

## When it goes wrong: a breach "fixed" with no assessment recorded

This is an illustration. In February 2027 an NRIO facilities officer emailed a list of 12 volunteers who open community halls for maintenance visits to the wrong contractor. The list gave their names, home addresses and mobile numbers. The officer's manager rang the contractor, was told the email would be deleted, thanked them and treated the matter as closed. Nobody told Ruth's team, and nothing was written down.

Ethan learned of it in May, when the manager mentioned it in passing at a training session. Ruth asked the contractor for written confirmation. The person who had taken the call had left, and the contractor could say only that its systems no longer held the list. Ruth's assessment, three months late, concluded that serious harm to the volunteers was not likely, given who had received the list and what it contained.

That may well have been the right answer in February. But for three months NRIO had no assessment, which s26WH(2) required, and a contravention of that duty is an interference with privacy (s13(4A)). The evidence that would have supported the remedial action exception, a dated confirmation from a named person, was never collected. NRIO chose to tell the 12 volunteers, and had to explain to them why it was doing so three months after the event.

The manager's call was sensible containment. The failure was treating containment as the end of the matter. NRIO's rule now is that anyone who suspects a breach tells the team's mailbox the same day, Ethan opens an entry, and only a recorded assessment closes it, even when the answer is "not eligible". The rule is part of induction training (APP Code s16(1)).

## Exercise: assess the spreadsheet breach

This is an illustration. These are the facts on Friday 25 June 2027:
- The spreadsheet has 31 rows: the grantee's name, the contact person's name and phone number (mostly mobiles), and the account name, BSB and account number for payments. It was not password protected.
- The grantees are incorporated not-for-profit organisations and councils. The bank accounts are theirs, not the contacts'.
- On Thursday 24 June, after Ruth's second email, the recipient replied. She had opened the attachment before realising it was not meant for her.
- On Friday 25 June she confirmed in writing that she had deleted the email and attachment from her inbox and deleted items, had not forwarded, saved, printed or copied them, and had no connection with any grantee.
- No grantee has reported unusual contact, and the CFO area's call-back check is in place.

Answer four questions. Is this an eligible data breach, with reasons under s26WE, s26WF and s26WG? What must NRIO do, and by when? What should it do that the law does not require? And what would change if, on Monday 12 July 2027, one grantee's contact reported a call from someone claiming to be from NRIO who quoted the group's account number and asked for her online banking details?

### Worked answer

**1. Not an eligible data breach, on the facts at Friday 25 June.** Start with what is personal information. The 31 contacts' names and phone numbers are personal information about identified individuals (s6(1)). The bank details belong to incorporated bodies and councils, so on their own they are not information about an individual. They still matter to harm: a caller who can quote a group's real account number and its grant makes a convincing impostor when ringing the named contact.

The disclosure was unauthorised. The spreadsheet went to a person NRIO did not intend to send it to and who had no reason to hold it (s26WE(2)(a)(i)). The question is serious harm, and the figure sets the s26WG matters as they stood on the Wednesday and on the Friday.

```figure

- Kind of information | Names and phone numbers of 31 contacts, beside the groups' bank details | Unchanged
- Sensitivity | No sensitive information; financial details that would lend a scam credibility | Unchanged
- Security measures | None: the file had no password | None, but the file has been deleted
- Who obtained it | An unknown holder of a lookalike address | An identified private individual with no link to any grantee
- What they did with it | Unknown | Opened it; did not forward, save, print or copy it; deleted it
- Nature of the harm | Scam calls to named contacts, with possible financial loss | The same harm, now unlikely
- Position | Grounds to suspect; assess under s26WH | Serious harm not likely (s26WE, s26WF)
```

On the Wednesday, a reasonable person could not rule out serious harm: an unknown holder, an unprotected file and details that would support a scam against 31 named people expecting grant payments. That is why NRIO had grounds to suspect and had to assess. By the Friday, NRIO's action had produced a prompt, specific and consistent written statement from an identified person with no link to the grantees, and nothing suggested misuse. On those facts a reasonable person would conclude that serious harm to any of the 31 is not likely.

Ruth does not need to decide whether the disclosure would have been an eligible data breach without NRIO's action. Either it never met s26WE(2)(a)(ii), or it did and s26WF(1) applies, because NRIO acted before any harm and the action changed what a reasonable person would conclude, so that it "is not, and is taken never to have been" an eligible data breach. Either way it is not an eligible data breach. With no reasonable grounds to believe there has been one, s26WK and s26WL do not apply. Ruth's record also states the limit of the evidence: NRIO cannot verify the deletion and relies on the recipient's statement, for the reasons given. The conclusion rests on the test alone. A journalist's FOI request about the same program was open at the time, and it played no part either way.

**2. What NRIO must do.** It must complete the assessment (s26WH(2)), and Ruth signs it off on Tuesday 29 June 2027. Counting from 24 June that is day 6, so 30 − 6 = 24 days remain before the limit of Friday 23 July. NRIO must also keep taking reasonable steps to protect personal information, including organisational measures (APP 11.1 and 11.3). Its chosen fixes: bank details move only through the finance system, never as email attachments; a second officer checks any bulk personal information sent outside NRIO; and the email program warns before a message goes to an external address. These are NRIO's choice of reasonable steps, not steps the Act names. The sent email and all the correspondence are kept as records.

**3. What NRIO should do.** Tell the 31 contacts what happened, what NRIO did and what the recipient confirmed. Add that NRIO will never ask for banking passwords by phone, and that any call about payments can be checked by ringing the number in the grant agreement. The law does not require this once the breach is found not to be eligible, but it lets the groups watch for impostors. Using the contacts' details for this purpose is related to administering their grants and is something they would reasonably expect (APP 6.2(a)). The officer who sent the email gets support and training, not blame.

**4. What would change on 12 July.** The call is new information that cuts against the basis of Ruth's conclusion, unless the details leaked from somewhere else. Ruth starts a new assessment at once. Section 26WF helps only if, as a result of NRIO's action, a reasonable person would conclude that serious harm is not likely, and a scam that appears to use the spreadsheet's details undermines that conclusion. If the caller knew details found together only in the spreadsheet, there are likely to be reasonable grounds to believe that an eligible data breach has happened. NRIO would then have to give the Commissioner a statement as soon as practicable (s26WK(2)). It would also have to notify all 31 contacts if practicable, or at least those at risk, as soon as practicable after preparing the statement, by email or phone as it normally does (s26WL). The first assessment closed on 29 June, so its limit of 23 July no longer applies. While NRIO has grounds only to suspect, it must take all reasonable steps to finish the new assessment within 30 days after becoming aware on Monday 12 July, by Wednesday 11 August 2027 (s26WH(2)(b)); once it has grounds to believe, s26WK applies at once. Ruth aims to finish within days.
