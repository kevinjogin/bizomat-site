Most of this book is about scrutiny that arrives from outside. The audit committee and internal audit work inside the agency. They can find a weak control, a doubtful number or a stalled action months before the Auditor-General, a Senate committee or the Ombudsman does. That early warning is worth only what management does with it: how quickly it fixes what was found, and how honestly it reports progress.

This is an illustration. NRIO's audit committee meets every quarter. Oscar Hale, the Audit Liaison Officer in Clare Whitfield's unit, prepares its papers on external scrutiny and keeps the recommendations register (Chapter 6). This chapter follows one internal audit of grant payments in the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) from the plan to a finding and a response, and one committee paper that should have said more.

## Why every entity has an audit committee

Section 45 of the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) is short. Section 45(1) says: "The accountable authority of a Commonwealth entity must ensure that the entity has an audit committee." Section 45(2) says the committee must be constituted, and perform its functions, in line with any requirements in the rules. Those requirements are in section 17 of the PGPA Rule.

The Rule's guide to section 17 states the purpose: to help ensure that the committee "provides independent advice and assurance to the entity's accountable authority". So the committee advises; it does not manage, approve spending or direct staff. It serves the accountable authority, not Parliament or the Minister. And its advice is worth having only if it is independent of the people whose work it reviews.

The committee is still visible from outside. A non-corporate entity's annual report must give the charter's electronic address and each member's name, qualifications, knowledge, skills or experience, attendance and remuneration (PGPA Rule s 17AG(2A)).

## The charter and the four required functions

Section 17(1) of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) says the accountable authority "must, by written charter, determine the functions of the audit committee". Section 17(2) sets the floor. The functions must include reviewing the appropriateness of the accountable authority's financial reporting, performance reporting, system of risk oversight and management, and system of internal control for the entity.

The last two echo section 16 of the PGPA Act, which requires the accountable authority to establish and maintain both systems. The committee does not run them. It reviews whether they are appropriate and says what it finds.

Anything beyond the four is the accountable authority's choice, written into the charter. Neither the PGPA Act nor the PGPA Rule mentions internal audit. A charter can still give the committee a role in it, and NRIO's does, because internal audit is one of the main ways the committee learns whether controls work.

This is an illustration. NRIO's charter, approved by the CEO and reviewed each year, adds four functions to the four the Rule requires:

- review the internal audit plan before the CEO approves it, and every internal audit report;
- monitor the actions NRIO has agreed with internal audit and the ANAO, through Oscar's register;
- meet the ANAO about its audits of NRIO;
- meet the Chief Audit Executive, who heads internal audit, without management present, at every meeting.

The CEO, the Chief Finance Officer and the Chief Audit Executive attend by invitation, and Clare attends for the external scrutiny items. None of them is a member, for reasons the next section explains.

## Membership and independence

Section 17 of the Rule sets four membership rules. The committee must consist of "at least 3 persons" with appropriate qualifications, knowledge, skills or experience to assist it to perform its functions (s 17(3)). For a non-corporate entity such as NRIO, "all of the members of the audit committee must be persons who are not officials of the entity" (s 17(4)(a)). A majority must be persons who are not officials of any Commonwealth entity (s 17(4)(b)). And whatever else applies, the accountable authority and the entity's Chief Financial Officer and Chief Executive Officer, however described, must not be members (s 17(5)).

Read the two tests in section 17(4) together. No official of the entity may be a member. An official of another Commonwealth entity may be, as long as the members who are not Commonwealth officials are the majority. So the committee need not come entirely from outside government; a majority must. A person employed or engaged mainly to be a member is treated as not being an official of the entity for this test (s 17(4A)).

This is an illustration. NRIO's committee has three members. The chair, who is independent, is a former finance executive from the private sector. The second member has managed community infrastructure programs for local councils. Neither is a Commonwealth official. The third member is a senior officer of another Commonwealth entity, with experience in performance reporting. Two of the three members are not Commonwealth officials, so the majority test is met.

Meeting the Rule is the minimum. Members should declare interests at each meeting and leave an item when an interest arises; NRIO's charter requires both. The committee should get its papers in time to read them, see internal audit and the ANAO without management in the room, and ask for any information it needs. Management should not decide what the committee is allowed to see.

## Internal audit and its plan

Internal audit is the accountable authority's own check on whether the entity's controls, processes and information work as intended. The law requires the systems of risk oversight and internal control. It leaves the means of checking them to the accountable authority, and internal audit is one of those means.

This is an illustration. At NRIO, the Chief Audit Executive heads internal audit, reports to the CEO and works to the committee under its charter, using its own staff and contracted auditors. Oscar does not audit. He makes sure auditors get the records they ask for, that managers respond by the agreed dates, and that every agreed action reaches the register.

A good internal audit plan should:

- start from risk: the risk register, the corporate plan, recent incidents, expected external scrutiny and the committee's requests;
- cover the four areas the committee reviews over a cycle of years;
- avoid duplicating the ANAO, by testing something it is not, or the same controls at a different time;
- keep some capacity free for work nobody predicted;
- be reviewed by the committee and approved by the accountable authority.

This is an illustration. The committee reviewed NRIO's 2027–28 plan on Thursday 17 June 2027, and the CEO approved it on Monday 28 June 2027. One audit in it was new: RCFP Round 1 grant payments. The plan gave four reasons. The round was NRIO's largest new grants program, A$9.98 million across 31 grants, each paid in three instalments. The first instalments were being paid in the last weeks of the financial year, when the pressure to pay is highest. Senators had asked about the round at Estimates on 2 June. And nobody outside the program had yet tested its payment controls.

## NRIO's audit of grant payments

This is an illustration. Internal audit tested the first instalment of each of the 31 grants against four controls:

1. no payment before the grant agreement is executed, that is, signed by both parties;
2. bank details confirmed independently before payment, especially where they differ from those in the application;
3. the amount matches the payment schedule in the agreement;
4. the payment is released by the CFO's area, separately from the officer who requested it.

All 31 amounts matched their schedules, and all 31 payments were released separately. There were two findings. The second, on bank details, is the exercise at the end of this chapter.

Finding 1, rated High on internal audit's scale, said the grants system lets an officer raise a payment request before any executed agreement is recorded, so the rule against paying early rests on two people's checks. Those checks had failed once: one first instalment had been paid before its agreement was executed. The program had found it, reported it through NRIO's process and changed its payment form before the audit began, and the other 30 payments complied. Internal audit's point was about what came next. Every grant has two more instalments, so 31 × 2 = 62 further payments would rely on the same manual checks.

The recommendation was a system control: a payment request cannot be submitted unless the grants system holds an executed-agreement record with the signed copy attached. Management agreed. The CFO took ownership, with a due date of Monday 31 January 2028, before the first mid-point reports fell due in March, and the manual checks would stay until the block went live. The committee accepted the report and the agreed actions on Thursday 9 December 2027.

When the ANAO's fieldwork on Round 1 began in December (Chapter 5), Oscar made sure the audit team had internal audit's report. Nobody gains when the same weakness is discovered twice.

## Working with the committee

Most of what follows is "should": the Rule sets the committee's functions and membership, not how managers deal with it.

**Papers.** A committee paper should say on its first page what the committee is asked to note, consider or advise on. It should give bad news before good, show every changed date beside the original, and say what evidence lies behind each status.

**Attendance.** The owner of an action should attend when it is discussed and answer directly. If you do not know, say so and follow up in writing. A question from the committee is the kind of request section 13(9) of the Public Service Act describes: an APS employee "must not provide false or misleading information" in response.

**Disagreement.** Disagree with a finding or a rating in the written management response, with reasons and evidence, not in a corridor. Internal audit's rating stays in the report, management's view sits beside it, and the committee weighs both.

**Between meetings.** Some news cannot wait three months. This is an illustration. NRIO's charter asks the CEO to tell the chair between meetings of any significant control failure, suspected fraud or new external scrutiny. Clare drafts those notes.

## When it goes wrong: a paper that said "on track"

This is an illustration. In late January 2028 the system vendor told the CFO's area that it could not deliver the payment block before June. The CFO's area agreed a new date of Friday 30 June 2028 and, on Friday 11 February, changed the due date in the register. At the time the register held one date for each action, so the original date of 31 January disappeared. The manual checks continued.

Oscar's paper for the committee's meeting on Thursday 16 March 2028 summarised the register by status. Every open internal audit action was on track against its current date, so the cover page said: "All open internal audit actions are on track." Each line was true. Together they misled. The action the committee had treated as most urgent had slipped five months, past the start of the mid-point instalments it was meant to protect.

The Chief Audit Executive raised it in the private session, and the chair then asked the CEO why the paper said "on track". The CEO had not known either: every report drawn from the register showed only the current date.

The cost was not money. No payment went out early. But the committee had wanted the block working before the mid-point payments began, and by the time it learned of the delay the first mid-point reports were already arriving. The CEO asked for a paper out of session by Thursday 30 March. The CFO's area added a second check, by an officer outside the payments team, on every RCFP payment until the block went live. And the chair wrote to the CEO that the committee could not rely on status summaries until the register showed its own history.

Nobody had set out to hide anything. The register overwrote dates, owners reported their own status, and Oscar's summary repeated it. The paper had no place for what had changed since the last meeting, so a slipped date looked like any other.

NRIO adopted four controls:

- the register keeps the original due date and every revised date, with the reason, who agreed it and when;
- an action whose date has moved is reported as "slipped" until the committee has noted the new date;
- every paper opens with what has slipped, worsened or failed since the last meeting, and only then with what is done;
- when an action internal audit rated High slips, the CEO tells the chair before the next meeting.

## Exercise: the management response on bank details

This is an illustration. Internal audit's draft report of Wednesday 17 November 2027 contains this second finding, rated Medium. Management responses are due on Wednesday 1 December.

- **Condition.** Of the 31 first instalments, 6 were paid to bank accounts different from those given in the application. For 4 of the 6, the file holds a full record of an independent call-back. For 2, it does not: one note says only "confirmed by phone", with no name, number or date, and the other file has no record at all. The two payments were A$64,000 and A$112,000. Internal audit wrote to both grantees, and both confirmed in writing that the accounts are theirs.
- **Criteria.** NRIO's procedure: before payment, confirm changed bank details by calling the grantee on a number from the application or its public records, and record who called whom, when, and what was confirmed.
- **Cause.** The payment request form has one free-text box, "bank details confirmed". The CFO's area checks that the box is filled in, not what it says.
- **Effect.** NRIO cannot show that the control operated for 2 of the 6 changes. A false change of details would send a grant to someone else, perhaps unnoticed until the grantee asked where its money was.
- **Recommendations.** (a) Replace the free-text box with required fields. (b) The CFO's area returns any request with changed details whose record is incomplete. (c) Re-confirm the six changed accounts before their mid-point instalments.

Write the management response. Say whether management agrees and why. Give each action one owner, a date and the evidence that will close it. State the numbers the response should give, and what the committee should hear on Thursday 9 December 2027.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

**Agree, with one addition.** The finding is accurate and the recommendations fit the cause. Arguing that no money was lost would miss the point: the control exists because a diverted payment is found late, if at all. Management adds one step: compare bank details with those last confirmed, not only with the application, so that a change made after the first payment is caught too.

**The numbers, stated honestly.** Changed details: 6 of 31 payments, and 6 ÷ 31 = 19.4 per cent. Changes without an adequate record: 2 of 6, and 2 ÷ 6 = 33.3 per cent. As a share of all first instalments that is 2 ÷ 31 = 6.5 per cent, but that figure answers the wrong question. The control matters only when details change, and when they changed it could not be shown to have worked one time in three. The response gives both figures and says which one matters. The amount: A$64,000 + A$112,000 = A$176,000, confirmed by the grantees, with no loss. The exposure ahead: the six changed accounts will receive 6 × 2 = 12 more payments, and the program as a whole 31 × 2 = 62.

Action 1 changes a form the CFO's area controls, so it does not wait for the system vendor. Action 2 covers the gap until the new form is in use. Action 3 is finished before the mid-point reports start to fall due in March 2028. Each action has one accountable owner and closes on something internal audit can inspect, not on a statement that the work is done.

**For the committee on 9 December.** One paragraph: the finding and its Medium rating; the 2 of 6 changed accounts without a record; both grantees' confirmation and no loss; the four actions, with owners and dates; and the new form in use by 17 December, before any mid-point payment. Do not lead with "no money was lost". It is true, but it answers a different question from the one the finding asks.
