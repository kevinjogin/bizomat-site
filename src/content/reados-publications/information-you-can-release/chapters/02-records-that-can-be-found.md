A freedom of information (FOI) request is a request for documents, and an agency can decide only on the documents it finds. Whether it finds them depends on choices made months earlier by people who were not thinking about FOI: where they saved an email, what they called a file, whether anyone wrote down a phone call.

This is an illustration. The journalist's request of Monday 7 June 2027 asks for Round 1 briefs, decision records and correspondence with the Minister's office for the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP). Priya Shah, the case officer, must find every document within it, with help from Sam Reid's RCFP team and NRIO's Information Management Unit. In the second week, an RCFP officer decides to tidy up a mailbox.

This chapter explains what a Commonwealth record is, when one may be destroyed, how to keep records a stranger can find, and why an FOI search is only as good as the records behind it.

## What counts as a Commonwealth record

The [Archives Act 1983](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02796/latest/text) defines a record broadly. It is a document or an object, "in any form (including any electronic form)", kept because of the information it contains or its connection with an event, person, circumstance or thing (s3(1)). A Commonwealth record is, in the main, "a record that is the property of the Commonwealth or of a Commonwealth institution" (s3(1)).

Two consequences follow. Format does not matter: a signed brief, an email, a spreadsheet, a chat message, a text on a work phone and a scanned decision sheet are all records if they are kept for what they say or what they relate to. Status does not matter either: a draft is a record as much as a final version, and a copy can be one too.

The [Freedom of Information Act 1982](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text) (FOI Act) casts a wider net still. A "document" includes any record of information and any copy of one, and a document of an agency is one in its possession, whether created there or received (s4(1)). An FOI search therefore reaches everything NRIO holds, wherever it sits: the records system, shared drives, inboxes, chat channels and work phones. The records system is where documents should be. The search has to go wherever they are.

Agency business on a personal phone or account is hard to find and manage. Avoid it; if it happens, save the substance to the records system that day.

## What the Archives Act forbids and allows

Section 24(1) says a person must not engage in conduct that results in the destruction or other disposal of a Commonwealth record, the transfer of its custody or ownership, or damage to or alteration of it. The penalty is 20 penalty units. Strict liability applies to the element that the record is a Commonwealth record (s24(1A)), so not realising that an email was one is no answer to that part of the offence. Under s24(5), treating an electronic record so that information can no longer be obtained from it counts as destroying it. Deleting an email or overwriting a file is destruction.

The exceptions in s24(2) are short. Section 24(1) does not apply to anything done:

- as required by any law;
- "with the permission of the Archives", or under a practice or procedure the Archives has approved;
- under "a normal administrative practice", unless the Archives has told the agency it disapproves of that practice; or
- to place records that are outside Commonwealth custody in the custody of an institution entitled to them.

Section 26 adds a rule for older records. Once a Commonwealth record has existed for more than 15 years, adding to or altering it is an offence unless a law requires it or the Archives permits it.

None of the exceptions turns on how a record reads or who would like it gone. A record that is embarrassing or out of date is still a record. Whether a particular draft or duplicate falls within an approved or normal practice is a question for the records area, not for the person holding it.

## Disposal under an authority from the National Archives

Planned disposal relies on the permission of the Archives, usually given in an authority from the National Archives. An authority describes classes of records, how long each must be kept, and what happens at the end: destruction, or transfer to the Archives. The agency's records area applies it, and should keep a record of what was destroyed, when and under which authority.

Personal information follows the same rules. Australian Privacy Principle (APP) 11.2 in the Privacy Act 1988 requires an agency to destroy or de-identify personal information it no longer needs, but not where the information is contained in a Commonwealth record (APP 11.2(c)). For agency records, the Archives Act decides how long personal information is kept.

This is an illustration. At NRIO, the Information Management Unit owns the records system and all disposal. Officers dispose of nothing beyond the routine practices the unit has published, and they ask when unsure. One NRIO rule overrides every retention period: nothing within an open FOI request, review or complaint is destroyed or altered until the matter, including any review, is finished.

The reasons come from the FOI Act. The right of access in s11 attaches to documents the agency holds, so a document that could be destroyed once asked for would make the right empty. And an agency may refuse access to a document it cannot find only after "all reasonable steps have been taken to find the document" and it is satisfied the document cannot be found or does not exist (s24A(1)). If a document within a request were destroyed after it arrived, the findings in the decision (s26(1)(a)) would have to explain what happened to it.

## Emails, chats, texts and calls

Most of an agency's business now happens in messages. NRIO's test is the same for each: does it make, record or explain a decision, give or receive advice, or record a commitment or an instruction? If so, it belongs on the file for that business in the records system, not only in an inbox or a chat channel.

- **Emails.** File the email that decides or advises, with its attachments, to the relevant file the same week.
- **Chats.** Save a thread that settles something to the file, or file a short note of what it settled.
- **Texts on a work phone.** Save the substance to the file the same day.
- **Calls and meetings.** Write a file note the same day: who, when, what was asked, what was said and what was promised.

A note written late should say when it was written and when the event happened. Never date it to look as if it was made at the time. To correct a record, add a dated annotation; never change what was there.

This is an illustration. On Wednesday 19 May 2027, the Minister's adviser rang Sam about Round 1. No note was made at the time and no email followed. When Priya began searching in June, there was nothing to find: the call existed only in two people's memories. Sam wrote a file note in July, dated the day he wrote it and saying when the call took place, and it joined the documents Priya identified. No search can find a record that was never made.

## Names and files a stranger can follow

A record can exist and still be lost. The usual causes are a vague name, a private location and too many versions with no way to tell which one counted.

This is an illustration. NRIO's naming rule puts the date first, then the program and round, the document type, and the version or status: "2027-05-07 RCFP R1 Brief to Minister FINAL". Dates in that form sort in order, and the title says what the document is without opening it. The rule applies to titles in the records system; it never changes the document itself.

Each piece of business has one file. For Round 1, NRIO keeps a master file: the guidelines and their approval, the assessment plan, the probity advice, the panel minutes, the brief to the Minister and the Minister's decision record. Each grant file cross-refers to the master file instead of copying it, so each shared document has one authoritative version.

Versions need care. Keep the final, and keep the drafts that show how advice changed, especially for a brief to a minister. Mark which version was used. Three copies called "final", "final v2" and "FINAL" tell a reader nothing.

## Why FOI searches depend on records

The FOI Act assumes documents can be found. A request must give enough information for a responsible officer to identify the documents (s15(2)(b)), and the agency then has to look. If it relies on s24A to refuse access to a document it cannot find, it must be able to show the reasonable steps it took. That is why every search needs a search record: where the officer looked, who was asked, the search terms and date range, when, and what was found.

Good records make a search short and defensible. A dozen inboxes, two shared drives and a chat channel take far longer than one well-kept file, and still leave the question of what was missed. Chapter 3 shows how search time feeds into planning and charges.

This is an illustration. Priya's search plan for the RCFP request has three layers. She starts with the master file. She asks the Information Management Unit to search the records system for Round 1 documents dated 1 March to 31 May 2027. And she sends Sam's team a written search request listing the three kinds of document and the date range, covering their mailboxes, shared drive and team chat channel. About 420 pages are eventually identified. They include the brief of 7 May with its ranked list, the Minister's annotated decisions, emails with the Minister's office, the 186-row assessment spreadsheet, NRIO's legal advice on the program's authority and, once it existed, the record of the 19 May call.

## When it goes wrong: drafts deleted to tidy up

This is an illustration. On Tuesday 8 June 2027, the day after the request arrived, Priya emailed Sam asking his team to gather the Round 1 documents. Her email described the request and the date range. It did not say "do not delete anything", and Sam forwarded it to his team without adding that.

The searches ran that week. On Thursday 17 June, an RCFP officer whose mailbox was near its storage limit, and who assumed the searching was over, emailed the team: "I'm going to delete my old draft emails on Round 1 to tidy up. Shout if anyone objects." The officer did not wait for replies. That afternoon a folder went: emails carrying earlier drafts of the 7 May brief, and unsent draft replies to the Minister's office.

Sam read the email the next morning, Friday 18 June, stopped any further deletion and rang Priya. Priya told Ruth, sent a written hold notice to every officer who might hold documents within the request, and asked the Information Management Unit to restore the folder. The unit restored it from the email system's backup on Monday 21 June 2027.

The damage was contained, but by luck. The unit spent most of a day on the restore. Priya recorded the deletion and the restore in the search record, because Liam's decision would rest on it. Ruth reported it to the General Counsel and the unit as a records incident, and the unit considered whether s24 had been breached. The officer, who meant no harm, had to explain in writing. Had nobody read the email for a few more weeks, the backup could have rolled over. NRIO would then have had to explain, in its decision, why documents within the request were destroyed after it arrived.

Two controls would have prevented it. The first is a written hold notice on the day a request is registered, sent to every likely document holder. It names the request and the date range and says plainly: do not delete, move or change anything that might fall within it, including drafts, chats and texts, until the case and any review are closed. The second is a records rule that drafts of a brief to the Minister go on the file anyway, so they never sit only in one person's mailbox. NRIO now sends the hold notice the day every request is registered, and Priya's case checklist will not open a case without it.

| A | Brief to the Minister, Fri 7 May 2027, with the ranked list | Master file: the final signed copy | Titled by the naming rule; two earlier drafts are in the restored folder |
| B | Decision sheet signed and annotated by the Minister, Mon 31 May 2027 | Master file: a scan | Titled "scan0047.pdf" |
| C | Emails with the Minister's office, 1 Mar to 31 May 2027 | The inboxes of Sam and three of his officers | None is on the master file |
| D | File note of the call of Wed 19 May 2027 | Master file | Written by Sam in July 2027, dated that day, saying when the call took place |
| E | Assessment spreadsheet, 186 rows | Attached to an email to the Minister's office; three more copies on the RCFP shared drive: "Assessment final", "Assessment final v2" and "Assessment FINAL moderated" | None is on the master file |
| F | NRIO's legal advice on the program's authority | Master file, a copy; the original is on the legal area's advice file | Nothing unusual |
| G | Not in the set: a chat thread from 31 May in which officers agreed how applicants would be told of the decisions | The RCFP team's chat channel | Not saved anywhere else |

For each item, say whether it is a Commonwealth record, whether it is where it should be, and what Priya should do now. Nothing may be destroyed or altered.

### Worked answer

All seven are Commonwealth records. Each is kept for the information it contains or its connection with Round 1, and each is NRIO's property, whatever its form (Archives Act s3(1)). Each is also a document of NRIO for FOI purposes, wherever it sits (FOI Act s4(1)). The questions are location and next steps.

**A: in place.** The final brief is on the master file under a proper title. The two drafts are records too, and NRIO's rule puts drafts of a brief to the Minister on the file. Priya asks the unit to capture copies to the master file, labelled as drafts, and leaves the restored folder untouched. She lists the drafts in the search record so Liam can decide whether they fall within "all briefs to the Minister".

**B: in place, poorly titled.** The scan is on the right file but a title search will not find it. The unit adds a description to the record's metadata, "2027-05-31 RCFP R1 Minister's decision sheet, signed", and the search record notes it. The scan itself is not touched.

**C: in the wrong place.** These emails are exactly what the request seeks, and none is on the file. Priya asks the unit to capture copies from the four inboxes to the master file and leaves the originals until the request and any review are finished. The search record lists each mailbox, the search terms and the date range. The cause is a habit: NRIO's rule is that an email that decides or advises goes to the file the same week.

**D: a late record, honestly made.** The note is properly dated, and the gap between the call and the note is a fact the record shows, not one to hide. Nobody redates or rewrites it to look as if it was made in May. If anything needs correcting, Sam adds a dated annotation. The lesson is the one the call taught: a file note the same day.

**E: in the wrong place, and unclear.** The copy attached to the email is the one the request reaches, and it is captured with the email (item C). The shared drive copies leave a reader unable to say which version the brief's ranked list came from. Priya asks the officer who prepared the brief, and the unit files that version to the master file with a note saying so. The rest are kept. Deleting "duplicates" now would break NRIO's rule, and they may not be duplicates at all.

**F: in place.** The master file holds a copy and the legal area's advice file the original. Both are documents of NRIO, so the search record lists both. Whether any exemption applies is for Chapter 4.

**G: not filed.** The thread is outside the request: it is not a brief, a record of the Minister's decisions or correspondence with the Minister's office. That fits the search record, which found only copies of known documents in the channel. It is still a Commonwealth record, because a record can be in any form, and it settled something. Priya tells Sam, and the unit exports it to the program's file with its date and participants. Outside the request does not mean free to delete.

Four items were on the right file (A, B, D and F), though B needed a description and D was made weeks late. Three (C, E and G) had to be captured. And A's drafts survived only because the backup still held them.
