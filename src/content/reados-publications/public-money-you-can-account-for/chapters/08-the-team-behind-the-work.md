Every control in the earlier chapters is carried out by a person. An invoice is matched by someone with time to match it. A contract amendment is reported within 42 days because someone knows it has to be. When a team is short, untrained or unsure what is expected, the controls slip first, because they are the work nobody sees until it is missing.

Lena leads four people: Arun, Grace, Tom and Jas. The questions she asks about the team turn out to be the questions she asks about money. What is the work? What does it cost in time? Who decides? What does the record show? The branch's accounts-payable work raises all of them at once, because it is about to lose a person.

## Plan the work in hours

Workforce planning starts with the work, not with positions. Count the units of work in a year, measure how long one unit takes and multiply. Work out how many productive hours one person gives in a year, then divide. The result is a full-time equivalent (FTE) figure: the number of full-time people the work needs.

This is an illustration. The branch processes 9,600 supplier invoices a year. Jas and a labour-hire temp timed a sample of 200 invoices over two weeks, from receipt until each was ready for payment approval: matching each to its order and receipt, coding it and resolving simple queries. The average was 12 minutes. NRIO plans on 1,500 available hours per person a year, after leave and public holidays, and assumes 85% of those hours are productive. The rest goes to meetings, training, system outages and team duties. Both are NRIO's planning assumptions, not Commonwealth standards.

```figure
type: formula
title: From invoices to FTE
caption: Illustration. NRIO's planning figures. The FTE is rounded to two decimal places only at the end.
formula: Workload hours = units × minutes per unit ÷ 60
formula: Productive hours per person = available hours × productive share
formula: FTE = workload hours ÷ productive hours per person
- Units | 9,600 invoices a year
- Minutes per unit | 12, from a timed sample of 200 invoices
- Available hours | 1,500 per person a year, after leave
- Productive share | 85%
step: 9,600 × 12 = 115,200 minutes; 115,200 ÷ 60 = 1,920 hours
step: 1,500 × 85% = 1,275 productive hours per person
step: 1,920 ÷ 1,275 = 1.5059, so 1.51 FTE and 2 people
```

The [Staffing Need](/tools/staffing-need/) tool runs the same calculation. It converts units of work, labour minutes per unit, available hours per person and productive efficiency into an FTE requirement and a whole-person figure, and it reminds you to allow for skill mix, coverage, supervision and leave. One caution applies to any calculation of this kind. If leave is already out of the available hours, as it is in NRIO's 1,500, do not take it out a second time through the efficiency figure.

Two inputs deserve the most care. Minutes per unit should be measured, not guessed: real work, timed by the people who do it, over a normal fortnight. Units should be a forecast, not last year's count. Two new sites joining the maintenance contract (Chapter 5), a change in how suppliers bill or a new finance system will all move the number.

## Turn the number into a decision

The calculation says 1.51 FTE. Nobody employs 0.51 of a person, and the right answer is rarely just "round up". Lena worked through four questions before she wrote anything for the Branch Manager.

**Coverage.** Today the work is done by Jas, who spends most of the week on it, and a labour-hire temp whose contract ends on 31 December. When the temp leaves, the invoices fall on one person: 1,275 productive hours against 1,920 hours of work, a shortfall of 645 hours. Late payments would follow, and the CPRs require payments to suppliers within the maximum payment terms once delivery is acknowledged and a correctly rendered invoice is received.

**Separation of duties.** The person who enters an invoice should not also approve it for payment (Chapter 3). One person cannot run the whole chain, whatever the hours say.

**Skill mix.** Matching and coding suit an APS 4 role. Disputed invoices, reconciliations and month-end checks need Arun's experience. The plan has to say which hours fall to whom.

**Peaks and slack.** Two people give 2,550 productive hours against 1,920 hours of work. The 630 spare hours are not idle time. They absorb month-end and year-end peaks, cover one person's absence, handle supplier queries and run the duplicate payment check from Chapter 7.

Lena recommended replacing the temporary arrangement with an ongoing APS 4 role, filled through a merit process, and working with finance on electronic invoicing to cut the minutes per invoice over time. Arun costed the role from NRIO's salary tables and checked it against the employees line of the branch budget, A$3,900,000 for 2026–27. That budget is internal, so it excludes GST. The temp is paid from the suppliers line, so the change also moves cost between lines, and the suppliers forecast from Chapter 2 needs to show it.

## Merit: a fair chance and the best person

The APS Employment Principles in [section 10A of the Public Service Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) include merit-based engagement and promotion. Section 10A(2) says a decision is based on merit if all eligible members of the community had a reasonable opportunity to apply; candidates' relative suitability is assessed through a competitive selection process; the assessment is based on the relationship between their work-related qualities and those "genuinely required" for the duties; it focuses on their relative capacity to achieve outcomes related to the duties; and merit is the primary consideration in the decision.

Each element becomes a step. HR advises which process applies to the kind of engagement. The business manager supplies what only the business area knows: what the duties really are and what good work in them looks like.

```figure
type: steps
title: A merit selection, step by step
caption: Each step answers part of section 10A(2) of the Public Service Act. HR advises on the process; the business area defines the work.
- Write down the duties | What the role does each week, taken from the workload plan, not an old position description.
- Choose criteria genuinely required | Accurate matching and coding, dealing with suppliers, using a finance system. Nothing merely nice to have.
- Advertise so eligible people can apply | A reasonable opportunity for all eligible members of the community, through the channels HR uses.
- Assess competitively | The same questions, work sample and referee checks for every candidate, by a panel that has declared its interests.
- Compare capacity to achieve the outcomes | Rank candidates on evidence against the criteria, not on familiarity.
- *Record the reasons | The selection report shows why the chosen person is the most suitable. Merit is the primary consideration.
```

## When it goes wrong: the friend's recommendation

In November the Facilities Director stopped by Lena's desk. "My neighbour's son is great with numbers and he's between jobs. Ask the labour-hire firm to send him in when your temp finishes. It saves you months."

Here is how that goes wrong. Lena, worried about the 645-hour gap, asks the firm for him by name. He starts in January. In March an applicant for the ongoing role, who has heard how the placement was made, asks the selection panel whether the outcome is already decided.

The arrangement fails in several ways at once. Nobody else has had a chance at the placement, and nobody has compared him with anyone. The Facilities Director has a personal connection and has not declared it, although the Code of Conduct requires reasonable steps to avoid any conflict of interest, real or apparent (Chapter 7). The ongoing selection now carries a perception problem it did nothing to earn. And it is unfair to the young man, whose place in the team will be questioned however good his work is.

What should happen is simple. Lena thanks the Director and explains that the temporary gap will be filled through the labour-hire arrangement on its normal terms, without naming anyone, and that the ongoing role will be advertised. The neighbour's son is welcome to apply. The Director declares the connection and takes no part in the assessment. If he is the best candidate, the record will show it, and nobody can say otherwise.

## Set expectations before you judge performance

The Employment Principles in section 10A also include one that "requires effective performance from each employee". That works in both directions. Nobody can perform effectively against expectations that were never set, so the manager's first job is to make them clear, specific and written down, through the agency's performance framework.

This is an illustration. In July Lena agreed expectations with each person, linked to the branch business plan. For Jas they included: invoices entered within five business days of receipt; fewer than 3 in every 100 coded to the wrong cost centre, measured by Arun's monthly sample of 300; and supplier queries answered or passed to Arun within two business days. Each says what, how much and how it will be checked. "Process invoices accurately" would have said none of those things.

Expectations also need a regular place to be discussed. Lena holds a fortnightly one-to-one with each person: what went well, what is stuck, what help is needed. Most performance problems surface there while they are still small.

## Raise underperformance early, privately and on the record

This is an illustration. Arun's October sample found 31 of 300 invoices coded to the wrong cost centre, about 10.3% against an expectation of under 3%. Two suppliers had also complained about late payment.

Lena did not wait for the annual review. Before speaking to Jas, she checked the causes she controlled. The workload was real: the temp had been away for three weeks in September, and Jas had covered alone. A new set of cost centres had started on 1 July with no training for the team. Neither excused the errors, but both changed what a fair conversation looked like. She also asked HR which part of NRIO's performance process applied and what to record.

She met Jas privately that week, described the facts with three examples from the sample, asked what was getting in the way and listened. They agreed that the standard stayed the same and so did the support: two coding sessions with Arun, a one-page coding guide and a protected two-hour block each week. They set a review in four weeks. Lena wrote a short note of what was agreed and gave Jas a copy.

The Code of Conduct requires every APS employee to treat everyone with respect and courtesy, and without harassment (13(3)). That includes a manager raising a problem. Early, private and factual is respectful. A comment in a team meeting, a pointed email, or six months of silence followed by a poor rating is not. If support is given and the problem continues, the next step is the agency's formal process, with HR advice, not a process the manager invents.

## Develop the people you have

The Stewardship value in section 10 of the Public Service Act describes an APS that "builds its capability and institutional knowledge". In a small team that is concrete. If Arun is the only person who can run the month-end reconciliation, his leave is a risk to the branch's reporting. If Tom is the only one who knows how to report a contract amendment on AusTender, a two-week absence can push a report past 42 days.

Lena's development plan pairs each person's growth with a coverage gap. Jas learns the reconciliation as Arun's backup once the coding standard holds. Arun spends time with the CFO's forecasting team. Grace and Tom learn each other's core reporting tasks. Each person writes a short procedure for one task only they do now, kept in the records system so the knowledge stays when people move on.

## Safety is part of the plan

Section 27 of the Work Health and Safety Act 2011 says an officer of a person conducting a business or undertaking "must exercise due diligence" to ensure the business or undertaking complies with its duties. Due diligence includes taking reasonable steps to acquire and keep up to date knowledge of work health and safety matters. Whether a business manager is an "officer" depends on the role, so ask your WHS area rather than assume either way. Every worker has their own duties under the Act regardless.

For Lena's team the risks are ordinary ones. Tom drives to remote sites for contract inspections, including in the wet season that Chapter 6 identified as a staffing risk for the contractor. Each trip has a travel plan and a check-in time agreed with NRIO's WHS area. Workload is the other risk. A month of overtime to clear an invoice backlog is a safety matter as well as a performance one, so ask your WHS area how your agency manages it. The workforce plan is partly a safety plan: a team with 630 spare hours can absorb a bad month without someone working every evening.

## Exercise: staff next year and plan the review

Part 1. This is an illustration. Finance plans electronic invoicing from 1 July 2027. Lena expects 10,400 invoices in 2027–28, with two new sites and more small call-outs, and a timed sample on the new system shows 9 minutes per invoice. NRIO's planning figures stay at 1,500 available hours per person and 85% productive. Work out the FTE requirement, the whole-person figure, and the gap or spare hours with one person and with two. Then say how you would staff it.

Part 2. At the four-week review, Arun's sample shows 6 of 300 invoices coded to the wrong cost centre. Plan the conversation with Jas: what to say, what to agree and what to record.

### Worked answer

Part 1, in the same order as the formula:

- Workload: 10,400 × 9 = 93,600 minutes; 93,600 ÷ 60 = 1,560 hours.
- Productive hours per person: 1,500 × 85% = 1,275 hours.
- FTE: 1,560 ÷ 1,275 = 1.2235, which rounds to 1.22 FTE (two decimal places). Whole people: 2.
- One person: 1,560 − 1,275 = 285 hours short.
- Two people: 2 × 1,275 = 2,550 hours; 2,550 − 1,560 = 990 hours spare.

The work still needs two people, for separation of duties and cover, but not two people full time on invoices. The full-time APS 4 gives 1,275 hours. The other 285 hours are about 22% of Jas's productive time (285 ÷ 1,275 = 0.2235, rounded to the nearest whole per cent). The rest of Jas's time can go to administration and to backing up Arun's reconciliation, which closes a coverage gap. Lena should review the plan once the system is running. If handling stays at 12 minutes, 10,400 invoices would take 10,400 × 12 ÷ 60 = 2,080 hours, or 2,080 ÷ 1,275 = 1.63 FTE.

Part 2: 6 ÷ 300 = 2.0%, under the expectation of fewer than 3 in 100 (fewer than 9 in 300). A plan for the conversation:

- Open with the result: the standard has been met, down from 10.3% to 2.0%.
- Ask what helped and what is still hard, and keep the support that worked, such as the protected block, for another month.
- Confirm that the standard is now the normal expectation and that the monthly sample continues.
- Agree the next development step: starting on the reconciliation backup.
- Record a short note that the improvement goal was met, with the date and the next check, shared with Jas and kept as NRIO's process requires.

A team that is sized, trained and safe can run the controls. Chapter 9 turns to the plans, measures and reports that show whether the work is achieving its purpose.
