Human beings are social creatures, and almost everything in business depends on relationships: winning customers, working with colleagues, negotiating with suppliers, persuading investors and leading teams. **Social skills**, meaning the verbal and non-verbal abilities we use to interact with others, shape how people respond to us, whether they trust us and whether they want to work with us again.

Some people seem naturally socially skilled, but these abilities can be learned and improved like any other skill. This article explains what social skills involve, why they matter in business, how to read non-verbal signals, a practical framework for building connections, ways to start conversations, and how to influence people by connecting with them first.

## What social skills involve

Social skills combine:

- **Verbal communication**: the words we choose, our tone and our volume.
- **Non-verbal communication**: body language, gestures, facial expression, eye contact, posture and appearance.
- **Listening and understanding**: noticing what others say and do not say.
- **Emotional awareness**: recognising and responding to others' feelings.

Non-verbal communication has always been powerful. Silent film comedians made audiences laugh without words. Traditional communities have used signals such as smoke or symbolic gestures to communicate without speech. In everyday business, people constantly send and read signals without speaking.

## Reading non-verbal signals

Learning to notice non-verbal signals helps you respond appropriately. For example, if the person you are talking to:

- Checks their watch or phone repeatedly,
- Taps their foot or fidgets,
- Turns their body or feet towards the exit,
- Gives short, distracted answers,

they are probably no longer engaged. It may be time to wrap up, ask a question to re-engage them or arrange to continue later.

Positive signals include leaning in, sustained eye contact, nodding, open posture and questions that build on what you have said.

Interpret signals cautiously and in context. A crossed arm may mean the room is cold, not that someone is defensive. Cultural differences also affect eye contact, personal space and gestures.

## Why social skills matter in business

1. **More and better relationships**, personal and professional. People enjoy spending time with those who engage warmly and attentively.
2. **Better communication.** Choosing the right words and reading the other person makes your message land.
3. **Greater influence.** When people feel understood, they are more willing to agree and to trust you.
4. **Career and business growth.** Employers value people who work well with others. Entrepreneurs with strong social skills build networks that bring customers, partners and opportunities.
5. **Adaptability and wellbeing.** People who are comfortable in social situations adjust more easily to new environments and tend to experience more satisfying relationships.

## A framework for connection: listen, communicate, stay positive, care and follow up

### Listen

Good speakers are first good listeners. When you listen carefully, people listen to you in return.

- Show genuine interest in people's stories.
- Remember what they tell you, so you can refer to it later.
- **Remember names.** Forgetting someone's name, especially after you have met before, undermines the impression you make. Repeat names when introduced, and write them down after meetings if needed.

### Communicate well

- **Do not feel obliged to fill every silence.** Brief pauses are natural.
- **Know how to end a conversation gracefully** when it has run its course or you need to move on: "It's been great talking with you. I'll let you get back to the event."
- **Maintain natural eye contact.** Looking constantly away suggests disinterest.
- **Read body language**, yours and theirs.
- **Keep a relaxed, upright posture**, avoiding excessive leaning or fidgeting.

### Stay positive

People avoid those who constantly complain, criticise or gossip. A cooperative, constructive attitude makes others want to spend time with you. Negativity may get polite attention to your face and criticism behind your back.

### Show care and empathy

- Greet people by name.
- Show genuine interest in their wellbeing.
- Be sympathetic when people share problems.
- Never mock others.
- Do not act superior when someone comes to you for help.
- Stay humble and kind.

### Follow up

It is easy to make a connection and easy to let it lapse. Following up turns brief encounters into relationships. If someone mentions an interest in a topic, send them a relevant article a few days later. Remember important dates or events they mentioned and ask how they went. Small follow-ups show that you listened and care.

## Starting conversations: the FORD topics

Many people want to connect but struggle to start conversations. A simple memory aid is **FORD**:

- **Family**: their family, where appropriate and welcomed, and sharing a little about yours.
- **Occupation**: their work, role, industry and current projects. This is often the easiest starting point in business settings, because most people are happy to talk about their work.
- **Recreation**: hobbies, sport, travel and interests, which often reveal common ground.
- **Dreams**: goals, plans and aspirations, which lead to deeper conversations as rapport builds.

Share as well as ask. A conversation that is all questions feels like an interrogation. Be mindful of privacy: some people prefer not to discuss family, so follow their cues.

## Two useful mental habits

### Deserve what you want

If you want respect, trust and good relationships, behave in ways that earn them. If you want a strong family relationship, invest time and care. If you want respect at work, do your work wholeheartedly, reliably and on time. Social standing follows behaviour.

### Invert the problem

Investor Charlie Munger popularised the idea of inversion: instead of asking only how to achieve something, ask what would guarantee the opposite, and avoid it. Applied to social skills:

- **Goal**: people like and trust me.
- **Inverted**: what would make people dislike and distrust me? Talking constantly about myself, gossiping, mocking others, breaking promises, never listening.
- **Inverted again**: so I should listen, be discreet, show respect, keep promises, show empathy and keep a good sense of humour.

## Influencing by connecting first

Many people try to persuade others immediately: pitching products, pushing ideas or arguing their case. It often fails. Salespeople who skip the connection stage find that most cold approaches are ignored or rejected. Effective influence begins with connection.

### Build an emotional connection

Products and ideas that connect emotionally are easier to accept. A music player designed to bring back nostalgic songs from earlier decades, for example, sells on memories and feelings, not specifications.

One way to remember how to connect is **SHE**:

- **Senses**: use your senses to connect. Smile, make eye contact and listen more than you talk. Smiling at people as you enter a room often prompts smiles in return and immediately warms the atmosphere.
- **Honesty**: show genuine interest in what people want, focus on their problem and find a connector, some shared experience or agreement. If a customer complains about short battery life on their current phone, acknowledging that frustration creates connection.
- **Empathy**: do not criticise, belittle or dismiss people's problems. Offer encouragement and look for solutions.

### Convey your message thoughtfully

Royal advisers in history were often notable for how carefully they chose their words. A memorable mnemonic for conveying messages is **CCA**:

- **Contemplate**: think before you speak. A careless or sarcastic remark can cause lasting damage, sometimes far beyond what the speaker imagined.
- **Customise**: adapt your approach to the person. Different people respond to different styles, priorities and evidence. Learn about the person first.
- **Avoid arguments**: winning an argument often loses the relationship. Dale Carnegie, in *How to Win Friends and Influence People* (1936), described how he resolved a dispute with an official not by arguing but by showing respect for the official's expertise. The official, feeling valued, became cooperative and resolved the matter in Carnegie's favour.

### Do not try to convince, connect

A good doctor does not simply prescribe. They ask about symptoms, listen, examine and explain. By the time they recommend treatment, the patient is already convinced, because they feel understood and trust the doctor's process.

The same applies in business. Understand the other person's situation, needs and concerns first. When people feel understood, they are far more open to your recommendation. No tricks are needed, only honest intentions and genuine understanding.

## Social skills for networking

Business events can be daunting. A few habits help:

- **Set a modest goal**, such as three meaningful conversations, rather than trying to meet everyone.
- **Approach people standing alone** or in open groups, where joining is easier.
- **Prepare a short, natural introduction** of who you are and what you do, focused on the problems you help solve.
- **Ask open questions** and listen.
- **Exchange contact details** where there is genuine interest, and follow up within a day or two.

## Emotional intelligence

Social skills are part of a broader set of capabilities often called **emotional intelligence**, a concept popularised by Daniel Goleman's 1995 book of that name. It is commonly described as including:

- **Self-awareness**: recognising your own emotions and their effect on others.
- **Self-regulation**: managing your emotions and impulses, especially under pressure.
- **Motivation**: drive that comes from within rather than external rewards alone.
- **Empathy**: understanding others' feelings and perspectives.
- **Social skill**: managing relationships, influencing and working in teams.

Emotional intelligence can be developed through reflection, feedback and practice. Keeping a brief journal of difficult interactions, noting what you felt, how you reacted and what you might do differently, is a simple way to build self-awareness and self-regulation over time. Leaders and professionals who understand and manage emotions, both their own and other people's, tend to build stronger relationships and handle pressure better.

## Social skills for introverts

Social skills are not the same as extroversion. Many introverts are excellent listeners, thoughtful conversationalists and trusted advisers. Introverts can play to their strengths:

- **Prefer depth to breadth**: a few meaningful conversations at an event can be more valuable than many brief ones.
- **Prepare**: think of a few questions and topics in advance.
- **Use one-to-one settings** such as coffee meetings, which suit many introverts better than large groups.
- **Follow up in writing**, which many introverts find comfortable and effective.
- **Plan recovery time** after demanding social events.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I remember names?** Repeat the name when introduced ("Nice to meet you, Sam"), use it once or twice in the conversation and write it down afterwards with a note about the person.

**What if a conversation stalls?** Ask an open question about something the person mentioned earlier, or move to another of the FORD topics. It is also fine to end politely and move on.

**Is it manipulative to use these techniques?** Not if your intentions are genuine. These skills help you understand and connect with people. They become manipulative only when used to deceive or exploit.

**How can I practise social skills?** Set small, regular challenges: start one conversation with someone new each week, ask a follow-up question in every meeting, or send one thoughtful follow-up message after each event. Reflect on what worked, and gradually expand your comfort zone. Joining a club, volunteer group or professional association also provides regular, low-pressure practice with people who share your interests.

## A worked example

A newly appointed business development manager at an engineering services firm struggles at industry events. He talks mainly about the firm's capabilities, hands out brochures and rarely gets follow-up meetings.

He changes his approach. He starts conversations by asking people about their current projects and challenges, listens carefully, and only mentions his firm's services when a genuine need appears. He notes names and key details after each conversation, and within two days sends each new contact something useful: an article, an introduction or a short note about an idea they discussed. Over the following months, his contacts begin to invite him to discuss projects, and his meeting conversion rate rises sharply.

## Summary

Social skills, both verbal and non-verbal, shape relationships, influence and success in business. Learn to read non-verbal signals in context. Build connections by listening, communicating well, staying positive, showing care and following up. Start conversations with family, occupation, recreation and dreams, sharing as well as asking. Earn the relationships you want, and use inversion to avoid behaviours that damage them. Influence by connecting first: engage the senses, be honest and empathetic, think before speaking, customise your approach and avoid needless arguments. People are convinced most readily when they feel understood.
