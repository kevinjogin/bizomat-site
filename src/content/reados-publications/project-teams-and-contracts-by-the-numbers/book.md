## About this book

A capital project of any size is delivered by several organisations at once: an owner, designers, a builder, specialist contractors and suppliers. Each has its own managers, its own cash flow and its own idea of a good result. The contracts between them decide who keeps a dollar saved and who pays a dollar lost, and people respond to that whether or not anyone intends them to. The project's result depends on whether those organisations behave as one team with one goal or as a set of separate businesses defending separate positions.

This book works through that problem with numbers. It starts by mapping the organisations, people and interfaces on one project, then shows how a contract shapes behaviour by moving savings and overruns between the parties. It works lump sum, cost-plus and target-cost contracts in turn, with pain and gain share calculations, bands, caps and target adjustments, then treats alliances and other collaborative contracts. The second half turns from contracts to people: team size and communication paths, staffing ramp-up and the cost of turnover and late additions, and shared goals, co-location and decision rights. The last chapter compares the delivery models by simulation and chooses one. Each chapter follows one running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for owners and sponsors, project and procurement managers, and the project leads of contractors and suppliers. It assumes spreadsheet arithmetic. Where an idea is already covered in the Library collections, such as how an alliance selects partners or how a delivery model is chosen, the book links to it and adds the numbers.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional project. This is an illustration. A family-owned Australian food manufacturer that makes sauces, soups and pantry goods has outgrown its warehouse and two leased third-party stores. It plans a new **distribution centre** on land it owns in Melbourne's west: an 11,500 m² warehouse with an 800 m² office, 14,000 pallet positions of selective racking 11 metres high, case-picking conveyors and controls, and a new warehouse management system. The approved budget is **A$18.0 million excluding GST**, of which **A$15.6 million** is the works allowance, and the target is a working distribution centre **18 months** after approval. Every week of delay costs the business about **A$80,000** in extra leases, shuttle freight and lost efficiency.

Six organisations deliver it: the owner, a designer, a builder, a racking supplier-installer, a conveyor and controls integrator, and a software vendor, with a core team of about twenty people among them. The book follows the choice of contract for the works, the commercial arithmetic of each option, and the team that has to make the chosen option work.

The business, its people, its contractors and every number in the case are invented. None describes BIZOMAT work, a real business or a real project.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first: the map of organisations and the way a contract moves money underlie everything else. Chapters 3 to 6 take the contract models in order of increasing sharing, from lump sum to alliance, and are best read in sequence. Chapters 7 to 9 cover the team itself and can be read in any order after Chapter 2. Chapter 10 brings the commercial and team results together into one choice. Every calculation was checked by running it, and the behaviour assumptions behind the comparison are stated where they are used, so readers can replace them with their own.

The book is one of a series of nine BIZOMAT Library books on project delivery, each worked through its own case. The most closely related are *Reference Class Forecasting by the Numbers*, which sets the budgets and contingencies that this book's contracts have to live within; *Modular Projects by the Numbers*, which covers learning curves and keeping an experienced team together across repeated work; *Front-End Planning by the Numbers*, which covers the definition work that makes a lump sum or a target realistic; and *Stage Gates and Stopping Rules by the Numbers*, which covers the approval decisions that the sponsor and leadership group in this book have to make.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The contract models, team research and Australian guidance used here are published, and the sources are listed with what each was used for. Standard forms of contract are named by number and scope only; their clauses are not quoted, and a real contract needs to be read in full, with its special conditions. The case's prices, shares, rates and behaviour assumptions are teaching values, not market data or research findings, and a real comparison needs its own estimates and advice. Contract choice and drafting have legal, tax and insurance consequences, and work health and safety duties are shared between businesses on the same site under the laws of each state and territory. This book is general information about project delivery. It is not legal, financial or safety advice, and decisions on real contracts are for the people accountable for them, with professional advice.
