Sam Reid joined the Regional Programs Branch of the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO) in mid-2026 as its Grants Program Manager, an EL1 role. In policy he made the case for spending money. Now he has to spend it in a way that survives a complaint or an audit.

This is an illustration. NRIO is a fictional non-corporate Commonwealth entity, and its Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) is invented for this book. The RCFP has A$20.0 million of administered funding over four years, 2026–27 to 2029–30, to upgrade halls, libraries, sports change rooms and similar facilities in towns of fewer than 15,000 people in NRIO's region. The money goes out in two open competitive rounds of A$10.0 million each. Sam is responsible for Round 1, from design to the last acquittal.

Before he designs anything, Sam has to settle a question that sounds too obvious to ask: is this a grant? The answer decides which rules apply, who approves the spending, what must be written down and where it is published. This chapter sets out that framework and the six steps the rest of the book follows. It builds on "Public Money You Can Account For", set in the same fictional agency, but stands on its own.

## What the CGRPs mean by a grant

The [Commonwealth Grants Rules and Principles 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) (CGRPs) define a grant in paragraph 2.3. It is an arrangement for financial assistance, by or on behalf of the Commonwealth, with two features. First, relevant money or other money in the Consolidated Revenue Fund is to be paid to a grantee other than the Commonwealth. Relevant money is, in short, money the Commonwealth or a corporate Commonwealth entity holds (PGPA Act, section 8). Second, the arrangement "is intended to help address one or more of the Australian Government's policy outcomes while assisting the grantee achieve its objectives" (2.3(b)).

The second feature does most of the work: a grant serves the government's outcome and the grantee's own objectives at once.

This is an illustration. In Round 1, Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. will receive A$420,000 to replace its hall roof and build accessible toilets. The committee wants a dry hall that everyone can use; the government wants small towns to have facilities people use. NRIO does not end up owning a hall, and it is not buying anything for itself.

Paragraph 2.4 says the CGRPs apply to "all forms and types of grants, including election commitments". A grant may follow a competitive or non-competitive process, be paid because someone meets eligibility criteria, or be made on a one-off ad hoc basis. That last phrase is narrow. It includes a circumstance where all four of these apply (2.4(c)): an urgent, unexpected need; no opportunity to run another process; one or very few recipients; and no repeats. Payments between non-corporate Commonwealth entities are notional and are not grants (2.5), and nor is a gift of public property (footnote 7).

## What is not a grant

Paragraph 2.6 lists fourteen kinds of financial arrangement that are "taken not to be grants" (Figure 1). The first is the one this chapter is mostly about: buying goods and services for the entity's own use, which the Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) govern instead.

```figure
caption: Paragraph 2.6 of the CGRPs, grouped. Its footnotes say some loans, and some other payments to states, territories, councils and education bodies, may still be covered, so check them before you rely on an item.
- Not grants under the CGRPs

  - Payments other rules already govern | (b) act of grace payments under section 65 of the PGPA Act; (c) compensation; (d) benefits or entitlements set by legislation; (e) tax concessions or offsets

```

The footnotes matter as much as the list. Some concessional loans may still be subject to the CGRPs (footnote 14), and so may other assistance to states and territories, local government and educational institutions (footnotes 15 to 17). The list excludes kinds of payment, not kinds of recipient. A council can receive a grant, because item (i) covers only payments made for the purposes of the Local Government (Financial Assistance) Act 1995.

## Telling a grant from a procurement

The CPRs describe procurement as acquiring goods and services, from identifying the need to awarding and reporting a contract (CPRs 2.7), including buying for another entity or a third party (2.8). The two sets of rules point at each other. CGRPs 2.6(a) takes "the acquisition of goods and services by a relevant entity, for its own use" out of the grants rules. The [CPRs](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2025L01263/latest/text) say in 2.9(a) that procurement does not include "grants (whether in the form of a contract, conditional gift or deed)".

So the form of the document does not decide the question: a grant can be a contract, and a purchase can be labelled a grant. Footnote 33 of the CGRPs says officials should consider the "substantive purposes and characteristics" of an arrangement. Paragraph 4.2 makes the step mandatory: "Officials must establish and document whether a proposed activity is a grant prior to applying the CGRPs." Paragraph 12.4 adds that officials should determine that a grant is the right mechanism at all, rather than, say, a procurement or a statutory power.

Five questions separate the two. Who needs the result? Who decides what is delivered? Whose objectives does the work serve? Who keeps what is produced? Is the payment a price for a service, or a contribution to the grantee's own activity? On the fourth, the CGRPs say entities should not generally assert ownership of intellectual property from grant activities, but should consider a licence for Commonwealth purposes (12.2). If an arrangement only makes sense with the agency owning the result, it is very likely a purchase.

## Where the rules come from

The CGRPs rest on the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text). Section 105C(1) says: "The Finance Minister may, by written instrument, make provision about grants by the Commonwealth." The CGRPs 2024 (F2024L00854) are that instrument. They were made on 19 June 2024, took effect on 1 October 2024 and replaced the Commonwealth Grants Rules and Guidelines 2017. Under section 105C(2), the disallowance provision of the Legislation Act 2003 does not apply to them. Guidance written for the 2017 rules uses different paragraph numbers, so check references against the 2024 text.

**Proper use.** Section 8 defines "proper" as "efficient, effective, economical and ethical". Section 15 requires the accountable authority to govern the entity in a way that promotes the proper use and management of the public resources it is responsible for.

**Ministerial approval.** Under section 71(1), a Minister must not approve a proposed expenditure of relevant money unless satisfied, "after making reasonable inquiries, that the expenditure would be a proper use of relevant money". Section 71(2)(a) requires the Minister to record the terms of the approval in writing as soon as practicable. For grants, the CGRPs add that the Minister must receive written advice from officials before approving (4.10(a)), and they set its minimum content (4.6; Chapter 6).

**Officials' duties.** Sections 25 to 29 bind every official: care and diligence; honesty, good faith and proper purpose; no misuse of position or information; and disclosure of material personal interests.

There must also be legal authority for the arrangement before money is committed (CGRPs 3.8). Paragraph 3.9 names three sources: section 23 of the PGPA Act, for the ordinary services and functions of government; section 32B of the Financial Framework (Supplementary Powers) Act 1997, where the arrangement is specified in its regulations; or specific legislation.

This is an illustration. Sam asks NRIO's legal area to confirm the RCFP's legal authority in writing before the design goes further, and files the advice. Paragraph 4.6(b) requires the written advice to the Minister to state it.

## Who the rules bind, and what "must" means

The CGRPs set the expectations for all non-corporate Commonwealth entities (1.2). They apply to grants administration by ministers, including Assistant Ministers, and by accountable authorities, officials and third parties acting for the Commonwealth (2.9). Paragraph 2.12 is short: "Officials must comply with the CGRPs." Corporate Commonwealth entities are not generally bound: they must comply with Division 6A of the PGPA Rule when a minister is involved in one of their grants, and may be subject to the CGRPs when they act as third parties (footnote 1).

The verbs carry the weight. Paragraph 1.5 marks requirements with "must", and says "The use of the term 'should' in the CGRPs denotes better practice." Part 1 holds the mandatory requirements. Part 2 explains how entities should apply nine key principles (1.4). Better practice is still not a free choice: accountable authorities and officials must put in place practices and procedures consistent with the nine principles (6.3), and officials must have regard to them (4.4(b)). A sound habit: when you depart from a "should", write down why.

Paragraph 2.8 of the CGRPs describes the grants lifecycle: design, assessment and selection, establishment of grants, ongoing management, and evaluation. This book breaks it into six steps and follows Round 1 through each of them (Figure 4).

## When it goes wrong: a study paid for as a grant

This is an illustration. In April 2026, before Sam arrived, the branch wanted a study of the condition and use of community facilities across the region, to inform the RCFP's design. A council in the region offered to do it. An officer drafted a letter offering the council a "grant" of A$80,000, excluding GST, to "deliver" the study by the end of August. NRIO wrote the questions, set the report format and would own the report. A delegate approved it as a grant, it was reported on GrantConnect as one, and A$40,000 was paid when the council signed.

Sam read the file in his second week. The study served NRIO's design work, not any objective of the council's own, so paragraph 2.3(b) was not met. NRIO was acquiring a service for its own use, which paragraph 2.6(a) puts under the CPRs. Calling it a grant changed the paperwork, not the substance.

The cost was real:

- None of the steps the CPRs require had been taken, and value for money was never tested.
- The GrantConnect record described a grant that was not one, so it had to be corrected.
- The council intended to apply to Round 1, so a likely applicant was writing the study that would shape the program's priorities, a probity problem that would have followed every decision in the round.
- The work stopped for seven weeks while NRIO took advice. The findings arrived at the end of October, two months late, after the design had been settled without them.

Sam did not relabel the letter or quietly cancel it. He told the Director in writing the same day and reported it through NRIO's process for possible non-compliance with the finance law. The CFO area, central procurement and NRIO's legal area decided what happened to the letter and the A$40,000 already paid. The remaining work was bought under the CPRs, and the council's part was recorded for the probity adviser to consider.

The control that would have caught it starts with paragraph 4.2: officials must document whether an activity is a grant before applying the CGRPs. NRIO's own rule sets the timing. A one-page record of the tests in Figure 3, signed by the program owner, is seen by central procurement before any arrangement with an outside party is drafted.

## Exercise: grant, procurement or neither

This is an illustration. Ivy has six arrangements on her desk. Classify each as a grant, a procurement or something else, and give the paragraph that decides it.

| | Arrangement |
| --- | --- |
| A | Under Round 1, NRIO offers the Shire of Eastbrook A$500,000 towards a A$800,000 library extension, with the council paying the rest |
| B | NRIO engages Hartley Probity Services to give probity advice on Round 1 |
| C | After an election, NRIO is asked to pay a cricket club in the region A$250,000 for new lights, an election commitment |
| D | A not-for-profit club asks NRIO for a A$200,000 loan at a below-market interest rate to build a clubhouse |
| E | A colleague asks whether a payment to a State, made for the purposes of the Federal Financial Relations Act 2009, should go through the RCFP |
| F | Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. offers to build 20 picnic tables for NRIO's depots for A$12,000 |

### Worked answer

**A: a grant.** Money goes to a grantee outside the Commonwealth (2.3(a)) and serves both the government's outcome and the council's own objective, a larger library (2.3(b)). Being a council does not take it outside the rules: item 2.6(i) covers only payments for the purposes of the Local Government (Financial Assistance) Act 1995, and footnote 16 says other assistance to local government may be covered. The council pays A$800,000 − A$500,000 = A$300,000, which is A$300,000 ÷ A$800,000 = 37.5 per cent. That is above the RCFP's minimum of 25 per cent, or 0.25 × A$800,000 = A$200,000.

**B: a procurement.** NRIO is acquiring a service for its own use (2.6(a)), so the CPRs govern it.

**C: a grant.** The CGRPs apply to all grants, "including election commitments" (2.4). Officials must develop grant opportunity guidelines for all new grant opportunities, including one-off ad hoc grants (4.4(a) and footnote 35), and publish them on GrantConnect (5.2) unless an exemption applies (5.8). If the Minister approves it, officials must first give written advice that meets 4.6. The selection process is a separate question (Chapter 4).

**D: not a grant under the CGRPs, but check.** Loans are excluded by 2.6(f), but footnote 14 warns that some concessional loans may still be covered, and a below-market rate makes this loan concessional. Ask NRIO's legal and finance areas first. Either way, the RCFP offers grants, not loans.

**E: something else.** A payment to a State made for the purposes of the Federal Financial Relations Act 2009 is taken not to be a grant (2.6(h)). It does not belong in the RCFP.

**F: a procurement.** NRIO is buying goods for its own use (2.6(a)). It makes no difference that the seller is a not-for-profit or an RCFP grantee, or that the price is low. The grant and the purchase are separate arrangements under separate rules.

Two are grants, two are procurements and two are something else, one of which needs advice first. In every case the finding is documented (4.2), under NRIO's rule before the paperwork starts.
