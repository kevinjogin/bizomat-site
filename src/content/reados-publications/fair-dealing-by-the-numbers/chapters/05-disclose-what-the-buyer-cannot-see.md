Chapter 4 dealt with describing the goods truly before the work starts. This chapter deals with what happens during the work, when things go wrong in ways the buyer will not see unless the seller tells them. Every fabrication job produces some of these moments: a weld that fails its test and is repaired, a mark from handling, a design assumption that turns out to need checking. Some must be disclosed. Some need not be. The difference matters to the customer, and it matters to the workshop's integrity.

Aquinas addresses the question directly: must a seller state the defects of the thing sold (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.77 a.3)? His answer turns on whether the defect is hidden or manifest. A hidden defect that endangers the buyer, or that lowers the value of the thing, must be disclosed; to conceal it makes the sale unjust and the seller owes compensation. His examples are vivid: a lame horse sold as fleet, a tottering house sold as safe, poisonous food sold as wholesome. A manifest defect, one the buyer can see, such as a horse with one eye, need not be announced, provided the seller takes an appropriate reduction from the price. Disclosing it anyway and lowering the price voluntarily is, he says, very virtuous, though justice does not strictly require it. This chapter applies that framework to three findings on the tank.

## Three findings during fabrication

This is an illustration. During the fabrication of the tank, three things come to light.

**A weld that failed its leak test.** The customer's specification requires the tank to be filled with water and held for 24 hours before polishing is finished. At the base-to-shell weld, a weep appears at one location. The cause, found when the weld is ground out, is a lack of fusion about 40 mm long. The welder grinds out the defect, re-welds it to the qualified procedure, re-polishes and the tank passes a second 24-hour test. The repair takes 3.5 hours, A$367.50 of the workshop's time, which it absorbs.

**A scratch on the outside.** While the tank is being turned, a sling buckle leaves a scratch about 180 mm long on the external shell. It is cosmetic: it does not affect the product-contact surface or the strength of the shell. It is visible to anyone who looks.

**A design load nobody checked.** The specification describes the product as a salted food with a density of up to 1,200 kg per cubic metre. The workshop's standard leg design for tanks of this size has been checked for water, at 1,000 kg per cubic metre, filled to the brim. Nobody has checked it for the denser product.

## The weld repair: hidden, and it affects value

A repaired weld that has passed its test is not a defective weld. The tank now meets the specification. Why disclose anything?

Because the repair history is information the buyer cannot see and might reasonably care about. A customer maintaining a food plant may want to know where a repair was made so that its inspectors can pay attention to it in future. A customer selling the tank on, or relying on its records for an audit, may need the full history. Aquinas's test is whether the concealed matter lowers the value of the thing or exposes the buyer to danger. A properly repaired and retested weld probably does neither in a strict sense. But the workshop is not entitled to make that judgement on the customer's behalf by keeping the information from them, and the cost of disclosure is a paragraph in the manufacturing data book.

### A failure: "it's fixed, nobody needs to know"

When the repair was done, the welder's view was that the matter was closed. The weld passed its retest, the tank met the specification, and writing it down would only "worry the customer". The view was sincere and wrong.

The error was detected by the workshop's own procedure, which requires every failed test to be raised as a nonconformance report, whatever the eventual outcome. When the owner reviewed the week's reports and found none, while the test log showed a failed first test, the gap was obvious. The correction was to raise the report retrospectively, and to include in the data book the location of the repair, the cause, the repair method, the welder's qualification and both test results. The customer's quality manager, receiving the data book, noted the repair and thanked the workshop for recording it.

The deeper lesson concerns Aquinas's distinction between keeping back the truth and asserting a falsehood. Not mentioning the repair in conversation would be one thing. Delivering a data book that showed a single passed leak test, when there had been two tests and a failure, would come close to asserting that the history was clean. Records are statements, and an incomplete record can mislead as surely as a false one.

## The scratch: manifest, so rectify or reduce

The scratch is a manifest defect. The customer's inspector will see it at delivery, and it does not affect the tank's function. Under Aquinas's rule, the workshop need not announce it if it reduces the price appropriately, and in a modern bespoke job the workshop would usually do better than that by simply putting it right.

The two fair options are equal in value to the workshop. Polishing out the scratch would take about 2.5 hours, A$262.50. Alternatively the workshop could leave the scratch and reduce the price by the same A$262.50, A$288.75 including GST. The workshop tells the customer about the scratch before delivery, describes both options and asks which they prefer. The customer, who will be mounting the tank against a wall with that side hidden, chooses the reduction.

What would not be fair is to leave the scratch and say nothing, hoping it is not noticed. Aquinas allows silence about a manifest defect only together with a reduction in price, because the reduction is what keeps the exchange equal. Silence without the reduction leaves the buyer paying for an unmarked tank and receiving a marked one.

## The design load: hidden, and it could endanger

The third finding is the most important, because it concerns safety. A tank holding a denser product puts more load on its legs and on the floor beneath them. The difference is not small.

```figure
- Water at 1,000 kg/m³ | 34.26 | 11.42 kN per leg
- *Product at 1,200 kg/m³ | 40.61 | 13.54 kN per leg
```

The brim volume of the 2,100 mm shell is about 3.233 cubic metres. Full of water, that is about 3,233 kg of contents, a weight of about 31.71 kN; with the tank's own mass of about 260 kg, 2.55 kN, the total is about 34.26 kN, or 11.42 kN on each of three legs. Full of the product at 1,200 kg per cubic metre, the contents weigh about 3,879 kg, 38.06 kN, and the total rises to about 40.61 kN, or 13.54 kN per leg, an increase of about 18.5%.

Whether the standard legs are adequate for that load is an engineering question that this book does not answer, and the illustrative figures here are not a design. What matters for fairness is that the workshop had been about to supply a tank whose structural design basis did not match the customer's stated product. That is a hidden matter, and it is one that could endanger people working near the tank. Aquinas's tottering house is the right comparison: a buyer cannot see whether a structure was designed for the load it will carry.

The workshop's engineer checks the legs and their base plates for the higher load, and the data book states the design basis explicitly: product density up to 1,200 kg per cubic metre, filled to the brim. The customer is also told the total load so that they can confirm their floor will carry it. If the check had shown the legs to be inadequate, the workshop would have redesigned them before delivery. What it would not have done, under any reading of fairness, was deliver the tank without saying.

## What disclosure looks like

Disclosure is not confession or apology. It is giving the buyer the facts they need, in a form they can use, at a time when they can act on them.

| Finding | What is disclosed | Where | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weld repair | Location, cause, repair method, welder qualification, both test results | Manufacturing data book; nonconformance report | Before delivery |
| Scratch | Its size and location; the two options | Email to the customer, then the invoice | Before delivery, so the customer can choose |
| Design load | Design density and total load; that the legs were checked for it | Data book; delivery documents | Before delivery, so the floor can be checked |

Each line answers the question a buyer would ask if they knew to ask it. That is the practical test of whether disclosure is adequate.

The language matters too. A disclosure written in adjectives, such as "a minor issue with one weld, now fully resolved", reassures without informing, and a careful reader will wonder what it is hiding. A disclosure written in facts, such as "lack of fusion about 40 mm long at the base-to-shell weld, 1.2 m clockwise from the outlet; ground out and re-welded to the qualified procedure; second 24-hour leak test passed", lets the reader form their own view. The workshop's practice is to write disclosures as if the reader were an inspector who will be on site in five years with nothing but the data book to go on. Often, that is exactly who reads them.

## The same duty when the workshop is the buyer

Disclosure is usually discussed from the seller's side, but the workshop is a buyer too, of sheet, fittings and subcontract services. Aquinas applies the same logic in both directions. In the article on defects in the thing sold, he notes that a buyer who knows the seller is undervaluing the goods is in the same position as a seller who knows of a defect (II-II q.77 a.2). Knowingly taking advantage of the other party's mistake makes the exchange unequal, whichever side the mistake is on.

This is an illustration. When the 316 sheet arrives, the supplier's invoice prices it at the rate for 304, about A$1,150 less than the quote the workshop accepted. The bookkeeper notices and asks whether to pay the invoice as issued. It is, after all, the supplier's mistake.

The workshop tells the supplier. The reasoning is the same as for the weld repair, only reversed. The supplier cannot see its own error, and the workshop can. Paying the lower amount in silence would take A$1,150 the supplier did not intend to give, on the strength of a mistake the workshop knows about. The supplier issues a corrected invoice and the workshop pays it.

There is a practical side as well as a moral one. A supplier who discovers months later that a customer knowingly paid a mistaken invoice will reasonably wonder what else that customer would take advantage of. A supplier who is told about its own error remembers who told it. In a small regional market, where the workshop depends on a handful of suppliers for quick deliveries and fair treatment when it makes its own mistakes, that memory is worth far more than A$1,150.

## Exercise: a second finding

Before delivery, the workshop's inspector finds a small weep at a nozzle weld during a final leak check, which is repaired and retested in 1.5 hours. The inspector also notices a second cosmetic mark on the lid, which would take 1.5 hours to polish out. For each, say whether it must be disclosed and how, and calculate the price reduction that would be fair if the customer chose to keep the lid as it is.

**Worked answer.** The nozzle weld repair is a hidden matter in the same category as the base weld: the tank now meets the specification, but the history is information the customer cannot see and may need. It goes into the nonconformance record and the data book with its location, cause, repair and retest. The lid mark is manifest and cosmetic. The fair options are to polish it out or to reduce the price by the cost of doing so: 1.5 hours at A$105 is A$157.50, or A$173.25 including GST. The workshop should tell the customer about both before delivery and let them choose how the lid is dealt with. Neither finding is a reason for embarrassment. Both are ordinary events in fabrication, and recording them plainly is what allows the customer to trust the rest of the record.
