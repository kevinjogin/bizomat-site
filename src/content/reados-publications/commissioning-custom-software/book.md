## About this book

Sooner or later, most growing engineering and manufacturing businesses pay someone to build software. A job-tracking app for the shop floor, a quoting tool, a portal for customers, a connection between the accounting system and the machines. The owners are usually expert in their own work and inexperienced at buying software, and the developers they hire are usually expert in software and inexperienced in the business. Both sides mean well. Projects still go over budget, deliver something different from what was imagined, or leave the business dependent on one developer it cannot replace.

This book is for the buyer. It covers deciding whether to build at all, writing a brief developers can price, choosing a developer and a pricing model, contracting so the business owns what it pays for, running the work in short increments, testing and accepting the result, going live on the shop floor and supporting the software for years. Each chapter works one decision through the running case with real numbers and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It assumes no software background. Where a technical idea matters, the chapter explains it in plain terms.

## The running case

The book follows a fictional **precision machining business** of about 40 people with 18 CNC machines, running about 350 jobs a month. Jobs are tracked on paper travellers and a supervisor's spreadsheet. Nobody knows, without walking the floor, which jobs are late, how long each operation really took or how much material was scrapped. The owner decides to commission a shop-floor job-tracking app, used on tablets at the machines and connected to the business's existing accounting and job system, from a local software developer.

All quantities, prices, hours and results are invented for teaching. This is an illustration. None of it describes a KEVOS® client, supplier or project. Money is in Australian dollars, excluding GST unless stated.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 before speaking to any developer: they decide whether to build and what to ask for. Chapters 3 and 4 cover choosing and contracting, and Chapters 5 to 8 cover running, accepting and living with the software. The companion books *CAD Automation by the Numbers* and [Engineering Software You Can Sell](/reados/publications/engineering-software-you-can-sell/) cover building software inside an engineering firm and selling it to others.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

A book on developing and distributing CAD software was supplied for this series. It informed only the developer's-eye view of planning and deployment. The structure, case, calculations and figures here are original. Contract, intellectual property and consumer law points are general information, not legal advice. Software contracts are worth a lawyer's review, and software that controls machines, affects safety or handles personal information carries obligations beyond this book's scope.
