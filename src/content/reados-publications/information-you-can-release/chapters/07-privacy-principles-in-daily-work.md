Most privacy decisions in an agency are small and quick. Does this form need a date of birth? May I send this list to another branch? What can I tell a caller about what we hold on her? Each has an answer in the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), and the answer is easier to give before the question is urgent.

This is an illustration. Besides its FOI work, NRIO's Information Access and Privacy Team handles about 25 privacy enquiries and complaints a year. On Monday 2 August 2027 the secretary of a community group emails the team. She asks for all the information NRIO holds about her personally in its client records, the system where officers log their dealings with community groups and councils. Her email does not mention the FOI Act. Ruth Adeyemi is NRIO's Privacy Officer, but most of the principles that decide the answer are applied, or broken, at someone else's desk.

## Personal information is wider than most people think

The [Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text) defines personal information as "information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable" (s6(1)), true or not, recorded or not. An individual is a natural person, so facts about an incorporated group or a council are not personal information; the name and mobile number of its secretary are. An officer's opinion counts too: a note calling someone "difficult" is personal information about them, fair or not.

Some personal information is also sensitive information (s6(1)), such as information about a person's racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, union membership, sexual orientation or criminal record, and health information, which includes information about an illness, disability or injury (s6FA). A note that a volunteer needs a ground-floor room because of a knee injury is sensitive information, with stricter rules for collecting and using it.

Section 15 says an APP entity "must not do an act, or engage in a practice, that breaches an Australian Privacy Principle". Every agency is an APP entity. A breach of an APP is an interference with the individual's privacy (s13(1)(a)), and the individual may complain to the Information Commissioner (s36(1)). The FOI Act uses the same definition of personal information (FOI Act s4(1)), which is why the two laws meet in s47F and in APP 12.

## APP 1: a policy that matches what the agency does

An APP entity must take reasonable steps to put in place practices, procedures and systems that ensure it complies with the APPs and any binding APP code, and that let it handle inquiries and complaints (APP 1.2). It must have "a clearly expressed and up-to-date policy" about how it manages personal information (APP 1.3), covering among other things what it collects and why, how to seek access or correction, and how to complain (APP 1.4). It must take reasonable steps to make the policy available free of charge and in an appropriate form (APP 1.5).

From 10 December 2026, APP 1 gains three subclauses on automated decisions, inserted by the [Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00128/latest/text) (Schedule 1, item 88). They apply where an entity has arranged for a computer program to make a decision, or to do a thing substantially and directly related to making one; the decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual's rights or interests; and the program uses personal information about that individual (APP 1.7). The policy must then describe the kinds of personal information used, the kinds of decisions made solely by such programs, and those for which they do a thing substantially and directly related to making the decision (APP 1.8). Refusing or failing to decide counts, and an effect may be beneficial or adverse (APP 1.9). The rules apply to decisions made after they start, even if the program or the data is older (item 89).

This is an illustration. Ruth's annual policy review now asks every branch three questions. Does a computer program make a decision about individuals, or a substantial step towards one? Could it significantly affect their rights or interests? Does it use their personal information? Three answers of "yes" put the program in the policy.

## Collect what you need, and say why: APPs 3 and 5

An agency must not collect personal information unless it is "reasonably necessary for, or directly related to, one or more of the entity's functions or activities" (APP 3.1). Sensitive information also needs the person's consent, unless an exception applies, such as collection required or authorised by an Australian law (APP 3.3 and 3.4). Collection must be "only by lawful and fair means" (APP 3.5), and usually from the person themselves (APP 3.6).

The test works field by field. A form that asks for a date of birth "in case we need it" fails. In a free-text field, write only what you would be content for the person to read, because under APP 12 they may.

At or before collection or, if that is not practicable, as soon as practicable after it, the entity must take reasonable steps to tell the person the matters in APP 5.2, or otherwise make sure they are aware of them (APP 5.1). They include who is collecting, the purposes, any law behind the collection, the usual recipients and how to seek access or complain. Describe the real flows: if information will go to a contractor or the Minister's office, say so. What people were told at collection is the best evidence of what they would reasonably expect later.

## Use it for the purpose it was collected for: APP 6

Information is collected for a primary purpose. An entity must not use or disclose it "for another purpose (the secondary purpose)" unless the person consents or an exception applies (APP 6.1). The exceptions met most often in agency work are:
- the person would reasonably expect it, and the secondary purpose is related to the primary purpose, or directly related if the information is sensitive (APP 6.2(a));
- it is required or authorised by or under an Australian law or a court or tribunal order (APP 6.2(b)).

When you rely on an exception, record which one and why.

## Protect it, and dispose of it lawfully: APP 11

An entity holding personal information must take reasonable steps to protect it "(a) from misuse, interference and loss; and (b) from unauthorised access, modification or disclosure" (APP 11.1). Since 11 December 2024 the Act says those steps include "technical and organisational measures" (APP 11.3): access limited to those who need it, but also training, a second check before bulk personal information leaves the agency, and rules about which channel may carry what.

APP 11.2 requires an entity to destroy or de-identify personal information it no longer needs, but not if it is in a Commonwealth record. For an agency, almost everything is. Disposal then follows the Archives Act and an authority from the National Archives (Chapter 2). "Delete it to protect privacy" is not a safe instinct in an agency.

This is an illustration. The spreadsheet emailed to the wrong address on Wednesday 23 June 2027 was an APP 11 question before it was a data breach question. Chapter 8 follows both.

## Access and correction: APPs 12 and 13

An entity that holds personal information about an individual must give the individual access to it on request (APP 12.1). An agency is not required to give access to the extent the FOI Act, or another Commonwealth access law, requires or authorises it to refuse (APP 12.2). It must respond "within 30 days after the request is made" (APP 12.4(a)(i)), in the manner requested if reasonable and practicable (APP 12.4(b)), and "must not charge" (APP 12.7). If it refuses, it must take reasonable steps to give access another way that meets both sides' needs (APP 12.5) and give a written notice of the reasons and the ways to complain (APP 12.9).

Under APP 13, if the entity is satisfied that information is inaccurate, out of date, incomplete, irrelevant or misleading for the purpose for which it is held, or the person asks, it must take reasonable steps to correct it (APP 13.1). It must respond within 30 days and must not charge (APP 13.5). A complaint about either principle goes to the agency first: the Commissioner must not investigate one the person did not first make to the agency, unless that was not appropriate (s40(1A)).

Chapter 1 set out the routing rule: a request for the person's own information that does not invoke the FOI Act is an APP 12 request. Because APP 12.2 adopts the FOI Act's grounds for refusal, the result is often similar either way. The paths differ in form, timing, notices and review, so tell people both exist and let them choose.

## The APP Code: who does what inside the agency

The [Privacy (Australian Government Agencies — Governance) APP Code 2017](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2017L01396/latest/text), in force since 1 July 2018, binds every agency (s7) and sets out how agencies are to comply with APP 1.2 (s8). An agency must:
- have a privacy management plan with specific, measurable goals and targets, and measure and document its performance against the plan at least annually (s9);
- have, at all times, "a designated Privacy Officer" (s10(1)), and ensure the Privacy Officer functions are carried out, including handling enquiries, complaints and access and correction requests, and keeping a record of personal information holdings and the register of privacy impact assessments (PIAs) (s10(5));
- have, at all times, a Privacy Champion who is "a senior official" (s11(3)), and ensure the Champion's functions are carried out, including promoting a privacy culture and reviewing or approving the plan (s11(4));
- conduct a PIA "for all high privacy risk projects" (s12(1)); a project may be one if the agency reasonably considers that it involves new or changed ways of handling personal information likely to have a significant impact on privacy (s12(2));
- keep a register of its PIAs and publish it, or a version of it, on its website (s15(1));
- include privacy training in staff induction, and take reasonable steps to train annually all staff with access to personal information (s16).

This is an illustration. At NRIO, Ruth is the Privacy Officer and the General Counsel is the Privacy Champion. The team keeps the PIA register and the record of holdings, and new projects that change how personal information is handled come to Ruth before contracts are signed.

## The statutory tort, and where agencies stand

Since 10 June 2025, Schedule 2 of the Privacy Act has given individuals a cause of action in tort for serious invasions of privacy. The plaintiff must show an intrusion upon their seclusion or a misuse of information about them, a reasonable expectation of privacy, an invasion that was "intentional or reckless" and serious, and a public interest in their privacy that outweighs any countervailing one (cl 7(1)).

The Schedule does not apply to an invasion of privacy by an agency, or by one of its staff, to the extent it is done in good faith in the performance or purported performance of the agency's functions, or the exercise or purported exercise of its powers (cl 16 and 16A). Intelligence and law enforcement bodies have separate clauses. The exemption depends on good faith and on a function or power: an officer who looks up a neighbour's file out of curiosity is not performing a function of the agency. And it concerns only the tort; the APPs and the Commissioner's complaint process still apply.

## When it goes wrong: the FOI register as a briefing note

This is an illustration. In March 2027 the manager of another NRIO branch was preparing to meet the president of a local historical society about a heritage building. He asked Ethan Brooks by email whether the president had made FOI requests, "so we know what we're dealing with". Ethan searched the team's case register and replied that afternoon with three requests from the previous two years and their subjects. At the meeting the manager remarked that the president had "been busy with FOI". The president complained to NRIO in writing two days later.

The use fails the APP 6 check at every step. The register's details were collected to process FOI requests; briefing a manager before an unrelated meeting is another purpose. There was no consent, no law behind it, and no applicant would expect it: the FOI Act even says a person's reasons for seeking access do not affect their right (s11(2)). NRIO had breached APP 6.1, an interference with the president's privacy (s13(1)(a)) that the president could take to the Commissioner (s36(1)).

The cost was out of proportion to one email. Ruth spent most of a week on the complaint and the apology. A meeting meant to settle a dispute made it worse, and one community leader learned that asking NRIO for documents gets noticed.

Ethan meant to help a senior colleague quickly. The missing control was a stop between the question and the register. NRIO's rule now is that the FOI and privacy case registers are open only to the team, and any request to use their contents for another purpose goes to the Privacy Officer, who runs the APP 6 check and records the answer. The case is now part of the team's annual privacy training (APP Code s16(2)).

## Exercise: the secretary's request

This is an illustration. The secretary's email of Monday 2 August 2027 asks for "all information NRIO holds about me personally" in its client records, with copies by email. It does not mention the FOI Act. Ethan's search finds:
1. her contact record: name, role in the group, mobile number and email address;
2. notes of 9 phone calls and 2 meetings, and 23 emails she sent or received;
3. a 2025 call note, by an officer who has since left NRIO, calling her "combative", with no other detail;
4. in one meeting note, a sentence that another committee member, named, missed the meeting "because of his surgery";
5. an email from NRIO's legal area to a program manager, attached to her record, advising how to respond to a complaint she made in 2026.

Decide the path and due date, what she receives and at what cost, how to treat items 4 and 5 and what notice she must get, and what NRIO must do if she then asks for "combative" to be removed.

### Worked answer

**Path and date.** The email does not state that it is an application for the purposes of the FOI Act, so it is not a valid FOI request (s15(2)(aa)). It is an APP 12 request. The 30 days run from the day after it was made: 3 to 31 August is 29 days, so day 30 is Wednesday 1 September 2027 (APP 12.4(a)(i)). Ethan registers it with that date on the day it arrives. NRIO's practice adds three steps: acknowledge it promptly; confirm her identity, because sending her information to an impostor would be an unauthorised disclosure (APP 11.1); and tell her about the FOI path. Handling the request is a Privacy Officer function (APP Code s10(5)(a)).

**What she receives.** Everything about her in items 1 to 3, by email, because she asked for that manner and it is practicable (APP 12.4(b)). That includes the "combative" note, because an opinion is personal information whether or not it is true (s6(1)). NRIO must not charge (APP 12.7).

**Item 4.** The surgery detail is not information about her. It is health information about another man, so it is sensitive information (s6FA). APP 12 reaches only information about her, so the detail is left out of her copy, and the covering letter says so. Under an FOI request the whole note would be in scope and the detail would be assessed under s47F.

**Item 5.** The email names her and discusses her complaint, so it holds her personal information. It is also legal advice. The FOI Act exempts a document that "would be privileged from production in legal proceedings on the ground of legal professional privilege" (s42(1)), unless privilege has been waived (s42(2)). If the advice is privileged, NRIO is authorised to refuse under the FOI Act, so APP 12.2 lets it refuse under APP 12; authorised is not required, and Ruth could release it. If she relies on the exemption, NRIO must take reasonable steps to meet the secretary's needs another way, such as a plain account of how her complaint was handled (APP 12.5), and give a written notice with the reasons and the ways to complain: to NRIO first, then the Commissioner (APP 12.9).

**Correction.** Her request engages APP 13. The client record exists to record NRIO's dealings with the group. Ruth finds that a bare label written two years earlier, with no facts behind it, is irrelevant and misleading for that purpose, so NRIO must take reasonable steps to correct it (APP 13.1). The note is a Commonwealth record, so the correction follows the Information Management Unit's advice: a dated annotation withdrawing the description, not a quiet deletion (Chapter 2). NRIO must respond within 30 days and must not charge (APP 13.5). Had Ruth refused, NRIO would have owed written reasons (APP 13.3) and, on request, reasonable steps to attach the secretary's own statement to the note (APP 13.4).
