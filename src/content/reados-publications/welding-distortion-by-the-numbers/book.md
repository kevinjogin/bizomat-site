## About this book

Every weld shrinks as it cools, and the shrinkage pulls on the parts it joins. A butt-welded plate draws its edges together and kinks at the seam; a beam with a stiffener welded on one side bows; a flange tilts away from a fillet weld; a thin panel buckles into a wavy surface. Distortion costs fabricators time in straightening, machining and rework, and sometimes scraps parts outright. Most of it can be predicted, at least roughly, and much of it can be prevented by choices made before the first arc is struck.

This book is for fabricators, welding supervisors, designers and manufacturing engineers. It assumes basic physics and strength of materials in SI units. It explains, with numbers, why welds shrink, how much shrinkage to expect across and along a weld, how it turns into bowing, angular distortion and buckling, how restraint trades distortion for stress and cracking, and how welding sequence, presetting, fixtures and straightening control it.

## Early manuals as a starting point

The early welders understood distortion from hard experience. The Davis-Bournonville course of 1919 told its students that whatever is expanded by heat must contract again, and that a welder who tried to prevent expansion or contraction was likely to crack the joint or something else. Campbell's Army manual of the same year showed how to jack a cast pulley wheel apart before welding a broken spoke, so that the weld's contraction would bring it back to shape. And Herbert L. Whittemore, testing welds at the University of Illinois in 1910, clamped the test plates with their edges open at the far end to allow for what Whittemore called creep, the drawing together of the edges ahead of the weld as the metal behind it cooled; when the allowance was left out, the plates warped badly.

Those observations are the foundations of distortion control. Each chapter takes one of them, puts numbers on it, and applies it to a modern fabrication.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A fabrication shop builds welded steel products: plate girders for small bridges and platforms, base frames for machines, and thin stainless steel panels for food-processing equipment. It has had trouble with girders that bow, frames whose mounting faces do not sit flat, and panels that buckle, and its production manager wants to understand the causes and control them.

The shop, its products, measurements and costs are invented for teaching. They are not BIZOMAT work. Real structures and products are made to the applicable standards and specifications, which set tolerances on straightness, flatness and dimensions.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 explains why welds distort. Chapters 2 and 3 cover shrinkage across and along a weld, Chapter 4 angular distortion and Chapter 5 buckling of thin plate. Chapter 6 deals with restraint and residual stress. Chapters 7 and 8 cover welding sequence and presetting with fixtures, Chapter 9 straightening, and Chapter 10 planning distortion control and keeping the record. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. The shrinkage estimates in it are illustrations based on simplified models; real distortion depends on many factors and is best confirmed by measurement on the shop's own work. Straightening by heat can damage some steels and must follow the material's limits. Structures and products must meet the tolerances of their applicable standards and specifications.
