Advice is the part of a business manager's work that leaders remember. A budget report, a risk register or a procurement plan matters because at some point someone must decide something on the strength of it. The business manager's task is to put that decision in front of the right person, early enough to matter, with the facts and the options laid out so that the choice is real.

This is an illustration. An early test of Lena's advice came from the suppliers line. At 31 October 2026 its forecast outturn was A$2,250,000 against a budget of A$2,100,000, a forecast overspend of A$150,000 (Chapter 2). An invoices-only view still looked comfortable. The Branch Manager needed to know, and needed to choose what to do.

## Advice that is frank, honest and timely

The APS Value of being Impartial, in section 10 of the Public Service Act, describes an APS that is "apolitical and provides the Government with advice that is frank, honest, timely and based on the best available evidence". The same standard serves a business manager advising a Branch Manager.

Frank means saying the unwelcome thing plainly, in the first lines, not softening it until it disappears. Honest means showing what you do not know as well as what you do, and never shading a number to make a position look better. Timely means arriving while the options are still open; advice that is right but late has little value. Evidence means numbers that someone else could check, with their sources named.

Advice is not a decision. Lena advises and the Branch Manager decides; that is how NRIO splits the roles (Chapter 1). If the decision goes another way and Lena still disagrees, she says so once, with reasons, and makes sure the record shows both the advice and the decision. Then she carries it out. The APS Code of Conduct requires employees to comply with lawful and reasonable directions (subsection 13(5)).

## The decision brief

A decision brief has one job: to get a clear decision from the right person by a stated date. Its shape follows from that job. Keep it to one or two pages and put the detail in attachments. Mark it "for decision" with the date at the top.

```figure
type: steps
title: The five parts of a decision brief
caption: The decision sought goes at the top as well as the bottom, so a reader who stops after the first paragraph still knows what is being asked.
- Issue and consequence | what is wrong, and what happens if nothing is done, in two sentences
- Facts | the numbers, where they come from and what is still uncertain
- Options | each with its cost, its main risk and who would have to act
- Recommendation | the option you advise and why, in a few lines
- * Decision sought | the exact words the decision-maker agrees to, and the date it is needed by
```

This is an illustration. Lena sent her brief to the Branch Manager on Thursday 5 November 2026, the fourth working day after the month ended and ahead of the monthly meeting on Wednesday 11 November. Its figures are the branch's internal budget figures, which exclude GST (Chapter 2); procurement values under the CPRs include GST.

The issue: the suppliers line is forecast to overspend by A$150,000, or 7.1% of its A$2,100,000 budget (150,000 ÷ 2,100,000 = 7.14%, rounded to one decimal place). The cause is reactive repairs: roofs and plumbing at older sites are failing faster than the allowance assumed, so reactive repairs are forecast at A$610,000 against an allowance of A$450,000, A$160,000 over. A renewed site services contract adds A$10,000 and planned maintenance is A$20,000 under: A$160,000 + A$10,000 − A$20,000 = A$150,000 (Chapter 2). If nothing changes, the branch will exceed the line by 30 June 2027, and the choices narrow each month as more of the remaining spend is committed.

The facts: A$620,000 paid, A$910,000 committed in open orders and contract call-offs, and A$720,000 forecast but not yet committed, A$2,250,000 in all. Tom checked every open order and call-off with the people managing the work. Paid and committed together are A$1,530,000, already 72.9% of the line. The least certain number is the A$330,000 of reactive repairs still forecast, because failures are hard to predict.

Any real choice sits in the A$720,000 not yet committed, and not all of that is open. Safety, legal and compliance work is not available for deferral, and neither are repairs that cannot wait. Nor is the facility condition survey (Chapter 3). It was inside the A$720,000 at 31 October, taking most of the A$200,000 forecast for planned maintenance, but Lena approved it in early November, so it is now a commitment. Staging it was set aside, because the maintenance tender depends on its results (Chapter 4).

| Option | What it means | Forecast outturn | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Absorb in full | Defer or hold A$150,000 of open items, 20.8% of the A$720,000 forecast at 31 October | A$2,100,000, with no margin | Deferred repairs move cost into 2027–28 and temporary repairs may fail; no room for further failures in eight months |
| 2. Ask for funds | Ask the CFO for A$150,000 at NRIO's mid-year review in February, on the evidence that the reactive allowance was too low | A$2,250,000 against a revised A$2,250,000, if approved | If refused in February, far less of the year's spend can still change |
| 3. Defer part, ask for part | Defer or hold A$90,000 now (12.5% of the forecast at 31 October), ask the CFO for A$60,000, and hold A$60,000 of reserve items uncommitted until the CFO decides | A$2,160,000; A$2,100,000 if the request is refused and the reserve items are deferred | Some cost moves into 2027–28; the reserve items wait until February |

The recommendation was option 3. It acts now on the items that can wait at least cost, asks the CFO for less, and keeps a fallback that still works if the answer is no. It also says plainly that deferring a repair moves its cost into next year rather than removing it, so the brief adds a step for the cause: reset the reactive repairs allowance for 2027–28 from the condition survey's results, so that the same gap does not open again. The decision sought is the subject of this chapter's exercise.

## Mapping the stakeholders

Before the brief went up, Lena mapped who would care about it and who could change it. Influence is the power to make or block the decision. Interest is how much the outcome affects someone's work.

```figure
type: matrix
title: Who needs what from the overspend brief
x: Interest in the suppliers forecast, low to high
y: Influence over the decision, low to high
hot: 2
caption: This is an illustration. The positions hold for this decision only. The same people sit in different cells for a different issue.
- Keep satisfied: the CFO | decides on extra funds; wants early warning and one clear request
- Manage closely: the Branch Manager and the Facilities Director | decide the deferral; own the program and its site priorities
- Monitor: central procurement and the risk team | involved only if a deferral changes a contract or a risk rating
- Keep informed: the site coordinators | carry out the change; hear it from Lena after the decision
```

The map changed what Lena did. The Facilities Director sets which sites come first (Chapter 1), so the deferral list was agreed with the Facilities Director before the brief went up; a Branch Manager should not learn of a disagreement from the brief itself. The CFO's area had a short early warning that a request might come, without a number until the Branch Manager decided. The site coordinators heard nothing until the decision was made, and then heard it from Lena. Contractors hear only what a decision means for their work, through Tom, once it is made.

## Competing priorities and escalation

Deciding what to defer is a priority question. The [Work Priority Score](/tools/priority/) gives a common scale for it. It scores impact, urgency, risk if delayed and effort, each from 1 to 5, and weights them 35%, 30%, 20% and 15%. Effort is reversed as ease, so easier work scores higher. The result is a score out of 100. The tool keeps mandatory safety, legal and compliance work outside the scoring: that work is scheduled, not ranked.

This is an illustration. Lena and the Facilities Director scored the open items in the uncommitted forecast. Safety and compliance work, such as fire safety servicing and electrical testing, stayed in the forecast unscored, as did make-safe repairs.

| Open item | Amount | Impact | Urgency | Risk if delayed | Effort | Score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Roof and gutter repair, remote office | A$60,000 | 4 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 83 |
| Grounds renewal, four sites | A$25,000 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 49 |
| Door and joinery repairs, four community buildings | A$35,000 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 46 |
| Travel and training not yet booked, January to June | A$40,000 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 42 |
| Permanent plumbing repairs, two community buildings already made safe | A$50,000 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 37 |

The roof score shows the arithmetic, with ease as 6 minus effort: (4 × 0.35 + 5 × 0.30 + 4 × 0.20 + 3 × 0.15) ÷ 5 × 100 = (1.40 + 1.50 + 0.80 + 0.45) ÷ 5 × 100 = 4.15 ÷ 5 × 100 = 83. The roof must be repaired before the wet season, so it stays. The two lowest scores, A$50,000 and A$40,000, make the A$90,000 deferral. The next two, A$35,000 and A$25,000, make the A$60,000 reserve. Option 1 would defer or hold all four lower-scoring items: A$25,000 + A$35,000 + A$40,000 + A$50,000 = A$150,000.

The score informs judgement; it does not replace it. The Facilities Director could move an item with a reason, and the reason would be recorded. Lena's first draft favoured option 1, finding the whole A$150,000 within the line. The Facilities Director objected to deferring all four items at once. Neither could settle that alone, so they built option 3 together and the brief set out both views. When two priorities collide and the business manager cannot settle them, the answer is to escalate early with options, not to choose quietly or wait. Escalate when the decision is above your authority, when it affects another person's area, or when the risk is more than you can accept. Bring the options, the cost and risk of each, and your recommendation.

## When it goes wrong: the brief that came too late

This is an illustration. Consider the brief Lena nearly wrote. She wanted December's numbers to be sure, then the end-of-year break intervened, and the brief went up at the end of February, after the mid-year review had closed. It ran to five pages of background, was marked "for noting" and put its only request in the last paragraph.

Suppose the uncommitted A$720,000 had been committed evenly over the eight months from November to June, about A$90,000 a month (720,000 ÷ 8). By the end of February, A$360,000 more would have been committed, leaving A$360,000 open, and much of that would be repairs that cannot wait and safety and compliance work. The low-priority items would already be ordered or spent. The Branch Manager would be left with two poor choices: overspend, or cut work that matters.

What should have happened is what did happen. The brief went up in the first week of November, with the decision sought on page one, marked "for decision" with a date. Where a number is uncertain, say so and give a range; waiting for certainty uses up the options. A brief that hides its decision or arrives late is not cautious advice. It is a decision made by default.

## The first ninety days

Lena's brief worked because she already knew the delegations, the people and the budget. Most of what made it possible was learned in her first months in the role. A plan for the first ninety days puts that learning in order.

```figure
type: timeline
title: A first ninety days for a business manager
caption: Learn the rules and the people before changing anything, then deliver one improvement that someone has asked for.
- Days 1 to 10 | Learn the frameworks and your own authority | the delegations instrument, the Accountable Authority Instructions and branch procedures; who holds which power (Chapter 1)
- Days 11 to 30 | Learn the people | the Branch Manager's priorities, the program owners, the CFO's area, central procurement, the risk team, and a one-to-one with each team member
- Days 31 to 60 | Understand the risks, the budget and the reporting cycle | each budget line with commitments, the risk register, the monthly and annual cycles
- * Days 61 to 90 | Deliver one wanted improvement | ask what slows people down, choose one fix you can finish, deliver it and write down how it works
- Day 90 | Review with your manager | what you learned, what you changed and the plan for the next ninety days
```

The order matters. Delegations come first because a newcomer's most likely early mistake is acting on authority they do not have. People come before process because most of what a branch knows is not written down. The improvement comes last, and it should be one that someone wants, not one the newcomer brought from a previous job. Ask each team member what they would change first; the people closest to the work usually know where time is lost. In the case, the Branch Manager's early question was what the budget really showed (Chapter 2), and a monthly report with commitments answered it. Writing down how the fix works is a small act of Stewardship, the APS Value that asks the service to build its capability and institutional knowledge.

## Showing the capability in a selection process

Sooner or later a business manager applies for a role. Section 10A(2) of the Public Service Act says a decision is based on merit if all eligible members of the community had a reasonable opportunity to apply, candidates' relative suitability is assessed through a competitive selection process, the assessment is based on the relationship between their work-related qualities and those genuinely required for the duties, it focuses on their relative capacity to achieve outcomes related to the duties, and it is the primary consideration in the decision. An application therefore has to show capacity to achieve outcomes, with evidence.

The APSC's guide for people outside the APS, [Cracking the code](https://www.apsc.gov.au/working-aps/joining-aps/cracking-code/3-applying-aps-job-cracking-code), describes selection criteria as the skills and qualities required to perform the job, used to make selection decisions, and notes that long and complicated criteria are becoming less common. For EL1 roles, the APSC's Integrated Leadership System names five capabilities: Shapes strategic thinking; Achieves results; Cultivates productive working relationships; Exemplifies personal drive and integrity; and Communicates with influence.

Specific examples carry the most weight. A simple shape is situation, task, action and result. This is an illustration. Lena's example might read like this. Situation: a third of the way through the year, invoices showed 29.5% of the suppliers line spent, but commitments and forecast put it A$150,000 over budget. Task: give the Branch Manager the true position and options in time to act. Action: rebuilt the position with Arun and Tom, ranked the open items with the Facilities Director, and sent a brief with three costed options four working days after the month ended. Result: the Branch Manager decided within a week; A$90,000 was deferred or held and A$60,000 was sought from the CFO, whose answer is not yet known. One example can show several capabilities: here, Achieves results, Communicates with influence and Cultivates productive working relationships.

Be exact about where your authority stopped. "I recommended; the Branch Manager decided" is stronger than a vague "we cut A$150,000", because it is true and a referee can confirm it. Claim only what you can substantiate from records or a referee. The Code of Conduct asks APS employees to behave honestly and with integrity (subsection 13(1)), and an application is no exception.

## Exercise: write the decision paragraph

Using the facts and option 3 above, write the "decision sought" paragraph of Lena's brief. ### Worked answer

This is an illustration. One version reads:

> **Decision sought by Friday 13 November 2026.** That you:
>
> (a) agree to defer to 2027–28 the permanent plumbing repairs at two community buildings that have been made safe (A$50,000), and to hold travel and training not yet booked for January to June (A$40,000), A$90,000 in all. The Facilities Director agrees. Agreed / Not agreed
>
> (b) agree that I prepare, for your signature, a request to the CFO for A$60,000 for the mid-year review in February. Agreed / Not agreed
>
> (c) note that A$60,000 of reserve items, door and joinery repairs at four community buildings (A$35,000) and grounds renewal at four sites (A$25,000), will stay uncommitted until the CFO decides, and will be deferred if the request is refused. Noted
>
> (d) agree that the branch's 2027–28 budget bid resets the reactive repairs allowance from the condition survey's results. Agreed / Not agreed
>
> A decision by 13 November is needed because, on average, about A$90,000 of the remaining spend will be committed each month.

The arithmetic behind it:

| Step | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Forecast outturn now | A$620,000 + A$910,000 + A$720,000 | A$2,250,000 |
| Forecast overspend | A$2,250,000 − A$2,100,000 | A$150,000 |
| Deferral list | A$50,000 + A$40,000 | A$90,000 |
| Reserve list | A$35,000 + A$25,000 | A$60,000 |
| Outturn after (a) | A$2,250,000 − A$90,000 | A$2,160,000 |
| Budget if (b) is approved | A$2,100,000 + A$60,000 | A$2,160,000 |
| Outturn if (b) is refused and (c) applies | A$2,160,000 − A$60,000 | A$2,100,000 |
| Monthly pace of commitments, if even | A$720,000 ÷ 8 months | A$90,000 |

Either way the line finishes within its budget, and the Branch Manager can see on one page what is being decided, by whom and by when.
