## About this book

Static electricity is the oldest electrical phenomenon people know and one of the least understood on a factory floor. It makes film cling and labels misfeed, attracts dust to freshly painted parts, shocks operators at a doorway, destroys electronic components that look undamaged, and, in the wrong place, ignites a flammable vapour or a dust cloud. The same physics, harnessed deliberately, charges powder coating so that it wraps around a part, neutralises charged film with a cloud of ions, and pulls welding fume out of the air onto metal plates.

This book explains static electricity as engineering. It starts with charge, field, voltage and stored energy, then how materials charge and how long the charge lasts, how gases break down into sparks and corona, and when a discharge can ignite something. It then covers earthing and bonding, electrostatic powder coating, ionisers, the control of electrostatic discharge (ESD) in electronics assembly, electrostatic precipitators, and running a static control programme.

It is for manufacturing, process, safety, quality and maintenance engineers, supervisors and technicians. It assumes engineering or trade training and comfort with voltage, current and simple algebra; the physics is explained as it is used. Each chapter works the running case with real numbers, includes one plausible mistake and how it was found, links the KEVOS® tools that do the chapter's calculations, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one factory. This is an illustration. A maker of steel electrical enclosures and control cabinets in Dandenong South, Victoria, cuts, folds and welds sheet steel, powder coats the enclosures, and assembles electronic control boards into some of them. Static matters in four places:

- **Powder line PL-2:** a conveyorised powder coating line with two automatic corona guns and a manual touch-up gun, a booth with cartridge filter recovery, and a curing oven at 200 °C. Enclosures hang from steel hangers on an earthed overhead conveyor.
- **Assembly area:** an ESD-protected area where technicians assemble and test control boards, and clean them with isopropyl alcohol.
- **Window line:** polycarbonate viewing windows arrive with a protective film that is peeled off before fitting.
- **Welding bay:** four welding stations extracted to a two-stage electrostatic precipitator that returns cleaned air to the building.

Charges, voltages, energies, airflows, prices and failure records are the factory's working figures or are calculated from stated assumptions. They do not describe KEVOS® work or any real business, equipment maker or supplier. Prices are in Australian dollars excluding GST, because a GST-registered business claims the GST back on its costs.

## How to use the book

Chapters 1 to 4 explain charge, discharges and ignition; Chapters 5 to 9 earthing, powder coating, ionisers, ESD control and precipitators; Chapter 10 brings them together into a programme. Read Chapters 1 and 3 first; the others stand on their own once the case is familiar.

Each chapter links the KEVOS® calculators it uses. The tools do the arithmetic; the chapter explains what the inputs mean and where they come from. Every result in the text was checked by calculation, and the numbers in figures match the text.

## Limits and responsibility

This book is educational. Electrostatic ignition of flammable atmospheres is a serious hazard. Hazardous area classification, the selection of equipment for hazardous areas and the control of ignition sources must follow work health and safety law, the applicable Australian standards and the guidance of competent people, and powder coating booths and their electrical equipment must meet the standards that apply to them. High-voltage equipment such as powder guns and precipitators must be installed and maintained to the maker's instructions by qualified people. Standards are named by number and scope only. Ignition energies, resistances and other values in the case are illustrations; use the material supplier's test data, the equipment maker's documents and your own measurements for real work.
