## About this book

Rubber behaves like no other engineering material. It stretches to several times its length and springs back. Its volume barely changes however it is deformed. Its stiffness comes mostly from entropy rather than from atomic bonds, so it gets stiffer, not softer, as it warms. And when it is inflated, it can reach a pressure beyond which it no longer resists, and simply keeps growing until it bursts. Designers who carry the habits of steel into rubber, small strains, linear springs and a fixed modulus, get answers that are wrong by large factors.

This book works through rubber's numbers in the order a stretched or inflated rubber part is designed: stiffness from hardness, stress at large stretch in one and two directions, inflation and its pressure limit, contact and friction, temperature and the thermoelastic effect, relaxation and ageing, and finally testing. Rubber mounts and vibration isolators are covered in Vibrating Machine Elements by the Numbers; this book concentrates on parts that stretch a long way.

It suits mechanical designers, product engineers and maintenance engineers. It assumes school algebra, the idea of stress and strain, and a little familiarity with gases and pressure. No previous study of rubber is needed.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional product. This is an illustration. A small manufacturer of plumbing test equipment designs TP-100, an inflatable test plug for pipes with a 100 mm bore. It is a plain rubber sleeve, 88 mm outside diameter with a 4 mm wall, clamped between two metal end plates 120 mm apart. Air at 150 kPa gauge inflates the sleeve against the pipe wall, sealing the pipe so that a section of drainage can be filled with water and pressure tested. The sleeve is a natural rubber compound of 50 Shore A.

Real test plugs are often reinforced with fabric or cord and carry their makers' own ratings. TP-100 is deliberately unreinforced so that the rubber's own behaviour can be followed through the book. The manufacturer, the compound's properties, the test pressures and every mistake are invented and do not describe BIZOMAT work or a real product.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The geometry, gas laws and rubber models in this book are standard, and every calculation was checked by running it. The models describe idealised rubber; real compounds differ with their recipe, filler, cure and age, and a compound's own test data takes precedence over any estimate here. The neo-Hookean and Gent models are used because they show the shape of rubber's behaviour clearly, not because they fit every compound.

Inflatable equipment stores energy and can fail violently. Pressure testing of plumbing and drainage is governed by Australian standards, plumbing regulators and the equipment maker's instructions, and nothing in this book replaces them. The book is educational; the design and rating of real pressurised rubber products are matters for competent engineers working from tested data. The thermodynamics text listed in the references was used only as a map of ideas; nothing from it is reproduced.
