## About this book

A mechanical drawing is an instruction to strangers. The machinist, the inspector, the buyer and the supplier's estimator will read it without you in the room, often years after it was drawn, sometimes after it has been printed at the wrong size or copied from a copy. Everything they need must be on the sheet, and every mark must mean one thing. The conventions in the Australian drawing standards exist to make that possible.

This book works through those conventions in the order a drawing is built: sheet and title block, lines and lettering, views and sections, dimensions, tolerances, surface texture, conventional representation of standard features, and the final check before release. It is written for people who make drawings and for people who must act on them. It assumes you can read a simple orthographic drawing and do basic arithmetic. It does not teach a CAD package; the Library’s CAD drafting collection covers software practice, and this book explains what the resulting sheet must say.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional part. This is an illustration. A small engineering business designs a bearing housing for a centrifugal pump. The housing is machined from a grey iron casting. It has a flange 140 mm in diameter and 12 mm thick, drilled with four 9 mm holes on a 115 mm pitch circle; a body 95 mm in diameter; a locating spigot 100 mm in diameter projecting 5 mm from the flange face; and a bearing bore 62 mm in diameter to take a deep-groove ball bearing. Four M6 tapped holes on a 78 mm pitch circle in the rear face hold an end cap with a lip seal, and an M8 × 1 lubrication hole enters the body at an angle. The overall length is 77 mm.

The housing is drawn on an A3 sheet at full size. Each chapter adds to the drawing, and most chapters include a mistake that a real drawing office or supplier could plausibly make, how it was found and how it was corrected. The part, the business and the mistakes are invented; none describes KEVOS® work or a real supplier.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The conventions in this book come from three supplied Australian Standards: AS 1100.101-1992 on general principles, AS 1100.201-1992 on mechanical engineering drawing, both reconfirmed in 2014, and AS ISO 128.24-2005 on lines for mechanical drawings. Values are cited with their clause, but the standards' tables and figures are not reproduced. Buy the standards your work calls up from Standards Australia, and check their current status: when this book was written, the Standards Australia store listed AS 1100.101 as pending revision.

Practice has moved on in places. Surface texture indication in particular follows later ISO standards on many overseas drawings, and Chapter 8 explains how to handle the difference. Where a contract or customer specifies a different standard, that standard governs. Tolerances, fits and surface finishes for real parts depend on function and on manufacturers' data; the values in the running case illustrate how to present requirements, not what they should be.
