Profit margins help you understand how much of each sales dollar survives different categories of costs. The most useful insights often come from comparing trends over time and against similar businesses.

## Gross margin

**Gross margin = (Revenue − Cost of goods sold) ÷ Revenue × 100**

Suppose a fictional company reports:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | $10,000,000 |
| Direct cost of goods sold | $6,500,000 |
| Gross profit | $3,500,000 |
| Gross margin | 35% |

The company retains 35 cents of gross profit from each dollar of revenue before paying other expenses. That might be excellent for one industry and ordinary for another. A service business, retailer and heavy manufacturer should not be judged against the same gross-margin target.

## Operating margin

Operating expenses include items such as administration, selling costs and depreciation. If those expenses total $2 million, the company has $1.5 million in operating profit, or a **15% operating margin**.

Operating margin gives a broader view of profitability than gross margin, although accounting classification differences can complicate comparisons.

## Net margin

After interest, tax and other relevant items, assume the company retains $900,000 in net profit. Its net margin is **9%**. A strong net margin can be encouraging, but it does not by itself establish financial strength or an attractive share valuation.

## What a five-year trend might reveal

Imagine revenue increased every year but gross margin fell from 40% to 35%, while operating costs rose faster than sales. The company is growing, yet its competitive position or cost control may be weakening. Alternatively, changes in product mix or a deliberate move into a lower-margin market might explain the trend.

Check for irregular gains or losses. A company may report attractive net earnings because it sold property or recorded a tax benefit. Such events should not automatically be treated as recurring operating performance.

## An important caution about research and development

Research spending is not inherently a weakness. In technology, pharmaceuticals and specialist engineering, continued investment can be necessary to sustain a competitive advantage. The right question is whether that spending produces lasting economic returns—not whether it can be eliminated.

### Key takeaway

**Consistency, industry context and the sources of profit matter more than one impressive percentage.**

### Quick reference

- Compare gross, operating and net margins over at least five years where available.
- Investigate unusual movements and one-off gains.
- Compare businesses with genuinely similar economics.
- Confirm that profits are supported by operating cash flow.
