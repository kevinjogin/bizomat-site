When a company has more cash than it needs, it has three broad options: reinvest it, pay it out as dividends, or use it to buy back its own shares. Buybacks have become one of the most common uses of surplus cash among large listed companies, and one of the most debated.

*Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements* treats a history of buybacks as one of the signs of a business with a durable competitive advantage, with some important conditions.

## What a buyback does

When a company buys its own shares, fewer shares remain in the hands of investors. Each remaining share now represents a slightly larger slice of the same business and the same earnings. Earnings per share rise even if total earnings do not change.

Shares bought back may be cancelled or held by the company as **treasury stock**. Treasury shares carry no votes and receive no dividends. On the balance sheet they appear as a deduction from shareholders' equity.

## Why buybacks can signal strength

A company can only buy back shares consistently if it generates more cash than it needs to run and grow the business. Businesses with heavy capital needs, high debt or erratic earnings rarely have that luxury.

That is why the book treats **the presence of treasury stock and a history of buybacks** as a positive sign: it suggests the business throws off surplus cash year after year. It fits with the other signals in this series, including high margins, low capital spending, low debt and consistent earnings.

## The tax angle

The book also notes a reason Buffett has favoured buybacks over dividends in some circumstances. A dividend is generally taxed as income when it is received. A buyback raises the value of the remaining shares, and owners who do not sell are not taxed on that gain until they eventually do. For long-term owners, that deferral lets more of the return keep compounding.

Tax rules differ between countries and change over time, including in Australia, where franking credits change the comparison considerably. The principle is about deferral, not a universal rule.

## When buybacks help, and when they do not

A buyback is an investment decision like any other. It helps the remaining owners only if the shares are bought for **less than they are really worth**. The same purchase at an inflated price transfers value from the owners who stay to the owners who sell.

Buybacks can also be misused:

- **To flatter earnings per share** when total earnings are flat or falling.
- **To offset shares issued to executives**, so the share count never actually falls.
- **Funded by borrowing**, which adds risk to make a short-term figure look better.
- **Instead of necessary investment**, starving the business of what it needs to stay competitive.

Buybacks also reduce shareholders' equity, which inflates return on equity. As another article in this series explains, adding treasury stock back to equity shows the return without that effect.

## Reading buybacks well

When a company reports buybacks, a few questions help separate signal from noise:

- Is the share count actually falling over time?
- Are buybacks funded from operating cash flow rather than debt?
- Does the company still invest adequately in the business?
- Were shares bought at sensible prices, or mainly at market peaks?

## What this means for founders

Most young businesses will not buy back shares for many years. But the underlying idea matters early: what should a business do with cash it does not need? The disciplined answer is to compare every option, whether reinvesting, holding a reserve, repaying debt or returning money to owners, on the return it will genuinely produce. A company investing in itself is only a good investment if the price is right.
