This is an illustration. Grace Mitchell's brief of Fri 13 Aug 2027 promised the Minister an options paper by the end of October, built on evidence NRIO did not yet have. Its working problem statement named three suspected causes: the cost of assessments, the shortage of bridge engineers and poor asset data. Nobody knew how many bridges were affected or what the limits cost.

Between Mon 23 Aug and Wed 6 Oct 2027, the Strategy and Policy Branch surveyed councils and ran three roundtables, and Tom Becker estimated what the load limits cost heavy vehicle operators. This chapter covers gathering and judging evidence like that, designing consultation that can change the advice, recording and reporting back, and the extra duties when a proposal is made by legislative instrument.

## Kinds of evidence, and how to judge them

Policy evidence comes in a few kinds: administrative data (the records agencies and councils keep as they work), surveys, consultation, estimates and models, and research and evaluations of past programs. Each answers a different question, and each has a typical weakness.

Put the same questions to every piece. Is it relevant to the problem, or only near it? Does it measure what it claims? Would it give the same answer again? Who is missing from it? Does the source gain from one answer? No evidence passes every test. The aim is a fair picture of what it shows and how confident you are.

```figure
type: compare
title: Kinds of evidence for a policy problem
cols: What it can show | Its usual limit | Check before relying on it
rowhead: Kind
caption: General guidance. Most advice needs more than one kind, because the limits of one are often covered by another.
- Administrative data | what was done, counted the same way over time | records only what someone needed to record | definitions, gaps and how the records are kept
- Survey | what many people report, on the same questions | who did not answer, and what respondents gain | response rate, wording, and how answers compare with records
- Consultation | experience, reasons, and what others missed | loud voices and organised interests | who was not in the room, and whether the record is fair
- Estimate or model | a size or cost from stated assumptions | only as good as its inputs | each assumption, and how the answer moves when it changes
- Research and evaluations | what worked elsewhere | different places, times and conditions | whether the conditions match yours
```

## The council survey

This is an illustration. Amara Osei designed the survey with Tom. It went to all 142 councils in NRIO's program area and ran from Mon 23 Aug to Fri 17 Sep 2027. For each council's road bridges and major culverts, it asked about numbers, the last engineering assessment of the load each can carry, precautionary load limits, closures, signed detour lengths, the cost of an assessment, and why structures had not been assessed. It defined a load assessment, so every council answered the same question, and counted a structure with no record of one in the last 10 years as not assessed. Three councils of different sizes tried a draft first.

The results:

- 96 of the 142 councils responded: 96 ÷ 142 = 67.6 per cent, reported as 68 per cent.
- Respondents own 4,410 road bridges and major culverts.
- 1,540 of these have had no load assessment in the last 10 years: 1,540 ÷ 4,410 = 34.9 per cent, reported as 35 per cent.
- 410 carry precautionary load limits, and 37 are closed.
- Councils said one assessment costs between A$8,000 and A$15,000.
- Their three main reasons for not assessing were cost, the shortage of bridge engineers and poor asset data.

## Reading the survey honestly

This is an illustration. A 68 per cent response is good for a voluntary survey, but it leaves 142 − 96 = 46 councils, or 46 ÷ 142 = 32.4 per cent, unheard. Response bias is the risk that those who answer differ, in ways that matter, from those who do not. Here it could run either way. Councils hoping for funding may have been keener to answer, making the problem look bigger. Councils with the poorest records may have been unable to answer, making it look smaller. Reasoning cannot settle the direction, so check what you can.

Tom compared respondents with non-respondents on what NRIO already knew about every council: its size and the length of its local road network. Non-respondents were, on average, smaller councils, and among respondents the small councils were the most likely to report poor asset data. Tom concluded that the survey probably understates the problem across the program area, and wrote down why. He did not scale the results up, and NRIO's advice describes every number as what respondents reported.

Three other limits needed the same care.

"No assessment in the last 10 years" does not mean unsafe. The 1,540 are structures nobody has recently checked. Some are sound and some are not, and the survey cannot say which. Calling them unsafe would be wrong, and would alarm people who cross them every day.

The costs are self-reported, and the range is wide: A$15,000 ÷ A$8,000 = 1.875, so the dearest is almost twice the cheapest. Councils may have reported a visual inspection or a full load rating, a quote or an invoice, with or without travel to a remote site. In a survey about possible funding, they also had a reason to aim high. At A$8,000 each, assessing respondents' 1,540 unassessed structures would cost 1,540 × A$8,000 = A$12.32 million; at A$15,000, 1,540 × A$15,000 = A$23.1 million. A range that wide is a starting point, not a unit rate. Chapter 5 builds a rate from engineering firms' indicative rates and councils' recent invoices instead.

Reasons, too, are reported, not proven, and the roundtables were the chance to test them.

## Tom's estimate and its assumptions

This is an illustration. The survey counted limits but did not price them, so Tom estimated what the precautionary limits cost heavy vehicle operators in extra distance and time.

```figure
type: formula
title: Tom's estimate of what the precautionary limits cost
formula: Yearly cost = heavy vehicle trips diverted a year × extra kilometres a trip × cost per extra kilometre
step: 150,000 trips × 20 km × A$3.00 a km
step: = A$9,000,000 a year, reported as about A$9 million a year
- Trips diverted | about 150,000 heavy vehicle trips a year at the 410 limited bridges
- Extra kilometres | 20 km a trip, the average signed detour councils reported
- Cost per extra kilometre | A$3.00, covering vehicle running costs and driver time
caption: This is an illustration. Each input is an assumption Tom stated, sourced and tested. The estimate counts only the limits that responding councils reported.
```

Tom recorded each input's source. The 20 km is the average signed detour in the survey. The 150,000 trips came from heavy vehicle counts some councils supplied for their limited bridges, applied to the rest and checked against operators' figures at the freight roundtable on Wed 22 Sep 2027. The A$3.00 a kilometre came from operators at the same roundtable.

He then changed each input on its own. With 15 km detours instead of 20, the cost is 150,000 × 15 × A$3.00 = A$6.75 million; with 25 km, A$11.25 million. At A$2.50 a kilometre it is 150,000 × 20 × A$2.50 = A$7.5 million; at A$3.50, A$10.5 million. He reported "about A$9 million a year", never a precise figure, with these tests beside it.

He also listed what the estimate leaves out: limits in the 46 councils that did not respond, the 37 closures, costs to light vehicles and other road users, and the safety risk of unsafe bridges left open. Operators said that on some routes they split loads across smaller trucks rather than detour, which the formula does not count. Most omissions would push the true cost up. The inputs from operators, who gain if limits are lifted, could push it the other way. Tom said both.

An estimate with stated assumptions is evidence; without them it is an assertion. Chapter 4 uses this one, assumptions attached, to value the benefit of easing limits.

## Designing consultation that can change the advice

Consultation is worth doing when it can change something: the problem, the evidence, the options or the design. If nothing it hears could change the advice, it is not consultation, and people can tell. Four questions shape it.

This is an illustration. Grace's answers for the bridge problem follow each question.

**Who.** Start from the problem statement: who is affected, who holds evidence, who would deliver and who could block. Then look for who is usually missing: here, small councils without engineers.

**When.** Early enough to change the options, late enough to test something concrete. Grace's last roundtable was just over three weeks before the options paper was due.

**How.** A survey gives breadth: the same questions to everyone. A roundtable gives depth: people explain, disagree and correct each other. Grace held three roundtables, because groups with different interests speak more freely apart: freight operators and industry bodies on Wed 22 Sep 2027, councils and their associations on Thu 30 Sep, and state and territory road agencies on Wed 6 Oct.

**What you will do with it.** Decide before you start what is open, and tell people. The invitations asked for views on the problem, the evidence, the causes and the kinds of response that might help. They also said that NRIO would advise, the Minister would decide, and NRIO could not discuss its advice or promise funding.

The roundtables changed the advice. At the councils' roundtable, several councils said they could not get an engineer to quote for a single bridge at any price. That made the shortage of engineers a cause in its own right, which matters for any option relying on councils buying assessments one at a time. At the road agencies' roundtable, discussion turned on recording results in one consistent form, an idea that reappears among the options in Chapter 3.

The problem statement that went into the options paper read: "Many councils do not know the safe load of their bridges, because assessments are costly and bridge engineers are scarce. They respond with precautionary limits and closures that impose detour costs and may hide real safety risks." The hedge "appear to" had gone, because the survey had counted the limits. Engineers stood beside cost as a cause. Poor asset data was folded into "do not know", because a council cannot know the safe load of a bridge it has no record of assessing.

## Recording and reporting back

This is an illustration. Amara kept one consultation record for the whole exercise. For each roundtable it shows who attended, the main points, where participants disagreed, any data offered and what NRIO did with it. She wrote each note the same day and sent participants a summary within two weeks, inviting corrections. One operator corrected a figure, and Tom's inputs reflect it.

Once the options paper had gone to the Minister, and with the Minister's office told first, Amara sent all 142 councils, respondents or not, and every roundtable participant a short summary of the survey results, the main themes and what would happen next. It left out the options and NRIO's advice, which were for the Minister: an APS employee must keep appropriate confidentiality about dealings with ministers and their staff (Public Service Act, section 13(6)).

Reporting back shows people their time was used, catches mistakes before they harden into advice, and makes the next consultation easier.

## The APS Values behind the work

Section 10 of the [Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) sets the APS Values, and section 13(11)(a) says an APS employee "must at all times behave in a way that upholds" them. Section 12(1) of the [Australian Public Service Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) says "The APS must incorporate and uphold the APS Values". Each Value's section then lists what upholding it requires, having regard to an individual's duties and responsibilities.

Section 10(5) of the Act, Impartial, reads: "The APS is apolitical and provides the Government with advice that is frank, honest, timely and based on the best available evidence." Two of its words pull against each other. Timely means the advice arrives when the decision is made, not when the evidence is perfect. Best available means using what can be had in that time, and saying what is missing.

Three provisions of the Directions bear directly on this chapter:

- Section 17(d), for Impartial: "understanding the needs of the Government and providing it with the best objective, non-partisan advice based on the best evidence available". Objective advice starts with evidence gathered fairly.
- Section 13(e), for Committed to Service: "managing data to enhance evidence-based policy advice, ensuring information is readily available to the community". In practice: data defined, documented and kept where others can find it.
- Section 15(f), for Respectful: "collaborating and being open to ideas in policy development, implementation, program management and regulation". Openness is tested when an idea is inconvenient.

This is an illustration. In the bridge work, that meant asking all 142 councils, not only letter writers; documenting the survey data and reporting it back; heeding the councils' point about engineers; and saying plainly what the evidence could not show.

These are requirements, not better practice, scaled to each person's role. None makes consultation a vote: the advice remains NRIO's, and the decision the Minister's.

## When a proposal needs a legislative instrument

Some proposals take effect through a legislative instrument, such as a rule or determination made under an Act. Then the [Legislation Act 2003](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A01224/latest/text) adds duties about consultation. This is an illustration. The bridge proposal, as it develops in this book, needs no new legislative instrument, so they do not apply to it.

The provisions use all three verbs. Section 17's heading says "Rule-makers should consult before making legislative instruments". The duty is firmer: under section 17(1), "the rule-maker must be satisfied that there has been undertaken any consultation that is" appropriate in the rule-maker's view and reasonably practicable. Section 17(2) lists matters the rule-maker "may have regard to", such as whether the consultation drew on relevant expertise and gave people likely to be affected an adequate opportunity to comment.

Under section 15J(2), the instrument's explanatory statement must, if consultation was undertaken under section 17, "contain a description of the nature of that consultation" (paragraph (d)); if not, it must "explain why no such consultation was undertaken" (paragraph (e)). Section 19 says "The fact that consultation does not occur does not affect the validity or enforceability of a legislative instrument." That protects validity; it does not excuse the explanation.

```figure
caption: General guidance on the Legislation Act 2003, sections 15J, 17 and 19. Your legal area and your agency's procedures add the detail.
- Find out early whether it needs one | ask your legal area; the answer changes the formal duties
- Consult so the rule-maker can be satisfied | s 17(1): consultation that is appropriate and reasonably practicable

- Keep a record the explanatory statement can draw on | s 15J(2)(d) requires a description of the consultation
- x Read section 19 as permission to skip consultation | it preserves validity, but s 15J(2)(e) still requires an explanation

```

## When it goes wrong: consulting after the decision

This is an illustration. From Mon 2 Feb to Fri 13 Mar 2026, another NRIO branch asked councils to comment on a new template for reporting the condition of assets that NRIO programs had funded. But the online reporting system had already been built to the draft template, and its contract signed, before the consultation opened.

Councils sent 52 submissions. Of these, 31, or 31 ÷ 52 = 59.6 per cent, said their asset systems did not record condition on the template's five-point scale, and several small councils said they had nobody to complete it. Neither problem could be fixed without rebuilding the system. NRIO's reply thanked councils and said their comments would be considered for future versions.

The cost came in three parts. Many councils guessed at the scale, so the first year's data could not be compared. The form was rebuilt in the first half of 2027 at a cost of A$140,000. And councils learned that NRIO's consultations might not be real: when Amara rang councils about the bridge survey in August 2027, several mentioned the template.

The controls are simple. Before consulting, write down what is open to change, and do not sign, build or announce those parts until the consultation has been analysed. If nothing is open, do not call it consultation: say what has been decided and why. Then report back on each main issue, saying what changed and why the rest did not.

## Exercise: plan the consultation

This is an illustration. It is Mon 16 Aug 2027. Grace's brief has gone to the Minister, the survey is due to open on Mon 23 Aug, and the options paper must reach the Minister by Fri 29 Oct. Grace asks Amara for a one-page consultation plan.

Write it: for each group, what NRIO needs to learn, the method and the timing; what is open and what is not; a minimum survey response and a check on non-respondents; how long the survey runs, and the time left after the last roundtable if clearance takes a week; and how NRIO will record and report back.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

| Group | What NRIO needs to learn | Method | When |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| All 142 councils | Structures, last assessment, limits, closures, detours, costs, reasons | Online survey, tried on three councils first, with reminders and phone follow-up | Mon 23 Aug to Fri 17 Sep |
| Freight operators and industry bodies | Where limits bite, trip numbers, detour costs | Roundtable, with a request for figures beforehand | Wed 22 Sep |
| Councils and their associations | Whether the survey findings ring true, and what would help | Roundtable | Thu 30 Sep |
| State and territory road agencies | How results could fit their roles and data, in general terms | Roundtable | Wed 6 Oct |

The survey comes first so the roundtables can test it, and operators come early because their figures feed Tom's estimate.

**Open and not open.** Open: the problem, the evidence, the causes and the kinds of response that might help. Not open: NRIO's advice, the Minister's decision and any funding. Every invitation says so.

**Survey response.** A minimum of 60 per cent is 0.60 × 142 = 85.2, rounded up to 86 councils. An aim of 70 per cent is 0.70 × 142 = 99.4, rounded up to 100. Reminders go out, and councils that have not answered by the last week get a call. In the event 96 responded: 96 − 86 = 10 above the minimum and 100 − 96 = 4 short of the aim. To check bias, compare the 46 non-respondents with respondents on what NRIO already knows.

**Timing.** Mon 23 Aug to Fri 17 Sep is 26 days, of which 20 are weekdays: four working weeks. From the survey's close to the options paper is 42 days. From the last roundtable on Wed 6 Oct to Fri 29 Oct is 23 days. With a week for clearance, the draft must be finished by Fri 22 Oct, leaving 22 − 6 = 16 days to absorb the last roundtable and write.

**Record and report back.** One consultation record, with a column for what NRIO did with each point; same-day notes and a summary to participants within two weeks; and, after the options paper reaches the Minister and the office has been told, a summary for all 142 councils and every participant, without the options or the advice.

Its most important line is what is not open: a promise NRIO can keep.
