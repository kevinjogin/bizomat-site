For most businesses, the internet is now where customers first look. Before calling, visiting or requesting a quote, people search online, check websites, read reviews and judge whether a business looks credible. A business without a professional digital presence risks being invisible, or looking less trustworthy than competitors.

Many small business owners rely entirely on social media pages or online marketplaces. Those platforms are valuable, but they are rented land. Building a business's digital identity on them alone carries real risks. This article explains why owning your website and domain matters, how to choose and protect domain names, the main hosting options, why HTTPS security is essential, how to back up properly, why professional email matters, and how to work with web developers and other partners without losing control of your digital assets.

## Rented land and owned land

Social media platforms and online marketplaces are like rented property. They can change their rules, algorithms, fees or features at any time. Accounts can be suspended, sometimes by mistake, and reach can fall overnight. You do not own the audience or the data.

A website on your own domain is like owning your home. You control the content, design, information and customer experience, and it cannot be taken away by a platform's decision. The best approach for most businesses is to use social media and marketplaces to reach people, while directing them to an owned website where you control the relationship.

## Your domain is your online identity

A domain name tells people who you are, what you do and how to find you. It appears on your website, email addresses, business cards, vehicles and advertising. Choose and protect it carefully.

### Choosing a domain name

- **Brand name domains** suit most businesses. Customers searching for your business by name should find it easily.
- **Descriptive or generic domains**, such as names describing the product or service, can help with search visibility for high-volume transactional businesses, but they are harder to protect as brands and are often already taken.
- **Personal name domains** suit professionals and personal brands.

Keep the name short, easy to spell and easy to say, and avoid hyphens and numbers unless they are part of your brand.

### Choosing extensions

Common options for Australian businesses include:

- **.com.au**: widely recognised for Australian businesses.
- **.au**: shorter direct names, available in Australia since 2022.
- **.com**: global and widely used.

Registering several relevant extensions for your main name protects your brand and prevents competitors or opportunists from registering them. Redirect the extra domains to your main website. Australian domain names have eligibility rules administered under the .au domain administration framework. Registrants generally need an Australian presence, such as an ABN or ACN, or Australian citizenship or residency.

### Register domains in the business's name, on its own account

A surprisingly common and costly mistake is letting an employee, developer or contractor register the business's domain in their own name or on their own account, perhaps using their credit card for convenience. The person who controls the registration effectively controls the domain. If they leave, fall out with the business or simply lose access, the business can lose its website and email, and recovering the domain can involve lengthy disputes.

Good practice:

- Register domains in the business's legal name, on an account the business controls.
- Use a business email address and business payment method.
- Give developers delegated access rather than ownership.
- Keep login details in a secure business password manager, with more than one authorised person.
- Turn on auto-renewal and multi-factor authentication.
- Keep contact details current, so renewal notices reach you.

## Hosting: where your website lives

Website hosting is the service that stores your website and delivers it to visitors. Options include:

- **Website builders**, all-in-one platforms where design, hosting and security are bundled. These are easy to use and suit many small businesses, though they offer less flexibility.
- **Shared hosting**: your website shares a server with many others. It is inexpensive and adequate for small, low-traffic sites, but performance can suffer when other sites are busy.
- **Virtual private servers and cloud hosting**: dedicated resources within shared infrastructure, with more performance and control.
- **Dedicated servers**: a whole server for your site. They suit very high-traffic or specialised needs, but are expensive and require technical management.
- **Static site hosting and content delivery networks**: for websites that do not need complex server-side processing, pre-built pages delivered from a global network can be extremely fast, secure and inexpensive.

Choose based on traffic, functionality, budget and technical capability. As traffic grows, upgrade before slow loading drives visitors away. Visitors abandon slow websites quickly, especially on mobile phones. Ask customers about their experience, and test your site's speed regularly.

## Security: HTTPS is essential

**HTTPS** encrypts data travelling between your website and visitors' browsers, protecting information such as contact form submissions, logins and payments. It uses TLS certificates (often still called SSL certificates).

Without HTTPS:

- Browsers mark the site as "not secure", which deters visitors.
- Search engines may rank it lower.
- Information submitted through forms can be intercepted.

HTTPS is now standard and often free. Many hosts provide certificates automatically, and free certificate authorities make it accessible to everyone. Note that HTTPS protects the connection. It does not prove that a website is legitimate, and the rest of the site still needs to be kept secure.

Other security basics:

- Keep website software, themes and plugins updated.
- Use strong passwords and multi-factor authentication for administrator accounts.
- Remove unused plugins and accounts.
- Use reputable hosting with security monitoring.
- Collect only the personal information you need, and publish a clear privacy policy.

## Backups: your website's lifeline

Many owners assume their website is automatically backed up. Sometimes it is, sometimes not, and sometimes backups are kept only for a short time or stored alongside the website, so they are lost in the same incident.

- **Confirm what your host backs up**, how often and for how long.
- **Keep your own backups**, at least monthly and after significant changes.
- **Store backups separately** from the website.
- **Test restoring** occasionally.
- **Back up content you create elsewhere**, such as product images, documents and blog posts.

Losing months of website content and customer enquiries because no one had a recent backup is a painful and avoidable mistake.

## Professional email on your domain

Using a free personal email address for business can make customers question whether a business is established and credible. An email address on your own domain, such as name@yourbusiness.com.au:

- Looks professional and builds trust.
- Reinforces your brand with every message.
- Keeps business communication under business control, so when staff leave, their mailboxes stay with the business.

Set up email authentication records, known as SPF, DKIM and DMARC, through your domain settings. They help prevent criminals from sending email that pretends to come from your domain, and improve the chance that your emails reach customers' inboxes rather than spam folders.

## What a small business website needs

For most small businesses, especially service and business-to-business firms, a website should include:

- **A clear statement** of what you do, for whom and where.
- **Services or products**, with enough detail for customers to decide whether you fit.
- **Evidence**: case studies, project examples, testimonials and credentials.
- **About**: who you are and why customers should trust you.
- **Clear calls to action**: a contact form, quote request, phone number or booking option.
- **Contact information** and service area.
- **Mobile-friendly design**, fast loading and accessibility for people with disabilities.
- **Privacy policy and terms**, appropriate to what you collect and offer.
- **Useful content**, such as articles, guides and answers to common questions, which builds trust and search visibility.

## Working with partners

Web developers, designers, hosting resellers and digital agencies can help, particularly for small businesses without technical staff. Many platforms work through networks of resellers and developers who serve small businesses locally. When engaging partners:

- **Retain ownership** of your domain, website content, design files and accounts.
- **Get access credentials** for everything, stored securely.
- **Agree in writing** who is responsible for updates, security, backups and support, and what it costs.
- **Ask how you would move** to another provider if needed, and avoid arrangements that lock you in.

## Accessibility and inclusion

An accessible website works for people with disability, including those using screen readers, keyboard navigation, captions or larger text, and it usually works better for everyone. Practical basics include sufficient colour contrast, descriptive link text, alternative text for meaningful images, captions for videos, logical headings, forms that can be completed with a keyboard and text that can be enlarged without breaking the layout. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) provide a widely used international standard, and accessibility can also be a legal consideration under Australian disability discrimination law.

## Reaching customers in their language

Businesses serving diverse communities can reach more customers by offering key information in the languages their customers use. One large domain and hosting company grew rapidly in India partly by offering its services in several regional languages and focusing on very small businesses. In multicultural markets such as Australia, translated pages or multilingual staff can be a genuine advantage for some businesses.

## Being found: search and local listings

A website only helps if people can find it. Practical steps:

- **Claim and complete your business profiles** on major search and map platforms, with accurate address, hours, service areas, photos and categories.
- **Use clear page titles and descriptions** that describe what you do and where, in the words customers actually search with.
- **Publish useful content** that answers customers' questions. Search engines reward helpful, original content.
- **Keep business details consistent** across your website, directories and social profiles.
- **Encourage reviews** from satisfied customers, and respond to reviews professionally.
- **Make the site fast and mobile-friendly**, which affects both search rankings and customer experience.
- **Measure** visits, enquiries and their sources, using privacy-respecting analytics.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much should a small business website cost?** Costs range from low monthly fees for website builders to significant investments for custom sites with complex features. Focus on what the website needs to achieve, such as enquiries, sales or credibility, and spend accordingly. A simple, fast, well-written site often outperforms an expensive, complicated one.

**Do we need an online shop?** Only if customers want to buy online and your products suit it. Many business-to-business and service firms need clear information and an easy way to request quotes more than a shopping cart.

**How often should the website be updated?** Keep core information, such as services, contacts and prices, accurate at all times, and add new content such as projects, articles and news regularly. An outdated website suggests an inactive business.

**Who should manage the website internally?** Assign one person responsibility for keeping content accurate and coordinating with any developer or host, even in a very small business. Clear ownership prevents broken links, outdated details and missed renewals.

## A worked example

A small engineering consultancy relied on a social media page and a free email address. When a former contractor who had registered its domain years earlier stopped responding, the consultancy discovered it did not control its own domain.

After recovering the domain through the registrar's dispute process, the owner took control of the business's digital foundations. Domains were registered in the company's name on a company account with multi-factor authentication and auto-renewal. A fast, simple website was built, with HTTPS, clear service pages, project examples and a quote form. Professional email was set up on the domain, with authentication records configured. Monthly backups were scheduled and tested. The social media page now directs visitors to the website.

## Summary

Use social media and marketplaces to reach people, but build your digital identity on a website and domain you own. Choose a clear domain name, register relevant extensions and make sure domains are registered in the business's name on accounts it controls. Choose hosting that suits your traffic and needs, secure the site with HTTPS and good maintenance, keep tested backups and use professional email with authentication records. Give customers a clear, fast, mobile-friendly website with evidence and calls to action, and work with partners in ways that keep ownership and control with the business.
