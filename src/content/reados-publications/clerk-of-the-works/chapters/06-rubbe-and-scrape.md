A seven-line poem, headed in the manuscript that preserves it as Chaucer's words to Adam, his own scribe, complains about copying. If Adam ever copies the *Boece* or *Troilus* again, the poet wishes him a scalp disease under his long hair unless he writes more truly to what the poet made. As it is, the poet must go over Adam's work many times a day, to correct it and "rubbe and scrape": to erase the ink from the parchment and write it again. And all of it comes from Adam's negligence and haste.

Scholars disagree about the poem. In 2004 the scholar Linne Mooney identified a London scribe, Adam Pinkhurst, as the copyist of some of the most important early manuscripts of Chaucer's works, and possibly the Adam of the poem. Others have since questioned whether Chaucer wrote the poem at all. Whoever wrote it, it describes a problem every project has: the author makes the original, others make the copies, and errors enter in the copying. This chapter follows one such error on the grandstand.

## Originals and copies

In Chaucer's world, every copy of a text was made by hand, and every copy could differ from its source. The author could correct a copy he saw. He could not correct the copies made from it, or the copies made from those. Errors propagated down the chain, and later readers met them without knowing they were errors.

A construction project is a copying system too. The design engineer makes a drawing. The superintendent issues it to the builder. The builder passes it to subcontractors. The steel fabricator's detailer redraws it as shop drawings, and the workshop cuts steel from those. Each step is a copy, and each copy can be of the wrong revision. Unlike Adam's parchment, a cut steel beam cannot be rubbed and scraped.

## What happened to revision C

This is an illustration. The seating plat drawings show the stepped profile of the raker beams, the sloping steel members that carry the seating rows. Revision B, issued for tender in week −4, showed the constant 300 mm risers. Revision C, issued for construction in week 2, showed the stepped risers from 240 to 305 mm described in Chapter 3. The builder had already sent revision B to its steel fabricator when it priced the job, so that the fabricator could start ordering steel and preparing shop drawings.

The superintendent issued revision C to the builder in week 2 under a transmittal, which the builder acknowledged. The builder filed it in the site office and marked its own copy of revision B superseded. It did not send revision C to the fabricator. The fabricator's detailer drew the raker beam shop drawings from revision B, the builder's project engineer approved them in week 6 without checking the revision they referenced, and the workshop began cutting.

## Detection at the fabricator's shop

The head contract included a hold point: no structural steel could be galvanised until the clerk of works had inspected it at the fabricator's workshop. The hold point existed because galvanising makes rework much harder and dearer, and the society wanted a look at the steel while it could still be changed.

The clerk of works arrived in week 10 with three things: the society's drawing register, showing the current revision of every drawing; a copy of revision C of the seating plat drawings; and a tape measure. The first check of any inspection is the cheapest. Before measuring a single beam, the clerk asked to see the shop drawing for the raker beams and read its title block. It referenced GS-S-03 revision B.

The rest followed quickly. The six completed beams were measured against both revisions. They matched B: every riser step was 300 mm. Under revision C, the front steps should have been 240 to 275 mm and the back steps 285 to 305 mm. Six more beams were still in cutting lists, also from revision B.

```figure
type: stats
title: The cost of the superseded drawing
caption: This is an illustration. Caught before galvanising, the error cost the builder rework on six beams and four working days. Caught after erection, it would have meant replacing the beams on site.
- 6 of 12 | raker beams fabricated to revision B | the other 6 still on cutting lists
- A$1,850 | rework per beam | cut and re-weld the riser seats, re-inspect
- A$11,100 | total rework | borne by the builder under its subcontract
- 4 days | added to fabrication | absorbed in the builder's programme float
```

Under the contract the cost was the builder's: it had received and acknowledged revision C and failed to pass it to its subcontractor. The society paid nothing. The four days were absorbed within the float on the steel activities, so there was no delay claim. But the result depended entirely on the hold point. Without it, the beams would have been galvanised in week 11, delivered in week 12, and erected in week 13. The error would then have been found when the seats would not sit level on the risers, with every beam to come down and the programme for the show at risk.

## Where the control failed, and where it held

The failure had three links, and any one of them, working, would have stopped it.

| Link | What should have happened | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution | builder sends revision C to everyone holding B | sent to the site office only |
| Shop drawing review | reviewer checks the revision referenced in the title block against the register | reviewer checked dimensions, not the revision |
| Superseded copies | revision B copies withdrawn or stamped superseded everywhere | stamped in the site office only |
| * Hold point | clerk of works inspects before galvanising, starting with the revision | held, and caught the error |

The first three were the builder's controls. The fourth was the owner's. That is the general shape of owner-side quality assurance: the contractor is responsible for its own system, and the owner's checks are placed at the points where an undetected failure would be most expensive. A hold point before galvanising, before concrete is poured, before a wall is lined, is the owner's version of Chaucer going over Adam's copy before it leaves the room.

## The drawing register

Chaucer's appointment as controller of customs in 1374 required him to write the rolls of his office in his own hand. The controller's roll was the counter-record: the collectors kept the main accounts, and the controller kept his own independently, so that the two could be compared. On a project, the owner's drawing register is the counter-record for documents. It shows, for every drawing, the current revision, the date of issue, and who it was issued to.

```figure
type: checklist
title: Document controls on the grandstand after week 10
caption: This is an illustration. The first four are the builder's, confirmed in writing at the week 11 site meeting; the last two are the clerk of works'.
- x Distribution list for every drawing | names every holder, including subcontractors; a new revision goes to every name
- x Acknowledgement from every holder | a returned transmittal, not an assumption
- x Shop drawings state the design drawing and revision they are based on | in the title block, checked on review
- x Superseded revisions withdrawn or stamped | everywhere they were issued
- x Owner's register checked against every shop drawing at every hold point | revision first, then dimensions
- x Register published to all parties after each issue | one page, current revisions only
```

The register itself is simple: a table of drawing numbers, titles, current revisions and dates, kept by the clerk of works and updated whenever the superintendent issues anything. Its value is that it is kept by someone who does not draw, does not build and does not fabricate, and so has no reason to assume a copy is current.

The clerk of works published the register as a single page after every issue, listing current revisions only, so that anyone holding a drawing could compare it in seconds. Superseded revisions were kept on a second page for the record, never mixed with the current list. Each Monday the builder's document controller and the clerk of works compared their registers line by line. In the first week of that practice they found two differences, both in the builder's register, both minor. In the remaining weeks they found none. A weekly comparison that finds nothing is not wasted: it is evidence that the two records still agree.

## Failure in the register itself

The register has its own way to fail, and the clerk of works found it two weeks later. The design engineer issued a revision D of one roof drawing by email to the superintendent, who forwarded it to the builder. Nobody sent it to the clerk of works, and the register still showed revision C. At the next hold point, the clerk checked the purlins against revision C, found them different, and was about to raise a nonconformance when the builder's engineer produced revision D.

The lesson is uncomfortable but useful: a counter-record is only independent if it receives every issue. The correction was to make the clerk of works a named recipient of every transmittal from the superintendent, so that the register is updated from the same issue the builder receives, and to start every hold point by confirming the current revision with the superintendent's office before checking anything. Rubbing and scraping a register entry costs nothing; raising a nonconformance against the wrong revision costs trust.

## What the same error costs at each stage

The cost of a superseded drawing depends almost entirely on when it is found. The builder's estimator, asked by the clerk of works for figures for the project's lessons-learned record, priced the same error caught at four points.

```figure

title: Cost of the revision B raker beams, by when the error is found
unit: A$ thousand
max: 60
caption: This is an illustration. The builder's estimates for six beams. The figure at the hold point is the actual rework; the others are what the same error would have cost earlier or later.
- At shop drawing review, week 6 | 0.6
- * At the hold point before galvanising, week 10 | 11.1
- After galvanising, before delivery | 24.0
- After erection, on site | 52.0
```

At shop drawing review, the fix is an hour or two of the detailer's time to redraw six beams: about A$600. At the hold point it is A$11,100 of cutting and re-welding. After galvanising, the coating must be stripped near the welds and the beams re-galvanised, roughly doubling the cost. After erection, the beams must be unbolted, lowered by crane with the seats and handrails removed, reworked or replaced and re-erected, and the programme for the show is at risk: the builder put that at about A$52,000 before any delay costs.

The pattern is familiar from quality work generally, and it is why owners place their few checks where they are. The clerk of works could not be at every shop drawing review. The clerk could make sure there was one hold point before the expensive, irreversible step, and that the first thing checked there was the cheapest: the revision in the title block.

## Haste and negligence

The poem blames two things: Adam's negligence and his haste. Both were present on the grandstand, and neither belonged to one person. The builder's document controller was covering two jobs that month. The project engineer approved the shop drawings on a Friday afternoon to keep the fabricator working. The fabricator started cutting from a set it knew was a tender issue, because the steel had arrived and the workshop was idle. Each decision was reasonable in isolation. Together they produced six wrong beams.

A control system that depends on nobody being rushed will fail on any real project. The controls that held were the ones that did not depend on anyone's care at the moment: a hold point written into the contract and a register kept by someone with no deadline riding on the result.

## Copies the owner makes

The society generated copies of its own. The committee printed the seating plat drawings for a newsletter, the grant body received a set for its records, and the secretary kept a folder of everything issued. None of these were controlled, and none needed to be, as long as nobody built from them. The clerk of works stamped every copy sent outside the project "For information only: not for construction", and the register recorded which copies were controlled and which were not. That distinction would have helped the steel fabricator too: its revision B copy was a tender copy, which should never have been used for construction without a check against the register.

## Exercise: trace a revision through a hold point

In week 15 the clerk of works attends a second hold point at the fabricator, this time for the roof purlins. The shop drawing references roof framing drawing GS-S-07 revision C. The register shows GS-S-07 revision D, issued in week 13, which changed the purlin spacing from 1,200 mm to 1,100 mm over the front two bays to carry a heavier roof sheet. The front two bays are 7.2 m wide each. List what the clerk should check, in order, and work out how many extra purlins revision D needs over the two bays.

**Worked answer.** The clerk should first confirm with the superintendent's office that revision D is the current issue, then check whether the fabricator received revision D and when, then compare the shop drawing against revision D, and only then measure the steel. Over one 7.2 m bay, spacing at 1,200 mm needs 7,200 ÷ 1,200 = 6 spaces and therefore 7 purlin lines; at 1,100 mm, 7,200 ÷ 1,100 = 6.55, which rounds up to 7 spaces and 8 purlin lines, with the spacing set at 7,200 ÷ 7 = 1,029 mm to stay within 1,100 mm. Each bay gains one purlin line, so the two front bays need two extra purlin lines. If the fabricator has cut purlins to revision C, the clerk records the mismatch, the hold point stays closed, and the superintendent directs the correction. The order matters: checking the revision first takes a minute and makes the rest of the inspection meaningful.
