Among the pilgrims is a manciple, the buyer of provisions for one of the inns of court in London where lawyers lived and trained. Chaucer's portrait of him is brief and admiring on the surface. Other buyers, he says, could learn from this man. Whether he paid cash or bought on credit by tally, he timed his purchases so well that he was always ahead and in good shape. He served more than thirty masters, all expert in law, a dozen of them fit to manage a great lord's estates. And this unlearned man's wit, the portrait says with a straight face, outran the wisdom of that whole crowd of learned men: he fooled them all.

The joke is that a man who buys for others and is always ahead is probably ahead at their expense. The portrait captures both halves of owner-side purchasing: the skill of buying well, and the risk of a buyer who knows more than the people paying. This chapter applies both to the one major purchase the agricultural society makes directly: the 480 seats.

## Buying by tally

The Manciple's "by taille" is a reference to the tally stick, the standard record of credit in Chaucer's England. A stick was notched to show the sum owed, then split lengthwise so that buyer and seller each kept half. The notches on the two halves matched only each other, so neither could alter the record without the other noticing. Chaucer received crown money the same way: one record of his time as clerk of the works shows £100 delivered to him by a single tally for works at the Tower.

The tally is a reminder that purchasing is as much about records as about price. A good buyer gets a good deal. A good purchasing system makes sure that the deal, the delivery and the payment can each be checked by someone other than the buyer.

## The seat purchase

This is an illustration. The society decided to buy the seats directly rather than through the builder, to choose the model and colour itself and to save the builder's margin. The budget allows A$86,400 excluding GST. The seats must be on site by week 22 of the 26-week programme, when the builder will install them on the finished steel. The society has two quotes for 480 stadium seats with fixing kits, both meeting the same specification for material, ultraviolet stability and fixing.

On price alone, Supplier B is A$12,000 cheaper, about 14% less. That was the comparison first put to the committee, by a committee member who had obtained both quotes and recommended B.

## Comparing on more than price

The clerk of works asked for the comparison to be redone on the four criteria that mattered to the society, with weights agreed by the committee before the scores were seen. The committee settled on price 40%, lead time 15%, quality 20% and delivery reliability 25%. Reliability was weighted heavily because seats arriving after the show would leave the society with a finished stand nobody could sit in.

The [Supplier Comparison](/tools/supplier-compare/) tool does this calculation. For each supplier it scores price as the lower price divided by that supplier's price, lead time the same way, and quality and reliability as the rating out of 5, all as percentages. It then weights the four scores by the weights entered and reports each supplier's total out of 100. It rejects a supplier rated 2 or below on quality or reliability, whatever its score, and it reports "too close to call" when the totals are within 3 points. It does not model the cost of a late delivery or the value of quality in service; the weights stand in for both.

```figure

```

The weighted result favours Supplier A by a clear margin. The extra A$12,000 buys a shorter lead time, better quality and, above all, a better record of delivering on time. The committee approved Supplier A at A$86,400, which is the figure in the budget.

## Timing the order

The Manciple's real skill, in Chaucer's account, was timing: he watched his purchases so that he was always ahead. For the seats, timing is set by the lead time and the date they are needed, with a margin for the delivery risk the reliability rating describes.

```figure
type: timeline
title: When each supplier's seats would have had to be ordered
caption: This is an illustration. Each order date allows the quoted lead time plus two weeks of margin before the seats are needed on site in week 22.
- Week 10 | latest order date for Supplier B | 70 days, 10 weeks, plus 2 weeks margin
- * Week 14 | latest order date for Supplier A | 42 days, 6 weeks, plus 2 weeks margin
- Week 20 | seats due on site, with margin | both suppliers, if on time
- Week 22 | seat installation starts | builder's programme
- Week 26 | practical completion | contract date
```

Supplier A's shorter lead time gave the society four more weeks before it had to commit. That mattered. In week 12, the design change in Chapter 3 confirmed that the stepped risers needed a different fixing bracket on the front three rows. Because the order had not yet been placed, the bracket change went into the original order at no cost. An order placed with Supplier B in week 10 would have needed amending after it was placed, with a likely charge and delay.

The society placed the order with Supplier A in week 13, with a 30% deposit of A$25,920 excluding GST and the balance due on delivery and inspection. The clerk of works checked the purchase order against the quote, the specification and the revised bracket schedule before it was signed.

## Failure: the buyer with an interest

The first recommendation for Supplier B was not only a price-only comparison. When the clerk of works asked every committee member involved in the purchase to complete a conflict of interest declaration, as the society's own rules required for purchases over A$10,000, the member who had obtained the quotes declared that a family member was a sales agent for Supplier B, paid partly on commission.

Nothing in the recommendation was dishonest. The member believed B was good value and had not thought the relationship relevant. But the situation was the Manciple's: a buyer with knowledge and an interest that the people paying did not have. Had the price-only comparison gone to the committee without the declaration, the society might have chosen B for reasons it could not later explain, and the member's position would have been impossible if B had delivered late.

Detection came from a rule applied to everyone, not from suspicion of anyone. The correction followed the society's procedure:

1. The member's interest was recorded in the minutes.
2. The member took no further part in the seat decision.
3. The comparison was redone by the clerk of works on criteria and weights the committee set before seeing the scores.
4. The decision, the scores and the reasons were minuted together.


## The interface the owner creates by buying direct

Buying the seats directly saved the builder's margin, but it created something the society had not had before: an interface. Under the head contract, everything the builder supplies is the builder's problem until handover. A seat the society supplies is the society's problem until someone agrees otherwise. Who unloads it, where it is stored, who is responsible if it is damaged on site, and what happens to the builder's programme if it arrives late are all questions the head contract answers only if it was written to.

The clerk of works checked the special conditions for an owner-supplied items clause before the order was placed. The draft had one, but it said only that the society would supply seats "in time for installation". The clerk asked the superintendent to issue a direction confirming four points:

1. The seats will be delivered to the builder's laydown area in week 20, and the builder will unload, store and protect them from that date.
2. Damage found at the joint receipt inspection is the society's to resolve with the supplier; damage after receipt is the builder's.
3. The builder installs the seats to the supplier's instructions, and is paid for that work under the contract sum.
4. If the seats arrive late, the builder may claim an extension of time for the delay to installation, and the society, not the builder, bears that risk.

The fourth point is the one owners overlook. Direct buying moves the risk of late delivery from the builder to the owner. With liquidated damages of A$1,500 per working day running against the builder, a late delivery by the society's supplier could have turned into an extension of time that cancelled them. That is a large part of why reliability carried 25% of the weight.

## Deposits and small suppliers

A deposit is money paid before anything is received. If the supplier fails before delivery, the deposit may be lost. The society's A$25,920 deposit was 30% of the order, the supplier's standard term, and the clerk of works treated it as a risk to be managed rather than a formality.

Three simple checks were made before the deposit was paid. The supplier's company registration and business number were confirmed on the public registers. The supplier's bank details were confirmed by phone on a number from the quote, as Chapter 1's payment rules require. And the clerk of works asked for the deposit to buy something identifiable: the supplier confirmed in writing that the society's seats would be moulded in a stated week and set aside, with the society's order number on the cartons. If the supplier had failed after that week, there would at least have been goods to point to. Where the sum at risk is larger, owners commonly ask for a bank guarantee or a deposit held in trust; the society took advice and judged that the size of this deposit did not justify the cost.

## Testing the price against the market

The Manciple's masters had no way to tell whether his prices were good, because they never saw anyone else's. Two quotes tell an owner which of two suppliers is cheaper, not whether either is reasonable. The clerk of works made one more check: the seat supplier for a grandstand completed two years earlier at another showground had published its tender result in the council's minutes. Adjusted for two years of price movement at the rate the supplier's own price list showed, that price was within a few per cent of Supplier A's. It was not proof, but it was enough to show the committee that A$180 a seat was a market price and not a premium.

## Receiving what was bought

The Manciple's masters could not see his accounts, so they could not tell what they had paid for and what they had received. The last control in a purchase is the receipt. When the seats arrived in week 20, the clerk of works and the builder's leading hand counted them against the delivery docket and the purchase order, opened ten cartons chosen at random to check for damage and correct fixing kits, and checked the brackets for the front three rows against the revised schedule. The receipt was signed by both, with photographs, before the balance was paid.

One carton of 12 seats had cracked backs from handling. The receipt recorded it, the supplier replaced the carton within a week at its own cost, and the balance was paid less the value of that carton until the replacements arrived. Without the receipt, the cracked seats would have been found during installation, with no evidence of whether the damage happened in transit or on site.

## Exercise: rescore with different weights

A committee member argues that the society is a community organisation spending public grant money, so price should carry 60% and the other three criteria 40% between them: lead time 10%, quality 15% and reliability 15%. Rescore both suppliers with those weights, say whether the tool would call a winner, and explain what the committee should consider before adopting the new weights.

**Worked answer.** Supplier A scores (60 × 86.1 + 10 × 100 + 15 × 80 + 15 × 80) ÷ 100 = 51.66 + 10 + 12 + 12 = 85.7. Supplier B scores (60 × 100 + 10 × 60 + 15 × 60 + 15 × 60) ÷ 100 = 60 + 6 + 9 + 9 = 84.0. The difference is 1.7 points, inside the tool's 3-point band, so it would report too close to call. Even with price weighted at 60%, B does not win clearly, because its lower ratings on the other three criteria cost it almost as much as its lower price gains. Before adopting new weights, the committee should note that the weights were meant to be set before the scores were seen; changing them afterwards to favour a result defeats the purpose. It should also ask what a late delivery would cost: if B's seats arrived after the show, the society would hire temporary seating at a cost likely to exceed the A$12,000 saved. If price truly matters more, the honest course is to say so before the next purchase, not to adjust the weights on this one.
