A minister cannot choose an option that nobody wrote down. The options paper is where policy advice either opens a real choice or quietly closes it. A paper with one serious option and two decoys has made the decision for the minister, and a careful reader will notice.

This is an illustration. By Wed 6 Oct 2027, Grace Mitchell's team in the Strategy and Policy Branch of the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO) had a problem statement, a council survey and three roundtables behind it (Chapters 1 and 2). Grace had promised the Minister an options paper by the end of October. It went on Fri 29 Oct 2027 and recommended a central assessment service. This chapter follows how the team got from a blank page to five options, and why one of them became the recommendation.

## Start from the objectives, not the request

Options answer objectives. They do not answer the request that started the work. The Minister's office asked on Tue 3 Aug 2027 for "a fund to fix regional bridges" (Chapter 1). Had the team started there, every option would have been a fund of some size, and the only question would have been how big.

This is an illustration. The problem statement says that many councils do not know the safe load of their bridges, because assessments are costly and bridge engineers are scarce, and that they respond with precautionary limits and closures. Grace's brief set three objectives (Chapter 1). Councils know the safe load of the bridges that matter most for freight and access. Limits and closures that are not needed are lifted or eased, and those that are needed stay. Unsafe bridges are found and acted on. None of these names a solution, and each can be measured.

Test every option against the causes, not just the symptoms. The council survey named three causes: the cost of an assessment, the shortage of bridge engineers and poor asset data. An option that removes one cause leaves the other two in place. Write the causes at the top of your working page and keep them in view while you generate options.

Targets come with options, because a target depends on what an option can deliver. This is an illustration. Options B and C both aim at 1,200 priority bridges, chosen by councils. That is a target, not the whole need: the councils that answered the survey alone reported 1,540 bridges with no load assessment in the last ten years. The paper says so, so that nobody mistakes 1,200 for the end of the problem.

## Describe the base case honestly

The base case is what happens if the government does nothing new. In the bridge paper it is option A: no new Commonwealth action, with councils continuing as now. Every other option is measured against it, so it deserves as much care as the recommendation.

Three errors are common:

- **Freezing the present.** The world keeps moving without the policy. Bridges age, and councils keep assessing a few bridges each year from their own budgets.
- **Painting a disaster.** If the base case is described as catastrophe, any action looks good, and the comparison tells the reader nothing.
- **Leaving it out.** Without a base case, a decision-maker cannot see how much difference an option makes.

This is an illustration. Tom Becker wrote the base case from the evidence. Councils keep paying for some assessments themselves. Most of the 410 precautionary limits and the 37 closures stay in place. Heavy vehicle operators keep paying for detours, which Tom estimated at about A$9 million a year (Chapter 2). Some unsafe bridges may stay open without anyone knowing. The base case costs the Commonwealth nothing, and the paper says so plainly, because nil cost is a real feature of option A.

Keep the base case to the end. It is not an option to screen out; it is the yardstick for all the others.

## Use the full range of instruments

Government has more tools than money. A useful check is to ask what each broad kind of instrument could do about each cause:

- **Information:** standards, guidance, data and education that help people act for themselves.
- **Regulation:** rules, including conditions attached to funding, that require people to act.
- **Spending:** grants, loans, subsidies and capital funds.
- **Service delivery:** government doing the work, or buying it, itself.
- **Doing nothing new:** the base case.

Consultation is a good source of options as well as evidence. Read the notes for ideas, not just for complaints, and ask people what they would do in your place.

This is an illustration. On Tue 12 Oct 2027, Grace ran a half-day options workshop with Tom, Amara Osei and Zoe Fraser. They worked through the roundtable notes and asked what each kind of instrument could do. The idea of recording every result in one consistent form came from the road agencies' roundtable (Chapter 2). The long list had eight options (Figure 1).

Three ideas did not survive the screening described below, and each was recorded with its reason. The funding condition adds a requirement without touching cost or scarcity, and it would weigh most on councils least able to comply. Loans defer the cost of an assessment rather than remove it, and add paperwork for councils and NRIO. Cadetships would ease the shortage of engineers, but only after years; the team noted them as a longer-term idea outside this proposal.

## Combine options where they fit together

Options are not always rivals. Some contain others, and some work only in sequence. Look for three kinds of combination:

- **Nested.** One option includes another. Option C needs a data standard to work, so it does much of what E would do, and more. The paper presents E as a cheaper stand-alone choice and says that choosing C includes its data standard.
- **Sequenced.** One option makes another work. A strengthening fund can only target the right bridges once assessments show which bridges need work. D after C is a coherent package; D before C is a guess.
- **Parallel.** Two options run side by side for different groups, such as grants for councils that want to hire their own engineers and a central service for the rest. A hybrid can suit everyone, but it runs two systems and two sets of rules.

When you combine options, analyse the combination as a whole. Do not add up the benefits of options that overlap; work out what the package achieves against the base case (Chapter 4 shows why).

A staged package also helps the conversation with a minister. "Not now, and here is what would make it ready" is easier to hear than "no". This is an illustration. The paper shows D as a possible later stage, once C's results show which bridges need strengthening.

## Grant against procurement: options B and C

Options B and C look alike at first. Both cost about A$15 million, both cover 1,200 priority bridges, and both pay engineers to assess them. They differ in who buys the work, and that changes almost everything else.

Under B, NRIO pays councils. Each council commissions its own engineer, sets the scope and owns the report. The arrangement serves the council's own objective, knowing the state of its assets, as well as the government's. That is the mark of a grant, and the Commonwealth's grants rules would govern it, from guidelines to acquittal. Under C, NRIO buys engineering services from a panel, sets the scope and the data standard, and holds the results in a shared register. That would be a procurement under the Commonwealth Procurement Rules, even though councils benefit.

The label does not decide which it is; the substance does. "Grants You Can Defend", Chapter 1, sets out the test and why it matters. For a policy officer the point comes earlier. The choice of instrument decides which rules apply, how long delivery takes and who carries the risk, so choose it on purpose and write down why (Figure 2).

B has real strengths, and a fair paper says so. Councils own the bridges and know them best. B respects that, needs no new delivery capability in NRIO and uses a familiar process. C's strengths are consistency and scale: one data standard, a panel that can schedule scarce engineers across the area, and an average price per assessment of A$9,500, at the lower end of the A$8,000 to A$15,000 that councils reported (Chapter 5 shows how it was built).

The evidence from Chapter 2 weighs here. At the councils' roundtable, several councils said they could not get an engineer to quote for a single bridge at any price. B would put up to 142 councils into that queue at once.

C's main risk is NRIO's own: it has never run a service like this. The paper names that risk and says how it would be managed (Chapter 9). Whichever way you lean, ask your legal area early about the legal authority for the spending, and ask your grants and procurement areas how long each route takes.

## Screen against criteria set in advance

Screening narrows a long list to a shortlist that deserves full analysis. Agree the criteria before anyone scores anything, and agree them with the person who will sign the paper. Criteria chosen after the options tend to favour the option someone already likes.

Four or five criteria are usually enough. One source is the [Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) (PGPA Act), which defines "proper" use of public resources as "efficient, effective, economical and ethical" (s 8). Another is the [Australian Public Service Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) (the Directions): upholding the APS Value of Impartial requires advice that "takes account of the context in which policy needs to be implemented" (s 17(f)). An option that cannot be delivered is not really an option.

This is an illustration. Before the workshop, Grace agreed four criteria with the Director, Strategy and Policy:

1. **Effectiveness:** does the option address the three causes?
2. **Cost:** is the cost to the Commonwealth in proportion to the problem?
3. **Feasibility:** can it be delivered within four years, given the shortage of engineers and councils' capacity?
4. **Fairness:** does it reach the councils least able to act for themselves?

Rate each option as meets, partly meets or does not meet, and give a sentence of reasons for each rating. A rating without a reason cannot be checked. Do not add the ratings up: one "does not meet" on effectiveness can rule an option out, however cheap it is.

The shortlist goes to full impact analysis (Chapter 4). Options screened out appear in the paper in a line each, with the reason, so the Minister can see that they were considered.

Take care with an option the decision-maker has already asked about. The office's first idea was a fund to fix bridges, which is option D. Screening it out quietly would look like the team dodging the request. Keep it in, analyse it as carefully as any other option, and let the analysis show its weakness.

## Present options fairly

An options paper is fair when a supporter of each option would recognise it. Upholding the APS Value of Impartial requires advice that is "relevant and comprehensive, is not affected by fear of consequences, and does not withhold important facts or bad news" (Directions s 17(e)). That applies to the options you do not recommend as much as to the one you do (Figure 4).

```figure
type: checklist
title: Is the options paper fair?
caption: Ask a colleague outside the team to read the paper against this list before it goes.
- The same structure for every option | what it is, how it works on the causes, cost, benefits, risks, who gains and who pays
- Each option at its best | the strongest version, as its supporters would put it
- A base case built from evidence | neither frozen nor a disaster
- Options described before the recommendation | so the reader can disagree with your choice
- Uncertainty stated | which estimates are firm and which are judgement
- Screened-out options listed | one line each, with the reason
- x Decoys | an option included only to make the preferred one look reasonable
- x Uneven effort | one option costed in detail and the others guessed
```

This is an illustration. Amara drafted the paper with one page for each option, in the same order of headings. Tom checked that every option's benefits were measured the same way, against the same base case. D appears at its strongest, including its results over 30 years, because strengthened bridges last longer (Chapter 4). The recommendation for C comes after all five options, with its reasons and its main risk. The senior adviser still pressed for D; Chapter 8 follows that conversation.

## When it goes wrong: a straw man options paper

This is an illustration. In mid-2026, before the bridge work began, another NRIO team was asked for options to give heavy vehicle drivers more places to rest on regional freight routes. The team already favoured a capital grants program for councils to build rest areas, and wrote a paper with three options:

- "do nothing", described as "fatigue crashes will keep rising", with no evidence of a trend;
- the grants program, fully designed and costed at A$60 million;
- the Commonwealth building and running its own network of rest areas, at A$400 million.

Only the middle option could be chosen, and the Minister's office saw that at once. It asked what else had been considered: better information about rest areas that already exist, opening council depots to trucks overnight, or a smaller fund for the worst gaps on the busiest routes. The answer was nothing. The paper went back, the team spent five weeks rebuilding the options, and the office read the team's later papers with far more suspicion.

The cost was more than five weeks. A paper that steers a decision is not frank advice: the Minister was offered a choice that was not a choice. The team's credibility, which every later paper relied on, took longest to repair.

Three controls would have caught it. First, build a long list across every kind of instrument before anyone chooses. Second, write the base case from evidence. Third, have someone outside the team read the paper for fairness, asking of each option whether its supporters would recognise it. A straw man fails that test at once.

## Exercise: screen the five options

This is an illustration. Use Grace's four criteria (effectiveness, cost, feasibility and fairness) to rate options A to E as meets, partly meets or does not meet, with a reason for each rating. The facts you need are in this chapter: the three causes (cost, scarce engineers and poor data), the option costs, and the 142 councils in the program area. Then decide which options go to full impact analysis, and how to treat D.

### Worked answer

| Option | Effectiveness | Cost | Feasibility | Fairness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A Base case | Does not meet: all three causes remain | Meets: nil | Meets: nothing to deliver | Does not meet: councils with least capacity fall further behind |
| B Assessment grants | Partly: removes cost, but not scarcity or the data problem | Meets: A$15.0m for 1,200 bridges | Partly: up to 142 councils commissioning at once compete for scarce engineers | Partly: councils with grant and procurement staff move first |
| C Central assessment service | Meets: lowers cost through scale, plans scarce engineers' work, sets one data standard | Meets: A$14.5m over four years | Partly: NRIO must procure a panel and build a new capability | Meets: councils nominate bridges; no application to write |
| D Strengthening fund | Does not meet: fixes some bridges, but nobody knows which ones need it | Does not meet, for now: A$120m, more than eight times C, before the need is known | Does not meet: cannot be targeted without assessments | Partly: competitive grants favour councils able to write strong bids |
| E Guidance and data standard only | Partly: addresses poor data only | Meets: A$0.8m | Meets: NRIO can do it alone | Does not meet: councils that cannot afford assessments are no better off |

The costs check: A$120m ÷ A$14.5m = 8.3, so D costs more than eight times as much as C.

Reading the ratings, not adding them:

- **C** is the only option that meets both effectiveness and fairness. Its one partial rating, feasibility, is a risk to manage, not a reason to stop. It goes to full analysis as the likely recommendation.
- **B** partly meets three criteria and fully meets one. It is the natural alternative to C, and the comparison between them is the heart of the paper, so it goes forward.
- **E** is cheap and feasible but works on only one cause. It goes forward as the low-cost choice, with a note that C includes its data standard.
- **D** does not meet three criteria today. It still goes forward, because it answers the commission's framing and the Minister is entitled to see it analysed rather than dismissed. The paper presents it as premature and as a possible later stage after C.
- **A** is not screened at all. It stays as the base case against which the other four are measured.

So all five reach the options paper, for different reasons, and the reasons are written down. The three ideas screened out of the long list appear in a line each.

Chapter 4 puts numbers on the shortlist: present values, benefit–cost ratios and the tests that show how far to trust them.
