## About this book

Most welding is still done by hand, and much of it should be. But a growing share of repeated welding, in trailers, frames, brackets, tanks, pipe and every kind of manufactured steel product, is done with the help of machines: wire feeders that keep the arc fed, carriages that carry the torch at a steady speed, positioners that turn the work, and robots that make the whole weld. Each step takes some part of the work out of the welder's hands and makes it repeatable. Each also costs money, demands better parts, and changes who does what in the shop. Deciding which steps pay is one of the most consequential decisions a fabricator makes.

This book is for fabrication managers, welding engineers and owners who are deciding what to mechanise or automate. It assumes a working knowledge of welding. It explains, with numbers, the levels of automation, the self-regulating arc that made wire feeding work, how travel speed governs heat and weld size, why automation needs repeatable parts and fixtures, how a robot cell pays back, how programming and batch size change the sums, how sensors and monitoring keep automated welds right, how cells are guarded, and what people and skills an automation plan needs.

## Early manuals as a starting point

Welding automation is older than most people think. Hamilton and Oberg's *Electric Welding* of 1918 described a semi-automatic resistance welding machine on which one operator welded the tangs of garden rakes to their backs at several thousand welds in a day, and automatic machines that made most of the chain then produced. H. A. Hornor's *Spot and Arc Welding* of 1920 quoted Harry D. Morton, an inventor of automatic arc welding machines, explaining how an arc fed with wire at a constant rate keeps its own length steady within limits, and how Morton widened those limits with controls governed by the arc itself. Morton reported welding several times faster than by hand. The Davis-Bournonville course of 1919 described machines that welded whole pipe joints with a ring of flame.

Their observations, on equilibrium between wire feed and burn-off, on the effect of a few degrees of torch angle, on how much faster and steadier a machine could be, are the foundations of modern welding automation. Each chapter takes one of them and works it through for a modern fabricator.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A fabricator in a regional manufacturing town makes trailer chassis, agricultural implement frames and a range of brackets and small weldments for local manufacturers, with about twenty welders on manual and semi-automatic gas metal arc welding. Its owner is considering mechanised carriages for long seams, positioners for the larger frames, and a robot welding cell for the brackets, and wants to know which will pay, what they will demand of the shop, and how to bring the welders along.

The fabricator, its products, customers, prices and results are invented for teaching. They are not BIZOMAT work. Robot cells must be designed, installed and guarded to the robot standards and the work health and safety laws, by people competent to do so.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 sets out the levels of automation. Chapter 2 explains the self-regulating arc, and Chapter 3 travel speed and heat input. Chapter 4 covers fixtures, positioners and fit-up. Chapter 5 works out the payback of a robot cell, and Chapter 6 the effect of programming and batch size. Chapter 7 deals with sensing and adaptive control, Chapter 8 with monitoring and quality data, Chapter 9 with guarding and safety, and Chapter 10 with people, skills and the automation plan. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Its prices, rates and paybacks are illustrations; real projects need quotations, trials on the actual parts and the advice of competent integrators. Robot systems are guarded and validated to the robot safety standards and the work health and safety laws, and welding procedures for automated welding are qualified to the welding standards that apply to the product.
