Identifying an excellent business is only half the problem. The other half is the price. An outstanding company bought at an extreme price can still deliver a poor result for years.

The final chapters of *Warren Buffett and the Interpretation of Financial Statements* deal with timing: when Buffett has tended to buy businesses with durable advantages, and the rare circumstances in which he has sold. The common thread is patience.

## Why great businesses are rarely cheap

Businesses with durable advantages are widely recognised, so they seldom trade at bargain prices by traditional standards. Investors following the strict bargain-hunting approach of Benjamin Graham often never own them, because they always look expensive.

The book's argument is that this is a mistake. For a business whose earnings keep rising, a fair price can produce an excellent long-term result. But "fair" still has limits. The lower the price relative to earnings, the higher the long-run return.

## When the price tends to be right

The book describes two kinds of moments when good prices appear.

**Broad market downturns.** In a bear market, nearly everything falls, including the strongest businesses. Their shares may still look expensive compared with weaker companies that have fallen further, but over the long run the stronger business is usually the better purchase.

**A one-time, solvable problem.** Sometimes an excellent company makes a mistake: a product launch fails, a division struggles, a lawsuit or scandal hits the headlines. The share price falls sharply. If the problem is genuinely solvable and the underlying advantage is intact, the market's reaction can create an opportunity. The emphasis is on *solvable*. A problem that damages the advantage itself is a different matter.

The book also identifies when to stay away: at the height of a boom, when even excellent businesses trade at historically high prices relative to their earnings.

## When selling makes sense

In this approach, a business with a durable advantage is ideally held indefinitely. Every sale ends the compounding and usually triggers tax on the gain. Still, the book describes three situations where selling can be right:

1. **A clearly better opportunity appears**, and money is needed to take it.
2. **The advantage is weakening.** Industries change. The book gives the example of newspapers and broadcasters, whose positions were strong for decades and then eroded as the internet changed how people get news and advertising.
3. **The price becomes extreme.** In a frenzy, shares can trade far above anything the business's earnings can justify. The book suggests that a price of around **40 times earnings** for even an excellent business may be a signal to sell, and that the proceeds should then wait in safe assets for the next downturn rather than chase other expensive shares.

## The arithmetic behind selling at a high price

The book frames the sell decision as a comparison. Suppose a business is expected to earn a certain amount over the next twenty years, and someone offers a price today. Selling makes sense only if that price, reinvested at a realistic rate of return, would grow to more than the business would have produced. When prices are extreme, that comparison can favour selling. When they are reasonable, holding usually wins.

## Patience as the real discipline

All of this asks for a temperament that is easy to describe and hard to practise: waiting, sometimes for years, for the right price; holding through periods when the share price falls; and resisting the urge to trade in and out.

## What this means outside investing

For business owners the same pattern applies to big decisions: buying equipment, acquiring a competitor, signing a long lease, entering a market. A good asset bought at the wrong price, or at the peak of enthusiasm, can burden a business for years. Downturns, by contrast, are often when good assets are available at sensible prices to those who kept cash in reserve.
