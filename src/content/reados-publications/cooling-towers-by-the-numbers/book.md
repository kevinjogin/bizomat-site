## About this book

A cooling tower rejects a plant's waste heat to the atmosphere by evaporating a small fraction of the water passing through it. It is one of the most effective heat-rejection devices there is, able to cool water below the temperature of the air around it, and one of the most misunderstood: its performance depends on the air's humidity, not its temperature; its water use depends on chemistry as much as on heat; and its water, warm, aerated and in contact with the air, can grow the bacteria that cause Legionnaires' disease if it is not managed. Each of those can be understood with a few numbers.

This book works through a cooling tower in the order an engineer or operator meets it: what it does, the moist air it works with, the plant's heat load and the water flow, the tower's performance at different weather, evaporation, drift, blowdown and makeup, water treatment, Legionella control and the law, fans and energy, siting and recirculation, and testing and monitoring. Each chapter ends with an exercise and a worked answer.

It suits plant, facilities and maintenance engineers, operations managers and building owners. It assumes school algebra. *Heat Exchangers by the Numbers* covers the coolers that tower water serves; this book is about the tower.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional installation. This is an illustration. CT-250 is an open, induced-draught counterflow cooling tower serving a manufacturing plant: two hydraulic press oil coolers, two air compressors and a water-cooled chiller for its moulding machines, a total heat load of 250 kW. At a design wet-bulb temperature of 24 °C, it cools about 12 L/s of water from 34 °C to 29 °C. Its weather, loads, water chemistry and every number in the book are illustrations chosen to show the method.

The plant and its numbers are invented; none describes KEVOS® work or a real site. Prices, where they appear, are illustrations in Australian dollars, with GST stated.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Cooling towers are regulated in Australia for public health, and each state and territory sets its own requirements for registration, risk management, maintenance, testing and reporting; the requirements that apply to a real tower are those of its jurisdiction, administered by its health department or local council. Water treatment, Legionella risk management and testing must be carried out by qualified people as the law requires. Tower design and performance testing follow makers' data and recognised codes. Nothing in this book replaces those obligations or that advice.
