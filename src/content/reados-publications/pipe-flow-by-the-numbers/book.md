## About this book

Every factory has pipes full of moving liquid: cooling water to moulding machines and compressors, hot water to wash bays, glycol to chillers, oil to hydraulic power packs, process water to tanks. Most were sized once, by rule of thumb or by a supplier, and are fault-found many times: a machine that runs hot, a pump that cannot deliver, a branch that gets no flow, a pipe that bangs or gurgles. Each of those problems has a number behind it, and the numbers are not hard.

This book works through liquid pipe flow with one running case. It starts with flow, velocity and the energy equation, moves through laminar and turbulent flow, friction in straight pipe and the losses in fittings, builds a system curve, divides the flow between parallel branches and balances them, and then deals with natural circulation, air and two-phase flow, and measuring what the system actually does. Each chapter calculates with the running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for mechanical and maintenance engineers, technicians, plumbers and designers. It assumes school algebra and a calculator or spreadsheet. READOS's Centrifugal Pumps by the Numbers takes the system curve from this book and matches pumps to it; Water Hammer by the Numbers deals with the pressure surges that sudden changes in flow cause; Ductwork by the Numbers applies the same ideas to air.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional system. This is an illustration. A plastics moulding plant cools six injection moulding machines with a water loop called CW-2. Water at 25 °C is pumped from an open 20 m³ tank through a steel supply header of 80.9 mm bore, overhead at 4 m, to the six machines, each taking 1.5 L/s through its mould and oil coolers. It returns through a matching header to the tank, where a separate cooler removes the heat. The design flow is 9.0 L/s, and the water returns at about 32 °C.

The plant, its dimensions, its equipment and its mistakes are invented. None describes KEVOS® work or a real business.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first: they set out the units, the continuity equation and the energy equation, which every later chapter uses. Chapters 3 to 6 build the system curve from friction and fittings; Chapter 7 divides the flow between branches; Chapters 8 to 10 deal with natural circulation, air and measurement. Water properties are taken at the stated temperatures from public data, and every calculation was checked by running it.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

Friction factors, fitting losses and equipment pressure drops vary with the actual pipe, fitting and product; the values marked as illustrations here must be replaced with the manufacturer's or measured data for real work. Hot water, steam, pressurised systems and hazardous liquids bring risks of scalding, bursting and release that are governed by regulation and qualified design. This book teaches the hydraulics. It is not a design for any system, and qualified review governs real work.
