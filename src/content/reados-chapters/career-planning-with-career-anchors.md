Many capable people work hard, gain qualifications and have real ambition, yet feel stuck or unfulfilled in their careers. Often the problem is not effort or ability but direction. They chased whatever job was offered, followed others' expectations or chose roles for salary alone, without understanding what genuinely motivates them, what they are best at, or what kind of organisation suits them.

Good career planning starts with self-knowledge. This article offers three practical tools: Edgar Schein's **career anchors**, which identify the deep motivations that shape career satisfaction; a **ten-point strengths self-assessment**; and a **nine-question framework for choosing an employer**. It finishes with advice on interviews, attitude and handling rejection. These tools help students choosing first jobs, professionals considering their next move and managers helping staff plan their development.

## Career anchors: what you will not give up

MIT professor Edgar Schein developed the concept of **career anchors** from long-term studies of managers' careers. A career anchor is the combination of motivations, values and perceived abilities that a person would be least willing to give up when making career choices. When a role conflicts with someone's anchor, they tend to feel dissatisfied, however well paid. Schein described eight anchors.

### 1. Technical and functional competence

People with this anchor love being expert in a specific field, such as engineering, coding, finance, design or manufacturing. They find satisfaction in mastering their craft and are often uncomfortable being pulled away from it into general management. Someone who spends nights writing software for fun, or who loves solving manufacturing problems, probably has a strong technical anchor.

**Suitable paths**: specialist and technical roles, expert career tracks, technical consulting.

### 2. General managerial competence

People with this anchor want to integrate the work of others and be responsible for overall results across functions. Generalist roles are sometimes seen as less prestigious than specialist ones, but general management means taking responsibility for whole outcomes. For example, a course producer might be responsible not only for one video but for an entire program: production, publishing, launch, marketing and technology.

**Suitable paths**: management roles, general management, business unit leadership.

### 3. Autonomy and independence

People with this anchor value freedom to work in their own way, at their own pace and by their own standards. They chafe under close supervision and rigid rules.

**Suitable paths**: entrepreneurship, consulting, freelancing, or "intrapreneurial" roles in larger organisations that offer significant independence.

### 4. Security and stability

People with this anchor value job security, predictable income and long-term stability. They trade skills, loyalty and time for security, prefer lower-risk choices and are less attracted to entrepreneurship.

**Suitable paths**: established organisations, government, roles with clear structures and long-term prospects. There is nothing wrong with this anchor. It simply points to different choices.

### 5. Entrepreneurial creativity

People with this anchor want to create something of their own: a product, service or business. This is not limited to high-technology ventures. A potter who constantly wonders how to make more interesting products and reach more discerning customers has an entrepreneurial creative anchor. Crowdfunding platforms are full of such creators.

**Suitable paths**: starting businesses, product development, innovation roles.

### 6. Service and dedication to a cause

People with this anchor are driven by values and the desire to make a difference, such as helping people, improving society or protecting the environment. Money matters less than meaningful work.

**Suitable paths**: not-for-profits, healthcare, education, social enterprises, purpose-driven companies.

### 7. Pure challenge

People with this anchor thrive on solving difficult problems and overcoming obstacles. Routine bores them, and they often exceed their own expectations when stretched.

**Suitable paths**: consulting, agencies, innovation and technology roles, competitive environments with varied, demanding work.

### 8. Lifestyle

People with this anchor want a career that fits with family, personal interests and a balanced life. They value predictable hours and time outside work.

**Suitable paths**: organisations with stable, defined roles and good work-life balance. Fast-growing start-ups, where long hours are common, may be a poor fit.

### Using career anchors

Reflect on the choices you have made, the work you enjoyed most and least, and what you would refuse to give up. Most people have one or two dominant anchors. Choose roles and organisations that align with them. Taking a job that conflicts with your anchor purely for money often leads to frustration and departure.

## A ten-point strengths self-assessment

Knowing your strengths helps you set goals, make better decisions and grow faster. Rate yourself from 1 (weak) to 5 (strong) on these ten competencies:

1. **Time management**: do you meet commitments and use time well? In essential services, poor time management is unacceptable, and the same applies to careers.
2. **Persistence**: how often can you bounce back after setbacks? Many successful founders have survived repeated failures, sometimes selling personal assets to pay staff, because their goals were clear.
3. **Communication**: can you explain ideas clearly in speech and writing?
4. **Analytical thinking**: can you break problems down and reason with data? A cricketer who calculates the required run rate and adjusts tactics accordingly is thinking analytically.
5. **Leadership**: do you take ownership of outcomes and get work done through others? Leadership is not reserved for those "born to lead". It grows from ownership.
6. **Teamwork**: can you work effectively with others? Few achievements are solo efforts.
7. **Discipline**: do you follow through consistently, resisting distractions? Disciplined people grow faster.
8. **Risk-taking**: are you willing to take calculated risks? Some start-up cultures see a failed attempt as more valuable experience than never trying. Calculated risk means using your other strengths and advice from mentors, not reckless gambles.
9. **Innovative thinking**: can you solve problems in new ways? Digital payment apps transformed everyday banking because their creators thought differently about customers' needs.
10. **Execution**: do you turn plans into results, end to end, despite obstacles?

Add your scores, giving a total between 10 and 50. Your highest scores show where your strengths lie and where your career should lean. Your lowest scores show gaps to develop, especially if they are foundational, such as discipline or execution, which affect success in any role.

Ask a trusted colleague, manager or mentor to rate you too. Other people's views often reveal strengths and blind spots you cannot see.

## Nine questions for choosing an employer

Graduates often accept the first offer that comes along, sometimes with restrictive bonds or roles unrelated to their skills. Experienced professionals sometimes join impressive brands only to find the reality disappointing. Before accepting an offer, ask:

1. **What problem does the company solve, and do customers pay for it?** Companies that solve important problems customers willingly pay for are more resilient. Some businesses that relied on investor funding rather than customer revenue cut jobs quickly when funding dried up.
2. **What is the culture like?** A famous brand with a toxic culture, characterised by silos, stress and poor treatment of staff, will not sustain your career or wellbeing.
3. **Is it growing, and can it scale?** Growing companies create opportunities for people. Small teams that build scalable products can create enormous value per employee.
4. **Who will you work with?** You join a manager and a team, not just a brand. Is your manager someone you can learn from? Is the team capable and innovative?
5. **Is the business model sound?** Do customers pay for the product, and is the business profitable or on a credible path to profitability?
6. **Is it likely to be around in five to ten years?** Some companies endure for a century, while others close within a few years.
7. **What do reviews say?** Read employee reviews, customer reviews and news coverage. Talk to current and former employees if you can.
8. **Does the role match your skills and anchor?** Taking an unrelated role in the hope of transferring later often leads to frustration.
9. **What is your career path?** Ask how the role could develop over one to three years. If nobody can describe a path, be cautious.

## Preparing for interviews

Senior executives who have recruited many graduates often emphasise a few qualities:

- **Preparation**: build expertise in your field over months, not the night before. Research the company, its products, customers and competitors.
- **Positive attitude**: often valued above knowledge, because it determines how people handle change, colleagues and setbacks.
- **Awareness of trends**: industries, customers and products change quickly. Show that you follow developments in your field.
- **Flexibility and adaptability**: the ability to work in different environments and learn new things.
- **Teamwork**: sport, group projects and volunteering all build teamwork. Recognise that no one is strong at everything, and success comes from combining strengths.

## Handling rejection

Rejection is part of every career. After an unsuccessful interview:

- Do not dwell on negative thoughts such as being unlucky or not good enough.
- Ask for feedback and use it.
- Stay psychologically resilient: knowledge and skills matter, but so does how you handle setbacks.
- Remember that the next opportunity may be a better fit.

Good times and bad times come to every career. How you manage the bad times often determines how quickly the good times return.

## A worked example

A mechanical engineering graduate receives two offers: a well-paid graduate role in a large corporation's commercial team, and a lower-paid design engineering role at a growing product company. Using the frameworks, she recognises that her strongest anchors are technical competence and pure challenge: she loves solving design problems and gets bored with routine. Her strengths assessment shows high scores in analytical thinking, persistence and execution, and a lower score in communication.

She investigates both employers. The product company solves an important problem for paying customers, has a collaborative culture according to current employees, and offers a clear path from graduate engineer to design lead. The corporate role would take her away from engineering. She chooses the design role and enrols in a presentation skills course to address her weaker area. Three years later, she leads a small design team and earns more than she would have in the corporate role.

## Using anchors as a manager

Career anchors are not only for individuals planning their own careers. Managers can use them to keep good people and place them well:

- **Ask about anchors in development conversations.** "What kind of work would you never want to give up?" and "What would make you leave?" reveal anchors quickly.
- **Create dual career paths.** In engineering and technical firms, strong specialists are often promoted into management because it is the only route to higher pay, and the business loses a good engineer and gains a reluctant manager. A senior specialist track lets technical people progress without leaving their craft.
- **Match assignments to anchors.** Give challenge-anchored people the hardest problems, autonomy-anchored people clear outcomes with freedom over method, and security-anchored people stable responsibilities with clear expectations.
- **Respect lifestyle anchors.** Flexible arrangements can keep capable people who would otherwise leave.

## Common mistakes in career planning

- **Choosing for salary alone.** Pay matters, but a role that conflicts with your anchor rarely stays satisfying, and unhappy people seldom perform at their best.
- **Following other people's expectations.** Family, friends and peers may push towards prestigious careers that do not fit you.
- **Ignoring weaknesses that block everything.** Poor discipline or execution undermines strengths in every role.
- **Judging an employer by its brand.** The manager, team and culture you join matter more than the logo.
- **Never reviewing the plan.** Anchors tend to stabilise with experience, but circumstances change. Review your direction every year or two.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can career anchors change?** Schein found anchors tend to become clearer and more stable after several years of work experience, because people learn what they value through real choices. Early in a career, treat your anchor as a hypothesis to test.

**What if I have no strong anchor?** That is common among students and early-career professionals. Try varied work through internships, projects and rotations, and notice what energises you and what drains you.

**Should I avoid jobs that do not match my anchor?** Not always. A short-term role that builds skills or pays the bills can be sensible, provided you know why you took it and plan the next step.

**How often should I reassess my strengths?** Once a year, or after a major change in role. Track whether your development efforts are lifting your weaker scores.

## Summary

Career satisfaction depends on alignment between your work and what genuinely drives you. Use Schein's eight career anchors, namely technical competence, general management, autonomy, security, entrepreneurial creativity, service, pure challenge and lifestyle, to understand your deepest motivations. Assess your strengths across ten competencies, and develop key gaps. Choose employers carefully: examine the problem they solve, culture, growth, people, business model, durability, reviews, role fit and career path. Prepare thoroughly for interviews, cultivate a positive and adaptable attitude, and treat rejection as a step on the way.
