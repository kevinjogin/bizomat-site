Some of the most memorable lines in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* describe a particular kind of reformer. Smith calls him the man of system. He is often wise in his own estimation, and so taken with the imagined beauty of his ideal plan that he cannot bear any part of it to be changed. He seems to think he can arrange the members of a society as easily as a hand arranges the pieces on a chessboard. What he forgets, Smith says, is that on the chessboard the pieces have no principle of motion except the one the hand gives them, while on the great chessboard of human society "every single piece has a principle of motion of its own." Where those principles run with the reformer's plan, things go smoothly; where they run against it, the result is disorder.

Smith had governments in mind, but the man of system is at least as common in businesses, and engineers are well equipped to become him. This chapter follows the job shop owner's complete set of standard work instructions, rolled out across the shop in one go, and what happened next. It rates the change with a change impact calculation, and shows how a pilot worked with the operators' own principles of motion rather than against them.

## A complete system

This is an illustration. Over a quiet summer, the owner writes standard work instructions for the shop's **40** most common recurring job types. Each is a laminated sheet: the set-up sequence, workholding, tools and offsets, cutting data, first-off checks and in-process checks, with photographs. They are good documents, consistent in format and thorough. The owner is proud of them, with reason.

His plan is to introduce all 40 on one Monday, with a short briefing, a sheet at every machine and a rule that the sheet must be followed exactly, with any deviation recorded and approved. The aim, from Chapter 1's restatement of the ends, is to reduce first-off rejects and rework on recurring jobs and to shorten the time for a new operator to run a recurring job unsupervised.

The plan has everything the man of system loves: completeness, consistency and a single moment of transformation. It also treats the operators as pieces whose only motion will be the one the sheets give them.

## Rate the change before making it

Before the rollout, the office manager suggests running the plan through the [change impact assessment tool](/tools/change-impact/) in BIZOMAT Tools. The tool turns five judgements into an impact rating out of 100: the additional cost of the change as a share of the value of the process it changes, the schedule disruption as a share of the time available, and ratings from 1 to 5 for the increase in risk and the effect on quality. It also compares the expected benefit with the added cost, and its checks prompt the user to confirm who can approve the change and to record it in a change register.

For the complete rollout, the office manager estimates:

- **added cost** of about **A$18,000**: a day of briefing and practice for ten people, and the output lost while everyone adjusts at once, against about **A$120,000** a year of current set-up losses and rework on recurring jobs;
- **disruption** of about **15 working days** of slower running across the shop, within a **60-day** quarter;
- a **risk** rating of **4**: forty documents, never trialled, introduced together;
- a **quality** rating of **4**: any error in a sheet would be repeated on every job of that type;
- an expected **benefit** of about **A$30,000** a year from less rework and faster set-ups once the sheets are working.

The tool gives a cost increase of 15%, a schedule increase of 25%, a benefit of A$30,000 against A$18,000 of added cost, and an impact rating of **50/100, moderate**. The benefit exceeds the added cost, but the rating says the change carries real risk and needs approval and a plan for problems.

The owner reads the result as acceptable. A moderate rating, a positive balance of benefit over cost: go ahead.

## The failure case: forty sheets on one Monday

The rollout goes ahead. By Wednesday of the first week, problems have appeared.

- **Errors in the sheets.** Operators find mistakes in **9** of the 40: tool numbers that changed since the summer, an offset that applies to one lathe but not the other, a first-off check that cannot be done with the shop's gauges. Each error is small, and each makes the sheet wrong for every job of that type.
- **Missing knowledge.** Several sheets omit things the operators do without thinking: warming the spindle before a tight-tolerance job, a particular order for tightening a fixture to avoid distortion. Following the sheet exactly gives worse parts than the operators' habit.
- **Quiet reversion.** By the second week, most operators have stopped reading the sheets and returned to their own methods, recording nothing. The sheets stay laminated at the machines, untouched.

First-off rejects on recurring jobs rise in the first two weeks, then return to their old level. The owner's complete system has become decoration.

The owner's first reaction is to blame the operators for not following the sheets. His second, after a frank conversation with the lathe operator, is closer to Smith's. The operators had their own principle of motion: methods learned over years, many of them good, some of them better than the sheets. A plan that ignored those methods, and introduced every change at once so that no error could be caught before it spread, was bound to meet resistance, and much of the resistance was justified.

### How it is detected

The change impact tool's rating was the first warning: risk and quality both at 4, for documents never trialled. The second was data: first-off rejects rising in the first week. The third, and the most telling, was silence: operators who had stopped raising problems because nobody had asked them to help write the sheets in the first place.

### How it is corrected

The owner withdraws the 40 sheets and starts again with a pilot.

## A pilot that works with people

Smith contrasted the man of system with a different kind of reformer: one who respects the established habits and interests of the people affected, tries to persuade rather than force, and, when he cannot make things right all at once, is content to make them better. The pilot is that approach applied to a workshop.

The owner runs the change impact tool again for the pilot: standard work for **five** recurring lathe jobs, co-written with the lathe operator and trialled for a month before any are made standard.

- **Added cost** about **A$6,000**, mostly the operator's and owner's time writing and trialling;
- **disruption** about **5 days**, confined to the lathe;
- **risk** rating **2**: five documents, trialled before adoption;
- **quality** rating **2**: errors found on the first jobs and corrected before the sheet is standard;
- the same eventual **benefit** of about A$30,000 a year once the whole set exists.

The tool gives an impact rating of **23/100, low**, with a benefit of A$30,000 against A$6,000 of added cost.

Two details of the pilot made a large difference. First, each sheet carries the name of the operator who wrote it, not the owner's, and changes to it go through that operator. People defend documents they wrote and ignore documents written about them. Second, the owner's role changes from author to editor: he keeps the format consistent, checks that safety and inspection steps are complete, and asks questions, but the method comes from the machine.

After four months, the shop has **15** standard sheets in use, written by the operators who use them, with the owner's format and checks. First-off rejects on those jobs have fallen noticeably, and Chapter 8 measures by how much. Fifteen sheets that are used are worth far more than forty that are not.

## What the rating does and does not measure

The change impact rating is a structured judgement, not a measurement. Half of it comes from two numbers, cost and schedule, expressed as shares of the process and the time available. The other half comes from two ratings on a 1 to 5 scale, which are opinions. That is not a weakness, provided it is understood: the tool's value is that it makes people state those opinions and see how much they drive the result.

In the complete rollout, the ratings of 4 for risk and quality contributed 40 of the 50 points. The owner, in his enthusiasm, had read the rating's total and ignored its composition. Had he asked why risk and quality were rated so high, the answer would have been the plan itself: forty untested documents introduced at once. A rating is most useful when its largest component is read as a question about the plan, not as a fact about the change.

## When a complete change is right

Not every change can be piloted. A new legal requirement, a customer's mandatory system, a safety hazard that must be removed at once, or a switch between two incompatible systems may force everything to change on one day. Smith's reformer accepted that sometimes a whole change is needed; his objection was to the man of system who prefers it.

When a complete change is unavoidable, the lessons of the failed rollout still apply in a different form. Test the new method somewhere before the day, even on paper or in a single trial run. Involve the people who will use it in finding its errors. Keep a way back for the first days. And expect the first week to go worse than planned, with time set aside to fix what breaks. A forced change done this way is still a change made with people rather than to them.

## Why the operators' motion matters

It would be easy to read this chapter as a lesson about persuasion, getting people to accept a plan. Smith's point is stronger than that. The pieces' own principles of motion are not just resistance to be overcome; they are often knowledge. The operators' habits contained the spindle warm-up, the fixture tightening order and a dozen other details that made parts good. A plan that overrode them lost that knowledge. A plan that started from them kept it and made it visible.

This connects with Chapter 7. Some of what a business does can and should be fixed in precise rules. Much of it cannot, because it depends on judgement that the people doing the work have and the person writing the rules does not. The man of system's error is not writing procedures; it is assuming that a procedure can capture everything, and imposing it as if it did.

## Signs that you may be the man of system

The owner, chastened, writes himself a short list of warning signs, to be read before the next big plan:

- The plan is complete before anyone who will use it has seen it.
- The plan is to be introduced everywhere at once.
- Disagreement is treated as non-compliance.
- The beauty of the plan is described more often than its results.
- Nobody has asked what the current method does well.

None of these makes a plan wrong. Each makes it worth slowing down.

## Exercise: rate a different rollout

The owner proposes introducing the scheduling package from Chapter 5 to every machine and the office on the same day. The office manager estimates added cost of about **A$9,000**, disruption of about **10 days** in a **60-day** quarter, risk **4**, quality **3**, against about **A$120,000** a year of current scheduling losses, and a benefit of about **A$25,000** a year. Using the change impact assessment tool's method, what is the impact rating? How would you change the plan?

**Worked answer.** The cost increase is 9,000 ÷ 120,000 = **7.5%** and the schedule increase is 10 ÷ 60 = **16.7%**. The impact rating is 0.25 × 7.5 + 0.25 × 16.7 + (4 ÷ 5) × 25 + (3 ÷ 5) × 25 = 1.9 + 4.2 + 20 + 15 = about **41/100, moderate**. The benefit of A$25,000 exceeds the added cost of A$9,000.

The rating is dominated by the risk and quality judgements, which is typical of a large change introduced at once. The plan should be changed in the way Chapter 5 already proposed: stage the introduction, running the job list alongside the whiteboard first, then scheduling the lathe alone, then the other machines. Each stage has lower risk and lower quality exposure, so each would rate lower, and problems would be found while the old method still exists as a fall-back.
