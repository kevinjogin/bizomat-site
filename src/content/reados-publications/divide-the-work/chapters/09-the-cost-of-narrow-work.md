Smith's praise of the division of labour comes at the start of *The Wealth of Nations*. His warning comes near the end. In Book V he describes the worker whose whole life is spent performing a few simple operations, always with the same effects, who has no occasion to exercise understanding or invention because no difficulty ever arises. Such a person, he argues, tends to lose the habit of judgement, and with it much of their capacity for conversation, for sound decisions about ordinary life and for the defence of their own interests. His remedy was public education. Rothschild and Sen point out in the *Cambridge Companion* how seriously Smith took this cost: the source of the wealth of nations was, in his own account, not in itself an uplifting sight.

A small engineering business is a long way from an eighteenth-century manufactory, and its stations are far broader than one-eighteenth of a pin. But the warning applies in a practical form. Dividing work creates roles that are narrower than the generalist roles they replace, and narrow roles have costs: fragile cover, lost judgement about the whole product, boredom and turnover, and repetitive strain. This chapter sets out how the bench maker plans against each, and closes with a different kind of division: buying specialist work from another business.

## Cover: every station needs two people

This is an illustration. Chapter 7 described what happened when the polisher was absent for a week: the line stopped. A divided flow is a chain, and any station without cover is a link that can break. The generalist arrangement never had this problem, because anyone could do anything.

The owner draws up a simple skills matrix: stations across the top, people down the side, and in each cell a rating from "cannot do it" to "can do it unsupervised at normal pace". The first version shows the problem plainly. Each station has one person rated fully competent, and the floater is competent only at cutting and folding.

The cover plan sets a rule: **every station has at least two people fully competent at it.** Training time comes from the spare capacity the staffing calculation leaves. The [staffing need tool](/tools/staffing-need/) gave 3.60 full-time equivalents for the contract's workload, so four people leave about 0.4 of a person, roughly **517 productive hours a year**, for cover, training, improvement work and peaks. The owner allocates about 150 of those hours to planned training at other stations, scheduled in quieter weeks.

The fixture and method sheets from Chapters 2 and 4 make this faster than it would otherwise be. The floater, starting welding with the fixture and the welder's written sequence, begins well down the learning curve rather than at the top, and reaches an acceptable pace in weeks rather than months.

### What cover costs, and how to reward it

Cover is not free, and it is better budgeted than hoped for. The 150 hours of planned training a year cost about **A$9,300** at the loaded rate of A$62 an hour, plus some lost output while each trainee is slower than the regular person. Against that, a single week with the line stopped for want of a polisher, as in Chapter 7, lost about fifteen benches of output and strained the relationship with the distributor. The owner treats the training hours as the premium on an insurance policy he has already seen claimed.

People also need a reason to learn a second station, which can feel like extra work for no reward. The owner ties part of each person's pay progression to demonstrated competence across stations, within the applicable award or agreement, and makes cross-skilling part of each person's annual review. In a small business, that link between skills and pay is often the difference between a skills matrix that improves and one that stays as it was drawn.

## Judgement: keeping the whole-product view

The weld shrinkage problem in Chapter 4 showed the second cost of narrow work. The welder, working only on frames, did not see that the frames no longer suited the tops; the folder did not see the frames until they returned for fitting. A generalist, building the whole bench, would have caught it on the first one.

The owner keeps the whole-product view alive with three simple practices:

- **First-off review.** The first bench of each week, and the first of any variant, is followed through every station by the person who started it, who checks how their part fits with the others.
- **Whole-bench builds for custom work.** Custom benches are still built by one person from start to finish, and the people at the stations take turns doing them, which keeps their whole-product skills current.
- **Shared inspection.** Final inspection is done in rotation, so everyone regularly sees finished benches and the faults that reach the end of the line.

These practices cost some efficiency, a few hours a week in total. They buy the judgement that Smith feared narrow work would lose, and they find the kind of fault that only appears when parts from different stations meet.

## Interest, turnover and strain

The polisher who left after seven months, described in Chapter 5, left partly because the role was under-used. But even a fully used narrow role can become tedious, and in a small business, losing one specialist can cost months of learning.

The bench maker's responses are modest and practical. The midday rotation between polishing and cutting, introduced in Chapter 3 for manual-task risk, also breaks up the day. Improvement time, from Chapter 4, gives each person a problem to solve at their own station. Cross-training gives each person a second skill and, with it, more say in how the line runs. None of these turns a station job into a craft career, but together they keep the roles from becoming the kind Smith described.

The physical side also needs attention. A person who polishes stainless steel for most of a day repeats similar movements and postures for hours. Safe Work Australia's model code of practice on hazardous manual tasks treats repetitive and sustained work as a risk to manage through the design of the task and the workstation first, with rotation as one supporting measure. The polishing jig in Chapter 4's exercise, which holds tops at a comfortable angle, is an example of the first kind of control; the rotation is an example of the second.

## The failure case: the absence plan that existed only on paper

Three months into the cover plan, the matrix showed two competent people at every station. Then the welder took two weeks of leave, and the floater, rated competent at welding, ran the welding station. In the first two days, output fell to about two benches a day and two frames needed rework.

The matrix had been filled in from training completed, not from performance demonstrated. The floater had spent about forty hours at the welding station over three months, scattered across odd afternoons, and the learning curve from Chapter 2 had been partly lost between sessions. "Competent" on paper meant "has been trained", not "can run the station at normal pace".

### How it is detected

The matrix needs a performance test, not just a training record. The owner's rule now is that a rating of fully competent requires the person to have run the station for a full day, within about 15% of its normal time per bench, with no rework, in the last three months. A short test day before planned leave shows whether the cover is real.

### How it is corrected

Training is concentrated rather than scattered: a full week at the station, twice a year, rather than an afternoon now and then, which keeps the learning from fading between sessions. The floater also does a test day the week before the welder's next leave. On that leave, output stays at three benches a day.

## Buying in: division of labour between businesses

Smith's division of labour runs between businesses as well as within them. A trade becomes a separate business once the market for it is large enough. For a small engineering business, this offers an alternative to creating a narrow role: buy the specialist work from a business that does nothing else.

The question arises at the bench maker when the distributor's peaks and the C-series launch push polishing beyond what the line can absorb. The owner estimates about **900 hours a year** of extra polishing. He could employ a fifth person as a second polisher, or send the overflow to a specialist stainless finishing business nearby, which charges about **A$70 an hour** excluding GST for comparable work.

The [hire or outsource tool](/tools/hire-outsource/) in BIZOMAT Tools compares the annual cost of employing someone for a defined workload with buying the same hours from outside. From the annual workload, the salary, the employment on-costs as a percentage, the hiring and set-up cost and the external hourly rate, it gives the employee's first-year cost, the outside cost, the difference and the break-even workload at which the two are equal. Its checks remind the user to include all on-costs, to weigh flexibility, knowledge retention and quality alongside cost, and to check employment and tax rules where an individual contractor is involved.

With a salary of A$68,000, on-costs of 25% covering superannuation, leave, insurance and payroll costs, and A$4,000 for recruitment and induction, the tool gives:

At 900 hours a year, buying in is cheaper by about **A$26,000** in the first year and A$22,000 in later years. The break-even is about **1,270 hours** in the first year: if the extra polishing grew to 1,400 hours, employing would become cheaper by about A$9,000.

Cost is only part of the decision, as the tool says. Buying in adds transport and handling between businesses, and a turnaround time the bench maker does not control. It also means the polishing skill for the overflow sits outside the business. Against that, it avoids creating a role that might be under-used if the C-series does not grow, which is exactly the mistake Chapter 5 described with the first polisher. The owner buys in the overflow for the first year, reviews the volume quarterly, and agrees a turnaround time and a finish standard with the finishing business in writing. Where an arrangement is with an individual rather than a business, the owner checks the Australian Taxation Office's guidance and Fair Work Ombudsman information on employees and contractors before relying on it.

## Smith's gains and limits, together

The nine chapters of this book have followed Smith's argument from the pin factory to its qualifications. The bench maker's experience can be summed up in his terms.

```figure
type: layers
title: What dividing the work gave the bench maker, and what it required
caption: This is an illustration. Each gain was real and measured; each depended on a condition that had to be managed. Together they took the business from six benches a week with three generalists to fifteen with four people.
- Gains | dexterity on the new frame; about 45 minutes a bench of switching removed; a fixture saving 35 minutes a frame
- Market | a committed contract large enough to keep each station busy, tested before roles were created
- Balance | stations rebalanced to takt, improvement directed at the constraint
- Stock | work in progress capped; station equipment paid for; a working capital facility in place
- *Pooling | shared queues in the office; a floater in the workshop
- Narrow work | two people competent at every station; whole-product practices; overflow bought in
```

The list also suggests an order for any business considering the same change. Confirm the market first, because without it the gains never arrive. Measure the gains next, at the stations where the new work is steepest on its learning curve. Then balance the line and cap its stock, set up the shared queues, and plan cover before the first absence rather than after it. Buying in comes last, as a way to handle work that the business's own market for that skill is not yet large enough to support.

None of this makes dividing work a universal answer. At six benches a week, the generalists were right. At fifteen, the stations were right, but only with the conditions in place. The owner's most useful habit was to treat each of Smith's gains as a claim to measure and each of his limits as a condition to check.

## Exercise: when to employ

The C-series is selling better than expected, and the owner now estimates **1,400 hours a year** of polishing overflow. Using the hire or outsource tool's method with the same salary, on-costs, hiring cost and outside rate, which option is cheaper in the first year, and by how much? Name two non-cost factors that should be weighed before deciding.

**Worked answer.** At 1,400 hours, buying in costs 1,400 × A$70 = **A$98,000**. Employing costs A$68,000 × 1.25 + A$4,000 = **A$89,000** in the first year. Employing is cheaper by **A$9,000**, and by more in later years without the recruitment cost.

Two non-cost factors to weigh are the **steadiness of the volume**, because an employee is a fixed cost and Chapter 5 showed what happens to a role created on a peak, and **knowledge and quality control**, because an employee builds the business's own polishing skill and works to its standards and timetable, while bought-in work depends on another business's capacity and priorities. Others include cover for the existing polisher, which an employee would provide, and the management time each option needs. The tool's answer shows where cost points; the decision also needs those judgements.
