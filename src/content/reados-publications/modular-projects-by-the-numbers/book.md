## About this book

Many projects that look large are really the same job done many times: a dozen identical machines, forty substations, a hundred classrooms, twelve packing lines. Treated as one large bespoke project, each repetition is engineered afresh, installed slowly in place, and exposed for weeks to whatever goes wrong on a working site. Treated as a programme of standard modules, the design is done once, tested hard on a pilot, built faster each time by people who are learning, and installed in days. The modular route is not free: it costs more before the first unit pays back, and it fails if the module is chosen badly or the standard is allowed to drift.

This book works through that choice with numbers. It starts from the outcome the programme must deliver and works back to what should be standard, then covers interfaces and variants, iterating the plan with models and a pilot, proving the pilot with an acceptance test of the right length, and the learning curves that make repetition cheaper. It goes on to experience and team continuity, the risk of long exposure on site, rollout cadence and batch size, an honest comparison of the bespoke and modular options, and the change control that keeps a standard standard. Each chapter follows one running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for operations, engineering and project managers, automation and manufacturing engineers, and owners who plan multi-unit equipment programmes. It assumes spreadsheet arithmetic and logarithms at school level. The estimating and risk ideas it relies on are developed in *Reference Class Forecasting by the Numbers*, and this book links to it rather than repeating it.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional programme. This is an illustration. A snack-food manufacturer with three Australian sites packs product by hand at the end of twelve production lines: six at its largest site, four at the second and two at the third. Each line delivers **120 packs a minute** into cases of **12**, and two operators per shift hand-pack cases and stack pallets. The business plans to replace all twelve manual stations with automated **end-of-line cells**, each a case erector, case packer, checkweigher and robot palletiser. Each cell is expected to save three operator positions across two shifts, a net benefit of **A$180,000 a year** after its own running costs.

Two ways of delivering the programme are compared throughout. In the **bespoke** option, each cell is engineered for its own line and installed in place over about three weeks of disrupted production. In the **modular** option, one standard cell is designed once, proved in a pilot, built repeatedly in an integrator's workshop, tested before it ships, and installed on its skids over a long weekend.

The business, its sites, its lines and every number in the case are invented. None describes KEVOS® work, a real business or a real project. All amounts are Australian dollars excluding GST.

## How to use the book

Read Chapter 1 first, because the outcome it defines is the yardstick for every later decision. Chapters 2 to 4 cover design and proving, Chapters 5 and 6 learning and people, Chapters 7 and 8 the rollout, and Chapters 9 and 10 the comparison and the long-run discipline. Every calculation was checked by running it, and the KEVOS® tools linked in the chapters reproduce the learning-curve and cost arithmetic.

The book is one of a series of nine KEVOS® Library books on planning and delivering projects, written to be read together. *Reference Class Forecasting by the Numbers* supplies the outside view used here to judge each option's risk, and *Prototypes and Simulation by the Numbers* goes further into models, prototypes and test sizes. *First-of-a-Kind Technology by the Numbers* covers the cost of being first, which a standard module avoids, and *Project Teams and Contracts by the Numbers* the contracts and teams that deliver a rollout. *Front-End Planning by the Numbers*, *Stage Gates and Stopping Rules by the Numbers*, *Outcome-First Projects by the Numbers* and *Demand and Benefit Forecasts by the Numbers* complete the series.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The ideas are established and the sources are listed with what each was used for. The case's costs, rates and hours are teaching values, not market prices, and a real programme needs its own quotes, data and risk assessment. Packaging machinery and industrial robots are hazardous plant: their design, guarding, installation and commissioning are governed by work health and safety laws, the applicable codes of practice and standards, and the competent people responsible for them. This book teaches how to plan and compare delivery options. It is not a safety assessment, an engineering design or advice for any real installation.
