A finished weld hides most of what made it trustworthy. Once ground and painted, the repair in the tank looks much like the repair a less careful workshop might have made. The difference lives in the record: what the damage was, why the repair was chosen, how it was made and what proved it sound. Without that record, the next person to inspect the tank starts from nothing, and the next failure cannot be traced to its cause.

The 1919 Airco booklet left almost no such trail. Its methods relied on the welder, and the evidence of success was a joint that held water and steam. This final chapter gathers the book's methods into a repair dossier, a modest set of documents that turns a weld into evidence.

## What the dossier contains

A dossier for a significant repair can be short, but it should answer these questions in order:

1. **The damage.** What was found, where, how large, and by which inspection method. Sketches, photographs and thickness maps from Chapter 1 and Chapter 8 belong here.
2. **The cause.** The damage mechanism identified, and what will be done to remove or control it.
3. **The decision.** Why a local repair, an insert or a replacement was chosen, and who made the decision. For regulated equipment, the approvals obtained.
4. **The design of the repair.** Groove or insert geometry, material, and the drawings or sketches used.
5. **The procedure and welder.** The welding procedure specification and the qualification of each welder who worked on it.
6. **The execution record.** Sequence followed, preheat and interpass temperatures measured, consumable batches, dates and any deviations.
7. **The inspection and testing.** Methods, timing, results, the inspector's qualification, and any repairs to the repair.
8. **The return to service.** A statement of what has been shown, under what conditions, and what should be inspected and when.

## Record deviations honestly

Repairs rarely go exactly to plan. The crack is longer than mapped, the plate is thinner under the scale, or a pass shows porosity and has to be ground out. A dossier that records only the plan is less useful than one that records what happened. A deviation recorded with the decision taken is evidence of control; a deviation discovered later is evidence of the opposite.

## A scoped return-to-service statement

The final statement should be specific about what the evidence supports. "Repaired and tested" is not enough. A better statement names the repairs, the inspection and tests passed, the conditions of use assumed, any limitations, and the next inspection due. It does not claim more than the evidence shows. If the repaired part is regulated equipment, the statement and approvals take the form the regulator or inspection body requires.

## The tank case, brought together

This is an illustration. The workshop's dossier for the tank contains:

- **Damage record:** a 170 mm fatigue crack at the toe of an old nozzle fillet weld, a riveted seam with three cracked holes in plate thinned to 6.8 to 7.4 mm, and a 350 mm by 250 mm corroded region near the base down to 6.5 mm, all mapped and photographed.
- **Causes and controls:** unsupported pipework vibrating the nozzle, now supported; seam corrosion from long-term leakage, removed with the seam; base corrosion from standing water against the plate, addressed by a drainage change.
- **Decisions:** excavate and weld the nozzle crack; replace the seam region with a welded insert, reviewed by the owner's engineer because it changes the joint type; replace the corroded base region with a 400 mm by 300 mm rounded insert.
- **Material:** plate composition measured, carbon equivalent 0.404, so a low-hydrogen procedure with measured preheat is used.
- **Execution:** back-step sequences for the crack and both inserts, preheat and interpass temperatures recorded, one porosity indication on an insert ground out and rewelded, with the deviation noted.
- **Inspection:** visual inspection of all welds, delayed penetrant inspection of every repair, thickness readings around the inserts and a fill test with no leakage.
- **Return to service:** the tank is returned to service for atmospheric water storage, with the repaired areas to be inspected at the next scheduled shutdown.

## What changed between 1919 and now

The booklet captured real engineering insight. Its back-step sequences, open roots, preheating to share shrinkage, triangular patches that avoid lines of holes and preference for replacing badly cracked sheets all respond to physical mechanisms that this book has explained in modern terms.

What changed is everything around the weld. Processes became more controllable. Metallurgy explained hydrogen cracking and the heat-affected zone. Inspection methods learned to see inside a weld. Procedures and welders became qualified rather than trusted. Regulators and standards set limits on what may be repaired, by whom and with what evidence. The skill of the welder still matters, but it is no longer the only thing standing between a repair and a failure.

Read the old booklet for its instincts. Plan a modern repair with the controls this book has described, and leave a record that lets someone else check the work.

## Final exercise: write the statement

Draft a return-to-service statement for the tank in no more than five sentences, using only the evidence listed above.

A strong answer names the three repairs, states the inspection and fill test results, limits the use to atmospheric water storage, records the controls applied to each cause, and sets the next inspection of the repaired areas. It avoids words such as "as new" or "permanently repaired", which the evidence does not support, and it does not mention pressures or uses the tank was never tested for.
