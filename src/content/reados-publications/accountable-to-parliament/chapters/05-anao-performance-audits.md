A performance audit is the closest look anyone outside an agency will take at how it runs a program. The auditors can read every file, ask any official to explain a decision, and report what they find to Parliament in words the agency does not write. What the agency controls is how well it gives access, how carefully it checks the facts, and how clearly it says what it will do.

This is an illustration. In November 2027 the Auditor-General notified NRIO's CEO of a performance audit of Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP), to be done by the Australian National Audit Office (ANAO), which assists the Auditor-General. Round 1 had received 186 applications. Nine were ineligible, so 177 were assessed, and on Mon 31 May 2027 the Minister approved 31 grants worth A$9.98 million, 2 of them against officials' recommendations. Oscar Hale, NRIO's Audit Liaison Officer, became the audit team's single point of contact. Clare Whitfield knew where it would end: in a report tabled in Parliament, with NRIO's written comments printed in it.

## An independent officer of the Parliament

Section 8(1) of the [Auditor-General Act 1997](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05248/latest/text) says: "The Auditor-General is an independent officer of the Parliament." Section 8(4) gives that independence its working meaning. The Auditor-General is "not subject to direction from anyone in relation to" whether a particular audit is conducted, the way it is conducted, or "the priority to be given to any particular matter."

Parliament has a voice, but not a hand on the controls. Section 10 says the Auditor-General must have regard to "the audit priorities of the Parliament determined by the Joint Committee of Public Accounts and Audit". The committee's own Act says that nothing in its list of duties "authorises the Committee to direct the activities of the Auditor-General" (Public Accounts and Audit Committee Act 1951, s8(1A)). Chapter 4 introduced the committee, and Chapter 6 returns to it.

For an agency, independence means that nobody, including the CEO and the Minister's office, can stop an audit, narrow it or slow it down. You can still make your case. Tell the audit team in writing, with reasons, when a criterion seems to misread the program or key staff will be away, and accept that the Auditor-General decides.

## What a performance audit examines

Section 17(1) says: "The Auditor-General may at any time conduct a performance audit of: (a) a Commonwealth entity". The Act defines a performance audit as "a review or examination of any aspect of the operations" of a person or body (s5). A performance audit asks how well something was done, not whether the accounts are right. It is separate from the audits of annual financial statements (s11) and performance statements (s15).

Ask for the audit's objective and criteria in writing at the start, because the proposed report will judge your agency against them. Then set up the basics: one liaison officer, a register of every request and what was provided, and a short brief for the accountable authority. PGPA Act s19(1)(a) requires the accountable authority to "keep the responsible Minister informed of the activities of the entity". Tell the Minister's office that the audit has begun and when it is expected to report.

This is an illustration. The ANAO's letter of November 2027 set the objective: whether NRIO designed and administered RCFP Round 1 effectively. It gave three criteria: the round's design and guidelines; the assessment of applications and the advice to the Minister; and the handling of outcomes, including feedback, complaints and reporting. Fieldwork ran from December 2027 to March 2028. Oscar logged 64 requests: 58 were met by the agreed date and 6 were late, each with a note saying why and when. The auditors interviewed 14 officials, Sam Reid, who manages the RCFP, among them.

## Access to records and people

Under s32(1) the Auditor-General may, by written notice, direct a person to provide "any information that the Auditor-General requires", to attend and give evidence, or to produce documents. A person must comply with such a direction (s32(3)), and the penalty for not complying is 30 penalty units. Under s33(1) the Auditor-General or an authorised official may enter Commonwealth premises at all reasonable times. They are entitled to "full and free access at all reasonable times to any documents or other property", and may examine and copy them. The occupier must give an authorised official all reasonable facilities to use those powers (s33(3)).

This is an illustration. Every request in the RCFP audit came by email, not by notice, but Oscar treated each one as if the formal power stood behind it. He set four rules for the program team:

- Give the record, then explain it. Never send a summary or a tidied copy in place of the source.
- If a record does not exist, say so. Never create one after the event to fill a gap. A note written later is acceptable only if it says when it was written and what it rests on.
- Say where you searched. A reply to "all advice to the Minister" lists the systems and mailboxes checked, so the auditors can see what "all" meant.
- Keep a logged copy of everything provided, so NRIO can show exactly what the auditors saw.

Do not decide on your own that a document is too sensitive to provide. The Act deals with sensitivity mainly when a report is published. Section 37 stops the Auditor-General including information in a public report where disclosure would be contrary to the public interest, for reasons such as Cabinet deliberations or the security of the Commonwealth. If a request touches Cabinet material, legal advice or personal information, tell the audit team and your legal area straight away, and let them settle how it is handled.

Answer auditors with the same care as Parliament. The Code of Conduct forbids false or misleading information in response to a request made for official purposes (Public Service Act s13(9)). Correct a wrong answer in writing as soon as you find it.

## The proposed report and the 28 days

After preparing a proposed report on an audit of a Commonwealth entity, the Auditor-General must "give a copy of the proposed report to an official who is, or is a member of, the accountable authority of the entity" (s19(1)(a)). For NRIO that is the CEO. The Auditor-General may also give a copy or an extract to any person, including a Minister, who has a special interest in the report (s19(6)).

Under s19(7), if the recipient gives written comments within the time allowed, the Auditor-General must consider them before preparing the final report. Here the time is set by s19(7)(b): "within 28 days after receiving the proposed report, or the extract from the proposed report." For an audit of performance measures under s18A, it is 14 days (s19(7)(a)). The period is 28 days, not a month and not 30 days.

Section 19(8) then says: "The Auditor-General must, in the final report, include all written comments received under subsection (7)." So write every comment for Parliament and the public, because it will be printed. And the duties to consider and print attach only to comments given within the period; late comments lose that protection.

Count carefully. Section 36(1) of the [Acts Interpretation Act 1901](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1901A00002/latest/text) says that a period expressed to begin after a specified day does not include that day. Its own example: a 28-day period after 2 August begins on 3 August. So day 1 is the day after the report is received.

This is an illustration. The CEO received the proposed report on Mon 10 Apr 2028. Day 1 was Tue 11 Apr, and day 28 was Mon 8 May 2028. The period held four weekends and three public holidays: Good Friday on 14 April, Easter Monday on 17 April and Anzac Day on Tuesday 25 April. That left 17 working days. Oscar asked the audit team to confirm the due date in writing that afternoon, and it did.

## Keeping the proposed report confidential

Section 36(3) makes it an offence for a person who receives "a proposed report (including a draft) under section 19", or an extract from one, to disclose "any information in the report or extract". The penalty is imprisonment for 2 years. The offence does not apply if "the Auditor-General has consented to the disclosure" (s36(4)). A separate offence in s36(1) covers people who obtain information while performing the Auditor-General's functions, such as the auditors themselves.

Three working rules follow. The duty travels with the document: everyone who receives it is bound, however far down a chain of emails. Consent is the Auditor-General's to give, so settle in writing who may see the report before anyone else does. And the risk lies in the content, not just the file: a summary in a brief, a paraphrase in a meeting or a finding repeated to a colleague not on the list all disclose information in the report.

Keeping the Minister informed does not require the report's contents. Tell the Minister's office that it has arrived and when comments are due. If the office needs the content, ask the audit team: the Auditor-General can give the Minister a copy or extract under s19(6), or consent to the agency sharing it.

This is an illustration. On the afternoon of Mon 10 Apr, the audit team confirmed in writing that the Auditor-General consented to eight named people seeing the report. They were the CEO; the Chief Finance Officer; the Chief Audit Executive; the Director, Regional Programs; Sam Reid; Clare; Oscar; and one lawyer from NRIO's legal area. The report sat in one restricted folder that only they could open, and was never sent as an attachment.

## A response that will be printed

A good response has three parts. A short summary, signed by the accountable authority, says what the agency accepts, what it has already done and what it will do. A response to each recommendation says agreed, agreed with a qualification, or not agreed, and gives the reason, the action, the owner and the date. Factual corrections come with evidence, raised with the audit team as soon as they are found.

Write for a Senator, a journalist or an applicant reading the tabled report, not for the auditors. Accept findings that are right, without adjectives. Where a finding is wrong, say exactly what is wrong and point to the record that shows it. Do not argue with the audit's judgement in general terms, and do not promise actions that nobody has funded. Chapter 6 explains why agreeing is a commitment.

Check consistency as well. The response should not contradict what the agency has already told Parliament, unless it explains the difference.

This is an illustration. Clare checked the draft against everything NRIO had said about the RCFP: the Budget estimates transcript of Wed 2 Jun 2027, the CEO's correcting letter of Fri 4 Jun 2027, the answers to the 19 RCFP questions on notice, NRIO's submission to the Senate inquiry and the transcript of the hearing on Thu 21 Oct 2027. She found one problem: a draft paragraph said that all applications were assessed, the error the CEO had corrected in June 2027. It was rewritten to give 186 received, 9 ineligible and 177 assessed.

## Tabling and the day of release

Under s17(4), as soon as practicable after completing the report, the Auditor-General must "cause a copy of the report to be tabled in each House of the Parliament". The Auditor-General must also give a copy to the responsible Minister and to an official of the entity's accountable authority. The timing is the Auditor-General's: ask when tabling is expected, but treat no date as fixed until it is confirmed.

Prepare for the day before it comes: a brief for the Minister, updated Estimates briefs (Chapter 2), and an owner and register entry for each agreed recommendation. The Joint Committee of Public Accounts and Audit has a duty "to examine all reports of the Auditor-General" tabled in each House (Public Accounts and Audit Committee Act 1951, s8(1)(c)).

This is an illustration. The report was tabled in June 2028 with four recommendations, all agreed, and NRIO's comments printed in full. That day Oscar entered the four recommendations in the register, Ravi Menon updated the RCFP issue briefs, and Clare sent the Minister's office a one-page summary of the findings and NRIO's actions.

## When it goes wrong: the proposed report on an all-staff list

This is an illustration. The proposed report reached the CEO's office by email at about 10 am on Mon 10 Apr 2028. Under the office's routine for incoming reports, an executive officer forwarded it to the Regional Programs inbox "for action". Just before noon a team leader in the branch forwarded it to the branch's all-staff list of 38 people, three of them contractors. Oscar's list of eight was confirmed that afternoon, about three hours too late.

Oscar found out on Tue 11 Apr, when a contractor emailed him a question about a finding. He and Clare told the audit team that morning. NRIO's IT area removed the email from 39 mailboxes, the 38 on the list and the branch inbox, and each of the 38 confirmed in writing that they had kept no copy and passed nothing on. Whether any step was an offence was a question for NRIO's legal area and the ANAO, not the team.

Two of the 17 working days, Tue 11 and Wed 12 Apr, went on containment, leaving 17 − 2 = 15 for the response. Thirty-eight more people now held information they were bound not to disclose, a risk NRIO could not take back. The audit team's trust took a knock. And the margin in Oscar's plan, shown in the exercise below, was used up: the comments went in on the last day, Mon 8 May.

The controls that would have prevented it are simple. Agree the access list with the audit team at the end of fieldwork, before the proposed report exists. Route anything from the ANAO marked as a proposed report to the accountable authority and the liaison officer only, never to a branch inbox. Hold the report in one restricted folder, never as an attachment. And give every reader the same short warning about s36(3) before they open it.

## Exercise: 28 days to a signed response

This is an illustration. It is the afternoon of Mon 10 Apr 2028. The proposed report has four recommendations: records of advice to the Minister, one reconciled set of program figures, complaints about grant outcomes, and lessons before any further round. Chapter 6 sets them out. Plan NRIO's response:

- confirm the last day and the working days available;
- say who may see the report and how it is held;
- set out the stages, owners and dates, with a margin for the unexpected;
- say who signs, what the response contains and how it is sent.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. **The last day.** The CEO received the report on Mon 10 Apr. A period that begins after a day excludes that day (Acts Interpretation Act s36(1)), so day 1 is Tue 11 Apr. April has 30 days: 10 + 28 = 38, and 38 − 30 = 8, so day 28 is Mon 8 May 2028. Oscar confirms that date with the audit team in writing today.

**Working days.** The 28 days hold four weekends, which is 8 days, so 28 − 8 = 20 weekdays. Good Friday, Easter Monday and Anzac Day fall inside them, so 20 − 3 = 17 working days. Plan to send the comments on working day 15, Thu 4 May, leaving 2 working days in reserve.

**Access.** Only the eight people the Auditor-General has consented to, through one restricted folder with an access log. The Minister's office is told the dates, not the content.

**Stages.** The plan uses all 17 working days:

| Working days | Dates | Step | Lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Tue 11 to Thu 13 Apr | Owners named; facts checked against the records; errors raised with the audit team as found | Oscar, Sam |
| 4 to 7 | Tue 18 to Fri 21 Apr | Responses drafted with action, owner, date and cost; consistency with what NRIO told Parliament checked | Sam, Clare |
| 8 | Mon 24 Apr | Draft cleared; cost of actions confirmed | Director, Regional Programs; CFO |
| 9 to 11 | Wed 26 to Fri 28 Apr | CEO's office reviews | CEO's office |
| 12 to 13 | Mon 1 and Tue 2 May | Changes made; access log checked against the agreed list | Oscar, Clare |
| 14 | Wed 3 May | CEO signs | CEO |
| 15 | Thu 4 May | Signed comments sent; receipt confirmed in writing | Oscar |
| 16 to 17 | Fri 5 and Mon 8 May | Margin | none |

The stages add up: 3 + 4 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 + 2 = 17 working days.

**Signature and content.** The proposed report went to the CEO under s19(1)(a), so the CEO signs. The response has a summary; a response to each recommendation, all four agreed, each with a reason, an owner and a date (Chapter 6); and factual corrections with references to the records. Section 19(8) will put all of it in the tabled report.

**Why the margin matters.** The failure above cost two working days at the start, so every later step moved two days and the comments went in on working day 17, Mon 8 May. Without the margin they would have been late, and the Act's duties to consider and print them would not have applied.

Chapter 6 follows the four recommendations from the CEO's signature to evidence that each one has been done.
