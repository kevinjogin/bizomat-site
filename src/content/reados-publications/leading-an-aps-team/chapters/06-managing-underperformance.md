Most underperformance is fixed in a conversation. The conversation works best when it comes early, in private, with specific examples and an honest question about what is getting in the way. It works worst when it comes late, as a surprise, or as the first page of a formal process.

This is an illustration. On Wednesday 30 September 2026, Leila Farah brought her check log to Hannah. Since the start of September, about 1 in 5 of Josh Baker's letters had needed rework before they could be sent. Before that it had been about 1 in 20. Josh is an APS 5 correspondence officer in Leila's team, with no concern on his record. Leila's first suggestion was to "put him on a performance plan".

This chapter follows what Hannah did instead: what she checked, how she planned to raise it and what support she could offer. It also sets out what NRIO's formal process would involve if it ever came to that, and where such a process can end.

## Your part, and the People Branch's

Section 50(g) of the [Commissioner's Directions](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) describes a supervisor "promptly and actively managing unsatisfactory performance" under the agency's policies. That includes identifying the nature of the problem "at the earliest opportunity" and "maintaining appropriate records". It also includes engaging with the employee, the HR area and the supervisor's own manager to "facilitate a collective understanding" of the problem. As Chapter 5 noted, section 50 describes how a supervisor upholds a principle. Your agency's procedure sets the steps you are required to follow.

The agency and the employee have parts too. Under section 49(2), the Agency Head ensures that policies on unsatisfactory performance are available, set out supervisors' responsibilities and the possible outcomes, and are applied "in a timely manner". Section 51(f) describes the employee cooperating to resolve the issues and undertaking training or remedial measures "as directed".

This is an illustration. At NRIO, Hannah manages Josh's performance: she holds his agreement, raises the concern, arranges support and keeps the record. Leila supplies evidence from her check log and gives day-to-day feedback, but she does not run the process. The People Branch owns NRIO's fictional procedure and advises at each step, and it must be involved before any formal stage begins. The Director, as Hannah's manager, is kept informed. A delegate decides any formal outcome.

## Check the facts and the causes first

Before the conversation, find out what the evidence shows and what else has changed. A sudden change in someone's work usually has a cause, and some causes are the manager's to fix.

This is an illustration. Hannah asked Leila for the log itself, not a summary. It showed:

- January to August 2026: 18 of Josh's 360 letters needed rework, which is 5 per cent or 1 in 20 (18 ÷ 360 = 0.05);
- September 2026: 9 of 45 needed rework, which is 20 per cent or 1 in 5 (9 ÷ 45 = 0.2);
- of the nine, four used out-of-date facility or program information, three missed a question the writer had asked, and two did not follow NRIO's template;
- all 45 letters still went out within the 15-business-day standard.

Each rework took about 50 minutes: 20 for Leila to mark up the letter and explain, and 30 for Josh to fix it. In September that came to 9 × 50 = 450 minutes, or 7.5 hours.

Hannah then checked the causes she could see. Josh's volume had not changed: 360 letters over eight months is 45 a month, the same as September. Nobody had changed the template or the types of letter, and the facility information pages were current. Nothing in the work explained the change, which made Josh's own account more important, not less. Other possible causes, such as health, family or personal life, are not the manager's to guess at. Josh might raise them or not. Either way, the conversation is about the work.

Hannah also asked the People Branch which part of NRIO's procedure applied. The adviser confirmed it was the informal stage: talk to Josh, agree the standard and the support, set a review date and keep a note. Josh's 2025–26 final assessment was also due in October. The concern had arisen in the last month of that cycle and had not been raised with him during it, so Hannah did not let it decide his rating (Chapter 5). She recorded it as raised and being addressed.

## Raise it early, privately and with examples

The first conversation sets the tone for everything after it. Five habits make it work:

- **Early.** Raise a pattern within days of seeing it. A month of data is often enough to show one, and waiting for the mid-point review can cost months.
- **Private.** Use a meeting room, not the open office, a team meeting or an email. Avoid late on a Friday. Tell the person beforehand what the meeting is about, so they are not ambushed.
- **Specific.** Describe the work, not the person. "Four letters last month gave old opening hours" is a fact the person can check. "You've been careless" is a judgement they can only resent.
- **Curious.** Ask what is getting in the way, then listen. The aim is the shared understanding of the problem that section 50(g) describes.
- **Clear.** Say what good looks like and that the standard stays. Then agree what will change, what support comes with it and when you will review it together.

Write a short note the same day and give the person a copy. The informal stage is not a lesser version of the formal one. It is the stage at which the problem is cheapest to fix, because nothing formal stands between two people talking about the work.

## Support, adjustments and wellbeing

Support should match the cause:

- **Skills or knowledge:** examples of good letters, a checklist to use before sending, coaching on complex complaints.
- **Information or tools:** fix the source, such as an out-of-date page.
- **Workload:** rebalance it for a period, and say for how long.
- **Health, disability or personal circumstances:** if the person raises them, ask your HR area about workplace adjustments and about leave under your enterprise agreement. Do not ask for a diagnosis.

Support does not lower the standard. It changes the conditions so that the standard can be met. Record what was offered and what was taken up, because a reviewer will ask.

Some powers are not tools for a conversation. The Public Service Regulations 2023 let an Agency Head direct an employee to a medical examination if the Agency Head believes the employee's health "may be affecting the employee's work performance" (s 11(1)(a)(i)). That is a formal step for the Agency Head or a delegate, taken on HR advice. It is not a manager's step.

Wellbeing runs through the whole process. Under the Work Health and Safety Act 2011, "health means physical and psychological health" (s 4). The Work Health and Safety (Managing Psychosocial Hazards at Work) Code of Practice 2024 lists "Lack of role clarity" and "Poor organisational justice" among the hazards (Code, 1.1). A vague standard and an unfair process are therefore safety risks as well as performance risks, and Chapter 8 returns to them.

This is an illustration. Hannah plans to mention NRIO's employee assistance service in the first conversation and again in her note. She will check in with Josh the next day and keep the matter between herself, Leila, the People Branch and the Director. If the conversations become hard, she will ask the People Branch for support for herself too.

## Evidence and a fair hearing

A performance record may be read by people who were not there: the People Branch, a delegate or a reviewer. Evidence holds up when it is first-hand, dated, specific, measured against an agreed standard and shown to the employee at the time, with their response noted.

```figure
- Source | Leila's check log and the letters themselves | "Others have noticed it too"
- Specific | Four September letters with out-of-date facility information | "Careless lately"
- Standard | At most 1 in 10 needing rework, agreed and written down | "Not up to scratch"
- Shared | Shown to Josh at the time, with his response noted | First produced at a formal meeting
- Context | Volume, template, information sources and support checked | A rate with nothing around it
```

Procedural fairness, often called natural justice, comes down to three things in practice. The employee knows the concern and the evidence behind it. The employee has a real chance to respond before any decision, and the response is genuinely considered. And the person who decides is unbiased and decides on the evidence. Section 49(1) of the Directions asks for performance policies that are "fair, open and effective". These three habits are what that looks like at a manager's desk.

Fair process protects the manager as well. The Fair Work Act 2009 says its definition of bullying at work "does not apply to reasonable management action carried out in a reasonable manner" (s 789FD(2)). Managing performance is management action, and both halves of that phrase matter. The action must be reasonable, and so must the way it is done.

## NRIO's formal process

This is an illustration. NRIO's underperformance procedure is fictional. It is set out here so the parts of a fair process can be seen together. Your agency's procedure and enterprise agreement set the real stages, time frames and outcomes, and this book gives no figures for them.

```figure
type: steps
title: NRIO's underperformance procedure
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's procedure is fictional. Your agency's policy and enterprise agreement set the real stages, time frames and outcomes.
- Everyday feedback | Specific, timely comments on the work, as part of normal management (Chapter 5).
- Informal stage | A private conversation covering the concern, examples, the standard, support and a review date, with a dated note the employee sees.
- Review | If the standard is met, normal management resumes, with the work still watched. If not, the supervisor goes to the People Branch.
- Formal stage | Opened only with the People Branch, after an informal stage on record. Written concerns, the standard, support, a set period and regular written reviews, with a support person and a written response allowed at each review.
- *Outcome | If the standard is met, the formal stage closes. If not, a delegate who has not managed the case decides, after the employee responds to the proposed outcome.
```

Three features matter in any agency's version. The formal stage starts only after an informal stage on record, with HR involved. The employee sees the concerns and the evidence in writing and can respond at every review. And the person who decides the outcome has not managed the case, so the decision rests on the record, not on the working relationship.

## Where a formal process can end

Termination is the far end of the road, and a manager should know where it sits. Section 29(3) of the [Public Service Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) lists "the only grounds" for terminating the employment of an ongoing APS employee. One of them is "(c) non‑performance, or unsatisfactory performance, of duties". A note to section 29(1) adds that the Fair Work Act 2009 has rules and entitlements that apply to termination. A termination is not open to review under section 33 of the Public Service Act (s 33(1)).

That is for awareness, not a step for you. The power belongs to the Agency Head, who may delegate it in writing (s 78(7)), and your HR area advises the delegate. The manager contributes the record: an honest, dated account of the expectation, the evidence, the conversations, the support and the employee's responses. Every earlier stage aims at improvement, and a good record serves that aim first.

## When it goes wrong: a performance plan as the first word

This is an illustration of what Hannah did not do. Suppose she had taken Leila's first suggestion. On Friday 2 October 2026 she adapts a template from her previous agency and emails Josh a "performance improvement plan", with the nine letters attached and a review date. It is the first he has heard of any concern.

Josh is shocked. He takes leave the following week and lodges a complaint that he is being targeted. When the People Branch sees the plan, it finds four problems:

- there is no record of any earlier conversation;
- the only written standard is "Produce high-quality correspondence", which has no measure;
- the template is not NRIO's procedure;
- no support was offered, or even asked about.

The plan is withdrawn, and the matter restarts at the informal stage, a month later than it needed to. Meanwhile, Leila is still fixing letters, and the People Branch is handling a complaint instead of helping with the problem. The trust the informal stage needs is now much harder to build. Even if the concern was valid, the way it was raised invites the argument that the action was not carried out in a reasonable manner.

Two controls would have stopped it. NRIO's procedure opens a formal stage only through the People Branch, after an informal stage with a dated record. And the manager's own test would have caught it: before any written step, is there a record of an earlier conversation, a clear standard and a fair chance to improve? If not, the next step is the conversation.

## Exercise: plan the first conversation with Josh

This is an illustration. Hannah has booked a meeting with Josh on Thursday 8 October 2026. She has the facts above and the People Branch's advice that this is the informal stage. She also has Josh's 2025–26 agreement ("Produce high-quality correspondence") and her draft 2026–27 expectation from Chapter 5: at least 9 in 10 letters approved at Leila's check without rework each month.

Plan the conversation:

1. List the evidence Hannah should gather before the meeting, and anything she should not use.
2. Work out what the standard means at Josh's volume, and what the rework costs now.
3. Set out how the conversation should run, what should be agreed and how it should be recorded.

### Worked answer

**1. Evidence to gather.**

- Leila's check log for January to August, September and October to date, so the rate is measured the same way throughout.
- The nine September letters with Leila's mark-ups, grouped by type: four with out-of-date information, three with a missed question and two off-template.
- Two or three letters Josh drafted well, for balance and as models.
- His 2025–26 agreement, his April 2026 mid-point record and any note of earlier feedback. There is none, which confirms this is the first conversation.
- Hannah's checks of workload, templates and information sources, so she can say what she has ruled out.
- NRIO's procedure, the People Branch's advice and the details of the employee assistance service.

Hannah should leave out impressions from other staff, comparisons with named colleagues, guesses about Josh's private life and anything she is not prepared to show him.

**2. The numbers.**

- **The change:** 18 ÷ 360 = 5 per cent before September and 9 ÷ 45 = 20 per cent in September. The rate is four times what it was (20 ÷ 5 = 4).
- **The standard at 45 letters:** 45 × 0.9 = 40.5, so at least 41 must pass first time and no more than 4 may need rework. In September, 45 − 9 = 36 passed first time, which is 80 per cent (36 ÷ 45).
- **The rework time:** 9 × 50 = 450 minutes, or 7.5 hours, in September. At the standard it would be at most 4 × 50 = 200 minutes, about 3.3 hours.
- **The review:** four weeks (28 days) after the meeting is Thursday 5 November 2026. At about 45 letters a month, Josh will draft about 40 letters by then, which is enough to see whether the rate has moved.

**3. The conversation.**

- **Before.** On Wednesday 7 October, Hannah tells Josh what the meeting is about: the letters Leila has been returning since September, and how she can help. She books a private room for 45 minutes.
- **Open.** She states the purpose plainly. This is an everyday conversation, not a formal process. For eight months, 19 in 20 of his letters passed first time, and something has changed.
- **Facts.** She gives the numbers, an example of each type of error and the effect on Leila's time and on people waiting for correct answers.
- **Ask and listen.** "What's been happening since September? What's getting in the way?" She lets Josh talk and does not diagnose. If he raises health or personal matters, she thanks him, asks what would help and says she will get advice on adjustments.
- **The standard.** At least 9 in 10 letters pass Leila's check first time, each answering every question with current information and the right template. It will go into his 2026–27 agreement.
- **Support.** They agree on whatever fits his account. Options include a weekly 15-minute review of returned letters with Leila, a checklist built from the three error types, or a temporary change to his mix of letters. She mentions the employee assistance service.
- **Review.** They meet again on Thursday 5 November 2026, with Leila's log for the four weeks.
- **Record.** Hannah writes a short note that day: the date, the concern and examples, Josh's account in his words as far as he is comfortable, the standard, the support and the review date. She gives Josh a copy, invites his comments and files it in NRIO's performance system. She tells the People Branch the informal stage has started, tells the Director briefly, and tells Leila only what Leila needs in order to give the support.
- **After.** She checks in with Josh the next day.

Three things stay out of the room: the words "performance plan", any comparison with colleagues and any promise about what happens next. If the review shows the standard met, the support tapers off and the regular one-to-ones carry on. If not, Hannah goes back to the People Branch with a record that shows exactly what was tried.
