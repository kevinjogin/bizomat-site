On 12 July 1389, Richard II appointed Geoffrey Chaucer clerk of the king's works. The post made him responsible for repairs and building at the Palace of Westminster, the Tower of London and a list of royal manors and lodges. He did not lay stone or cut timber. He bought materials, hired and paid workers, kept the accounts and answered for them to the Exchequer. Within months he was overseeing the scaffolds built for jousts at Smithfield, the medieval equivalent of temporary grandstands.

Six centuries later, the job title survives. A clerk of works on a modern project is usually the owner's eyes on site: someone who watches what is built, checks it against what was agreed and keeps the record. The work has changed less than the title suggests. This chapter describes the role then and now, sets up the case the book follows, and puts in place the controls that Chaucer's own records show were needed.

## Chaucer's office

The records of Chaucer's two years as clerk of the works are unusually full, because the post was an accounting one and the Exchequer kept the paperwork. A few details matter for this book.

Money arrived by assignment. The National Archives holds one of several orders from 1389 authorising Chaucer to collect £100 from the London wool customs for repairs at the Tower. The receipts were recorded by tally, a notched stick split between payer and payee so that neither could alter the record alone. Chaucer then accounted for how the money was spent.

He took over the office's stock formally. An indenture dated November 1389 records the unused stock and tools delivered to him as the new clerk. An indenture was written twice on one sheet and cut apart along a wavy line, so that each party held a copy that matched only its partner. The handover was itself a controlled record.

He carried cash on the road. In September 1390, travelling on the king's business, he was robbed by highwaymen near a place called the Foul Oak in Hatcham, Surrey, and lost about £20 of the king's money. The surviving accounts differ on whether he was robbed once or more than once. What is clear is that the loss was his to answer for, and that by a writ of 6 January 1391 the king released him from repaying it. He left the office in June 1391.

His pay was two shillings a day. The responsibility was considerably larger than the pay suggests: a clerk who could not account for the money was personally liable for it.

## The role today

There is no single legal definition of a clerk of works in Australia, and the role varies by sector and contract. In general terms, a clerk of works is appointed by the owner to observe the work on site, check it against the contract documents, keep records of progress, quality and events, and report to the owner and to whoever administers the contract. On a project under a form such as AS 4000, the contract usually names a superintendent who gives directions, assesses claims and certifies payment. The clerk of works may be the superintendent's representative, or may report to the owner separately. What the clerk of works should not do is direct the builder outside the contract's channels, because that blurs who is responsible for the work.

```figure

```

Chaucer's office combined several of these roles: he was purchaser, paymaster and accountant as well as overseer. A modern project separates them deliberately, because the medieval arrangement put too much in one pair of hands. That separation is the first lesson the role offers.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A regional agricultural society in New South Wales has run its annual show at the same ground for more than a century. Its timber grandstand, built in the 1950s, has been closed after an engineer's inspection found decay in several main posts. The society has raised funds, partly from a state grant, to replace it with a 480-seat steel grandstand: 12 rows of 40 seats under a roof, with a kiosk and toilets underneath.

The society's approved budget, excluding GST, is:

| Item | Amount (A$, ex GST) |
| --- | --- |
| Head contract with the builder, lump sum | 1,020,000 |
| Seats, bought directly by the society | 86,400 |
| Design and certification fees | 48,000 |
| Services connections | 25,600 |
| Base budget | 1,180,000 |

GST at 10% adds A$118,000, for A$1,298,000 including GST. The head contract runs for 26 weeks, holds 5% retention from each progress payment and provides for liquidated damages of A$1,500 per working day of delay. Chapter 2 deals with contingency, which is not in the base budget.

The society appoints a clerk of works: a semi-retired structural engineer who lives near the showground and will spend two days a week on the project. The rest of this book follows that person's work. The society, the builder and every figure are invented for teaching and are not BIZOMAT work.

## The first week: an indenture for the old stand

Chaucer's first recorded act as clerk was to receive the office's stock under an indenture. The clerk of works' first act on the grandstand is the same in spirit: to record exactly what the builder is taking over, before anything is moved.

The old stand is not an empty site. It contains salvageable timber the society wants to keep, a memorial plaque, an electrical switchboard that also feeds the show pavilion, water and sewer connections, and a stack of old drawings in a filing cabinet in the secretary's office. Each of these is a potential dispute later if nobody wrote down its condition and ownership at the start.

The clerk of works prepares a site handover record in two copies, signed by the society and the builder:

1. Photographs of every elevation of the old stand and every service connection, dated and numbered.
2. A list of items the society is keeping, where they will be stored and who is responsible for removing them.
3. The meter readings for power and water on the day of possession.
4. The location of the switchboard and the circuits that must stay live for the pavilion.
5. A copy of every existing drawing found, with a note that their accuracy is not warranted.

The record costs half a day. In week 9, when an unrecorded water main is found under the old stand's footing, it is the photographs and the existing drawings register that show the main was not on any drawing the society supplied. That settles, in a page, a question that could otherwise have become a claim.

## Failure: the robbery on the road, in modern form

Chaucer lost the king's money to highwaymen. Owners today lose it more quietly, but more often, to payment redirection: an email that appears to come from a supplier or builder, asking that the next payment be made to a new bank account.

This is an illustration. In week 5, the builder's first progress claim is assessed. The work completed is valued at A$135,000 excluding GST. Retention at 5% is A$6,750, so A$128,250 is payable, plus GST of A$12,825: A$141,075 in total. Two days before the payment is due, the society's treasurer receives an email, apparently from the builder's accounts officer, with the builder's logo and a plausible signature, saying that the company has changed banks and attaching a letter with new account details.


The treasurer forwards the email to the clerk of works, as the first-week controls require for any change to payment details. The clerk of works rings the builder's office on the number in the signed contract, not the number in the email. The builder has not changed banks. The email came from a look-alike address with one letter different from the real domain.

Detection worked because the control had been set before it was needed. The correction, already in place, was a written rule agreed at the start-up meeting:

1. Bank details for every payee are recorded once, from the signed contract or a signed supplier form, and confirmed by phone on a known number.
2. Any request to change bank details is confirmed by phone on the number already held, by someone other than the person who received the request.
3. Payments are approved by two people: the treasurer, and the president or vice-president. Neither can approve alone.
4. The clerk of works checks every payment against the superintendent's certificate before it is released.

None of this is sophisticated. It is the modern version of the tally and the indenture: two parties, two copies, and no single person able to change the record alone.

## Separating the hands

The deeper lesson of Chaucer's office is about how many functions one person holds. As clerk of the works he certified what was needed, bought it, received it, paid for it and accounted for it. That made him efficient. It also meant that the only check on his honesty and accuracy was the Exchequer's audit, months later. When money went missing on the road, the only evidence of what had happened was his own word.

A small organisation commissioning a building faces the same temptation. The person who knows the project best is asked to approve variations, check invoices and authorise payments, because nobody else understands them. The clerk of works should be the person who checks, not the person who pays.

## What the clerk of works does not do

A clerk of works who is good at the job is tempted to do more of it. The builder's foreman asks whether a bracket can move 50 mm to clear a downpipe, and the clerk of works, who can see at a glance that it is fine, says yes. The answer may be right. It is still a direction given outside the contract, by someone with no authority to give it, and it leaves nobody responsible if the bracket later turns out to clash with something else.

The society's appointment letter therefore lists what the clerk of works does not do:

1. Give directions to the builder. Queries go to the superintendent, who answers in writing.
2. Approve variations or agree prices. The clerk of works can comment on a proposed variation; the committee approves it through the superintendent.
3. Accept work on the society's behalf. Inspections record what was seen; acceptance is the superintendent's.
4. Tell the builder how to do the work safely. Safety on site is the builder's duty; the clerk of works follows the site rules and reports hazards to the builder's site manager.

The list protects the clerk of works as much as the society. A clerk who has given informal approvals has, in effect, taken on part of the design and the builder's responsibility, without the insurance or the authority that go with them.

## Records as the clerk's real product

What survives of Chaucer's working life is almost entirely records: appointments, assignments, indentures, accounts and writs. They outlasted the buildings he repaired. On a modern project the same is true in a smaller way. The grandstand will stand for fifty years. The clerk of works' diary, photographs, inspection records and correspondence are what will answer questions about it in the first five, when defects, warranty claims and disputes arise.

The clerk of works keeps four records from the first day:

| Record | What goes in it | Why it matters later |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Site diary | weather, workforce, plant, deliveries, visitors, events | supports or rebuts claims for delay |
| Photograph register | numbered, dated photos with locations | shows what was covered up and when |
| Inspection record | what was checked, against which drawing and revision, and the result | proves quality, or locates a defect |
| Correspondence register | every instruction, query and response, with dates | shows who decided what |

## Exercise: assess the second progress claim

In week 9 the superintendent assesses the work completed to date at A$312,000 excluding GST. The first payment, made in week 5, was for A$135,000 of work. Using the same 5% retention, calculate the value of work in this claim period, the retention held on it, the amount payable before GST, the GST and the payment. Then state the total retention held by the society after this payment, and list the checks the clerk of works makes before recommending payment.

**Worked answer.** The work in this period is 312,000 − 135,000 = A$177,000. Retention is 177,000 × 5% = A$8,850, so A$168,150 is payable before GST. GST is A$16,815, and the payment is A$184,965. Total retention held after this payment is 6,750 + 8,850 = A$15,600, which is 5% of the A$312,000 completed. Before recommending payment, the clerk of works checks that the amount matches the superintendent's certificate, that the claimed work is visible on site and in the photograph register, that no work in the claim has failed an inspection, that the invoice is a valid tax invoice from the builder, and that the bank details on the payment match those confirmed at the start of the project. The recommendation then goes to the treasurer and president, who approve it with two signatures.
