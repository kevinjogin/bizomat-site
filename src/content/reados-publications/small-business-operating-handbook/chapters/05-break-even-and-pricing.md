Break-even answers a limited but essential question: what sales level is needed to cover the costs included in the model? It is a threshold, not a growth strategy, cash forecast or adequate owner-income target.

## Contribution margin
If a product sells for $100 excluding GST and costs that vary with the sale are $40, contribution is $60. The contribution margin ratio is **$60 ÷ $100 = 60%**. That contribution pays fixed expenses and then contributes to profit.

The usual simplified formula is:

**Break-even revenue = fixed costs ÷ contribution margin ratio.**

For fixed monthly costs of $12,000 and a contribution margin of 60%, break-even revenue is **$12,000 ÷ 0.60 = $20,000**. At that level, variable costs are $8,000, fixed costs are $12,000 and simplified operating profit is zero.

## Units and sales mix
If contribution per unit is $30 and fixed expenses are $9,000, break-even volume is **300 units**. Round up when the result is not a whole unit. For a multi-product business, calculate an appropriate weighted contribution margin using a defensible expected sales mix; if the mix changes, the threshold changes.

## Price discount warning
Suppose an offer sells for $100 with $40 variable cost. Contribution is $60. A discount to $90 with unchanged variable cost cuts contribution to $50, a **16.7% reduction in contribution per sale**, even though the selling price fell by only 10%. You would need 20% more unit sales to generate the same total contribution, assuming costs and mix stay unchanged.

## Capacity also matters
A business cannot sell infinite volume. Consider delivery hours, machine capacity, replenishment time and service quality. An offer with high theoretical margin may still be unattractive if it blocks more valuable work.

## Build a pricing decision sheet
For every offer record **price ex GST | direct variable cost | contribution | capacity used | expected demand | payment terms | returns and rework risk**. Compare a base and alternative price at the same service level. Avoid calculating a margin on GST-inclusive receipts where GST is being remitted.

## Profit target versus break-even
To approximate revenue required for a target operating profit, add that profit target to fixed costs and divide by the contribution ratio. For a target of $3,000 per month with $12,000 fixed expenses and a 60% contribution ratio, required revenue is **($12,000 + $3,000) ÷ 0.60 = $25,000**. Additional tax, finance and owner drawings require separate planning.

## Exercise
Calculate one break-even scenario using real recent expenses. Then stress-test it at contribution margins five percentage points lower and higher. Record which costs are excluded so the result is not mistaken for total cash sufficiency.

*All examples are simplified illustrations, not a substitute for cost accounting or pricing validation.*


## Extended study: applying the method

### Trace each number to its origin

Begin with actual selling prices excluding applicable GST and the expected mix of products or services. For each sale estimate materials, packaging, payment charges, variable freight, commissions and other costs that arise because a sale occurs. Use historical data or supplier quotes and record the period. Next list operating expenses that remain even if sales volume drops substantially, such as committed rent and basic subscriptions. Identify owner economic compensation and financing needs separately. An attractive break-even figure supported by hidden assumptions is less useful than a conservative estimate whose sources can be inspected.

### Contribution is not the same as profit

Suppose an item sells for $250 and consumes $90 of variable costs. Contribution is $160, or 64%. If fixed monthly costs are $8,000, a single-product break-even approximation is $8,000 divided by $160, or 50 units. Selling 50 units produces $12,500 revenue and $4,500 variable cost, leaving $8,000 to cover the fixed expenses. It does not follow that the business has enough cash for VAT or GST settlements, loan principal, stock purchased for future sales or the owner's personal living costs. Keep the scope of the formula beside the result.

### Test the effect of different sales mixes

If product A contributes $30 and product B $80, the break-even number depends on the proportion sold. Assume 70% A and 30% B by units. Weighted average contribution is $45 per unit, so $9,000 fixed costs imply 200 combined units at that mix. If sales shift to 90% A and 10% B, weighted contribution falls to $35; the same fixed costs require approximately 258 combined units, rounding up. The forecast should consider whether customers actually buy the assumed mix and whether production capacity supports it.

### Discounts can create hidden volume pressure

For a $100 product with $40 variable cost, contribution is $60. A 10% price reduction to $90 reduces contribution to $50, or by one sixth. At the original price, 100 units contribute $6,000. At the discounted price, 120 units are required to contribute the same $6,000, assuming no added fixed costs and no change in variable cost. Those additional 20 units may consume labour and working capital. Promotional prices should specify objective, quantity limit, duration, evidence to measure and a stop condition.

### Cash break-even and operational break-even

Accounting break-even does not automatically cover depreciation differences, debt principal, new equipment, owner withdrawals or tax payments. A practical cash target should add the necessary cash commitments not already represented in the contribution model, while avoiding double counting expenses. A business can also face an operational capacity constraint: 200 orders might cover fixed costs mathematically but require 240 production hours when only 160 are available. Model staffing, machinery and delivery capacity alongside sales. The relevant question is not merely how much needs to sell, but whether that volume can be delivered reliably and collected in time.

### Sensitivity and margin-of-safety review

Calculate break-even at realistic contribution ratios, not only the most favourable one. If fixed costs are $15,000, break-even is $25,000 revenue at 60% contribution, $30,000 at 50% and $37,500 at 40%. These differences show why uncontrolled rework, material escalation and discounts deserve attention. Margin of safety compares expected revenue with break-even revenue; express it in currency or as a proportion of expected sales. A narrow margin means modest forecast error could remove profit. Do not present these hypothetical examples as industry-standard margins.

### Repeatable pricing approval sheet

For every proposed quotation, record scope, promised time, cost sources, variable expense estimate, planned contribution, volume assumptions, payment terms, exclusions, currency and exchange exposure if relevant, approval person and revision date. For custom work, include a change-order mechanism. After delivery, compare the estimate against actual resource use and rework. Feed that evidence into the next quote. Good pricing is a feedback system: estimates become orders, orders reveal cost, and results correct future estimates.

## Implementation laboratory: quote quality and contribution margins

### Define a realistic starting point

Before changing anything, create a baseline record dated at the beginning of the exercise. State what is currently happening, who owns the process, what happens when it fails, and what evidence is available. For this topic, give special attention to **discounted orders consuming scarce production capacity**. A single recent incident is not necessarily a representative trend, so look for several examples where available. If the process is entirely new, say that there is no observed baseline and use a synthetic rehearsal rather than describing assumptions as history.

Write a one-sentence objective in the form: "Over the next four weeks we want to improve [measurable outcome] while maintaining [quality or safety requirement]." Avoid using a vague outcome such as working harder, being more professional or creating growth. A useful objective contains an observable difference and a control that prevents apparent efficiency from concealing new problems.

### Identify the people and decision boundaries

Consider the different responsibilities of the **sales, estimator, production and approver**. In a microbusiness one individual may hold several roles, but those roles should still be conceptually separated. Identify who prepares the information, who checks it, who approves a change, who carries it out and who records the result. If outside advice is required, identify the point at which the process must pause for that advice. Avoid giving software or an AI assistant implicit authority to make legal, financial or employment commitments.

Prepare a short responsibility table with the columns *Input owner*, *Process owner*, *Independent check*, *Final approver* and *Backup contact*. For a sole trader, the independent check may be a periodic external professional review instead of a second employee. The objective is accountability, not artificial complexity.

### Build a small evidence package

An evidence package should contain the current procedure, one or more real or simulated examples, relevant figures, the expected output and a list of known exceptions. Remove confidential personal information from teaching materials. For a real business, store operational evidence in an approved private location with access limited to the people who need it. Link each figure to its source instead of copying it manually between documents without a reference.

Separate observed facts from forecasts and estimates. An observed payment date is a fact; a future customer's payment is an assumption, even when it is a reasonable one. A change in accounting classification or business circumstances may alter which facts are comparable. Mark missing information explicitly and assign responsibility for collecting it.

### Work through an exception rather than only the easy case

Normal scenarios are useful for learning the steps; exceptions test the quality of the process. Imagine that **discounted orders consuming scarce production capacity** occurs just before an important customer or statutory deadline. Which facts would you need immediately? Which decisions can be made under the existing authority? Which tasks could be paused safely? How would the customer, supplier, employee or adviser be informed where appropriate? What written record would be necessary to explain the response later?

Run the situation twice. First follow the present procedure. Then follow the improved procedure and compare clarity, elapsed time, error risk and confidence in the information. Note where the new approach merely moves work to another person. An effective improvement should not reduce one person's workload by hiding costs or risk elsewhere.

### Choose a review metric and define it precisely

The principal indicator for this exercise is **contribution per unit and hours of constrained capacity**. State the numerator, denominator, measurement period and data source where applicable. Decide what level of movement would be meaningful before seeing the results. Pair a speed or output measure with at least one quality indicator. For example, a process that closes actions more quickly but increases uncorrected mistakes has not clearly improved.

Review results using a small table: *Period*, *Baseline or target*, *Actual*, *Difference*, *Reason*, *Corrective action* and *Owner*. Do not infer causation from a single good week. Demand, seasonality, staff absence or unusual orders can affect outcomes independently of the process change.

### Put an approval gate before an irreversible action

The major decision boundary is **special pricing or acceptance of loss-making work**. Before crossing it, prepare a concise decision note: the available options, evidence, total cash exposure, dependencies, legal or customer obligations, reversibility, downside risk and the person authorised to approve. If information is missing, a smaller pilot may be preferable to a permanent commitment. Refusing to invent an uncertain input is a sign of a trustworthy process.

Agree a review date and an exit rule. An exit rule might limit an experiment to a fixed amount of money, a defined sample size or a certain number of weeks. The rule should be proportionate to the risk rather than copied from another business. Keep written approval evidence for consequential commitments and use specialist advice where required.

### Complete a four-week practice cycle

**First week:** Describe the present method and collect baseline evidence. Do not redesign everything at once. Check whether the records can support **contribution per unit and hours of constrained capacity** and choose one narrow intervention.

**Second week:** Create the new procedure, checklist or template. Test it on a simple example and on a difficult exception. Document each correction and identify whether missing inputs or ambiguous responsibility caused the problem.

**Third week:** Conduct a limited live pilot where appropriate and authorised. An example of a suitably narrow outcome for this topic is **defining change orders and minimum viable quote margins**. Measure time, quality and unintended consequences. Keep the ability to return to the prior process if the pilot fails.

**Fourth week:** Compare results, collect feedback from the people affected and write a decision: adopt, refine, retest or stop. Assign an owner and future review date. If no clear benefit appears, keep the evidence but do not claim success. The purpose of a pilot is to learn accurately, not to defend the original idea.

### Reflection and transfer

Explain the principle to someone who has not read the chapter, without using jargon. Ask them to reproduce the calculation, review or process from your instructions. Any point requiring your verbal rescue is an opportunity to improve the documentation. Then consider where the same logic applies in another process: setting clear inputs, acknowledging uncertainty, naming decision rights, measuring outcomes and checking the effect on both money and people.

**Completion evidence:** a dated baseline, one-page procedure, exception test, recorded results and approved next action. These five records convert general business advice into a verifiable operating improvement.
