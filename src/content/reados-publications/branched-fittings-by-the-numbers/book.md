## About this book

A duct that must serve two machines, a chute that must feed two bins, a manifold that must collect from three hoods: each needs a fitting that splits one round section into several. The simplest is a tee, a branch pipe cut to sit on the side of the main. The smoothest is a fitting that divides the main gradually into prongs, each prong turning and shrinking towards its own duct, and meeting its neighbour along a crotch. Those fittings, called breeches, forks, wyes or manifolds, are some of the most satisfying work in sheet metal and some of the most feared.

This book develops them with numbers. It starts with the oblique cone, which is the building block of most branched fittings, and its frustum. It then develops two-pronged fittings with parallel ends, prongs at an angle, unequal prongs, prongs at different angles and fittings with three prongs, and ends with a round pipe joining an oblique cone and with making and checking the set. Along the way it proves a useful result: when every prong of a fitting is a cone standing on the main's circle, every crotch lies in a plane, and the hardest line in the fitting can be found exactly. Each chapter calculates with one running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for sheet metal workers, ductwork and chute fabricators, apprentices and drafters, and for engineers who check branched fittings. It assumes school algebra and trigonometry and the basics of pattern development. The Library’s Sheet Metal Patterns by the Numbers covers tees and branches on round pipe, and Transitional Elbows by the Numbers covers the coordinate method and true lengths in more detail.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional job. This is an illustration. A ductwork fabricator is making the fittings for a fume-extraction system, called FX-4 on its drawings, in a steel fabrication shop with two welding bays. The system takes fume from wall hoods in each bay, combines the branches, and runs a Ø355 mm main to a filter unit. Its branched fittings are:

- **G1**, a wall hood that tapers from a Ø600 mm skirt to a Ø250 mm duct, flat on the wall side, an oblique cone frustum;
- **G2**, a breeches from the Ø355 main to two Ø250 branches with ends parallel to the main's;
- **G3**, a breeches from the Ø355 main to two Ø250 branches angled 30° outward;
- **G4**, a breeches with unequal prongs, Ø300 and Ø200, for bays of different sizes;
- **G5**, a wye with one Ø300 prong straight on and one Ø200 prong at 45°;
- **G6**, a three-way manifold from a Ø400 duct to three Ø230 branches;
- **G7**, a Ø150 pipe joining the side of G1's hood.

The fittings are made in 0.8 mm galvanised steel sheet. The fabricator, the shop, the dimensions and the mistakes are invented. None describes KEVOS® work or a real business.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 to 3 first: they set out branched fittings, the oblique cone and its frustum, which every later chapter uses. Chapters 4 to 8 develop the breeches and manifolds in order of difficulty, and each relies on the one before. Chapter 9 deals with a pipe meeting a cone, and Chapter 10 with allowances, assembly, checks and the material list. Values are given to 0.1 mm so that the arithmetic can be followed; patterns are usually marked to the nearest half millimetre. Every number in the book was calculated by running it.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The cone patterns in this book are exact, and the triangulated prongs are as accurate as their divisions allow. Seam, lap and flange allowances are illustrations, to be calibrated with trial pieces in the shop's own sheet. Fume-extraction systems must be designed for their duty and for the hazards of the fume they carry, and the balance of flow between branches is a matter for the system's designer. This book teaches pattern geometry. It is not a design for any system, and qualified review governs real work.
