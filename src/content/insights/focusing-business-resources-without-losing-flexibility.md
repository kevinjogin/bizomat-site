Small businesses often face more plausible opportunities than they can pursue properly. A new customer group, a product extension, a marketing channel and an internal improvement may each look worthwhile. Starting all of them can leave every effort short of the attention it needs to produce a useful result.

The strategic principles in Brian Tracy's *Business Strategy* put weight on concentrating resources, taking initiative, adapting to change and acting on an advantage. Applied to a small business, these ideas create a useful tension: commit enough to learn and deliver well while preserving the ability to change course. The practical approach below interprets those principles through customer needs and operating capacity, with explicit limits on how far an early success should be extended.

## Make focus a choice about resources

**Strategic focus** means directing a meaningful share of limited resources towards a defined objective or opportunity. It becomes visible in the calendar, the work accepted, the improvements funded and the opportunities deferred. A broad statement about being customer focused does not, on its own, establish any of those choices.

The scarce resource may be the founder's attention, a specialist's time, access to equipment or the ability to support customers after delivery. Identify that resource before choosing how many initiatives to run. A business with spare production capacity can still be constrained by quoting, design or installation work.

A **bottleneck** is the part of a process that limits the flow of work through the whole. Sending more demand towards an unchanged bottleneck may create a longer queue rather than more completed work. A focused strategy might therefore improve one delivery step before seeking additional customers.

Tracy's emphasis on concentration is useful here: scattered effort can prevent a business from becoming consistently good at anything important. For a small team, the practical question is whether the chosen opportunity receives enough sustained attention to produce evidence and a dependable customer outcome.

## Define the customer problem narrowly enough

A **customer segment** is a group of potential buyers with relevant needs or circumstances in common. A **niche** is a more specific part of a market that a business chooses to address. Useful boundaries come from the problem and buying situation, as well as from an industry label.

For example, all small businesses is too broad to guide most product decisions. Businesses with limited storage space and frequently changing stock may share a more concrete problem. Their requirements can shape dimensions, setup, ordering information and support in ways that a broad demographic description cannot.

Describe the situation, the current workaround and the consequence of the problem. Then ask whether the people in that situation recognise it and are willing to change their behaviour to solve it. Being able to imagine a specialised offer does not establish that there is enough demand to sustain one.

A narrow starting point also helps define exclusions. An early product might suit one operating environment and be unsuitable for another. Stating the boundary lets the team learn what it can reliably deliver and avoids treating every unusual request as an immediate product requirement.

## Find a difference customers can use

**Competitive advantage** is a reason customers choose an offer that a business can deliver better, more reliably or more economically than relevant alternatives. It must matter to the customer. A technically unusual feature may add complexity without improving the decision to buy or the experience of use.

Tracy's discussion of surprise encourages looking beyond familiar ways of competing. A practical interpretation is to search for a useful difference that alternatives overlook. Clearer setup instructions, fewer ordering errors or compatibility with an existing workflow may matter more than a large set of additional features.

Study alternatives broadly. A potential buyer might compare the proposed product with a spreadsheet, a manual process, an existing supplier or doing nothing. Understanding those options helps identify the actual effort and uncertainty involved in changing.

Write the proposed advantage as a claim that could be checked. Easier to use is vague. A first-time user can complete a defined setup task without assistance is more observable. The second formulation suggests what to test, who should participate and what evidence would challenge the claim.

Do not assume that a useful difference will remain unique. Consider whether consistent delivery, accumulated knowledge or a well-designed process could make it easier to sustain. The immediate aim is to deliver a benefit reliably; the longer question is how the business will keep improving it as alternatives change.

## Concentrate learning before making a large commitment

A new business may not yet know which niche deserves attention. In that situation, concentration can apply to the next investigation rather than to a permanent commercial position. Give a clearly defined question enough effort to answer it before opening several more.

An **experiment** is a deliberate test of an uncertain assumption, with an observation that will inform a decision. Useful experiments specify the question, the participants or conditions, the time and resource limit, and what result would justify the next step.

Suppose the assumption is that customers need faster setup. A prototype trial can reveal where users get stuck. It cannot, by itself, establish how much they will pay, whether the product can be made consistently or how much support a wider release will require. Keep those questions separate even when one trial informs several of them.

The practical distinction is between exploring options and attempting to build all of them. [Why GoCore is starting broad](/insights/why-gocore-is-starting-broad/) describes the reasoning behind a period of deliberate exploration. A founder can keep several possibilities open while concentrating current effort on a small number of tests with clear end points.

## Preserve room to adapt

**Strategic flexibility** is the ability to change the route or allocation of resources when new information warrants it. It depends partly on how commitments are designed. A short trial with reusable materials is easier to revise than a large build that only works for one assumed customer need.

Distinguish reversible decisions from commitments that are difficult to unwind. A mock-up, a limited test or a manual process may be appropriate while the question remains uncertain. Larger investments may become reasonable after demand, delivery requirements and likely support effort are better understood.

This does not mean choosing the cheapest option at every stage. A low-cost shortcut can prevent a valid test if it removes the feature that customers need to evaluate. Match the commitment to the question: spend enough to produce useful evidence, then avoid adding polish or capacity that the decision does not yet require.

Reserve some time for unexpected problems and for interpreting results. A schedule filled to its theoretical limit leaves no practical room for adaptation. The size of that reserve should follow the uncertainty and workload of the business; there is no universal percentage that makes every plan resilient.

## Set decision rules before enthusiasm builds

A **decision rule** is an agreed condition for continuing, changing, expanding or stopping an initiative. It helps keep the response tied to evidence when people have invested effort and become attached to the original idea.

Use several kinds of evidence. Customer behaviour indicates whether the problem and proposed improvement matter. Delivery evidence indicates whether the business can fulfil the promise. Resource evidence shows whether the work fits available capacity. A positive result in one area does not automatically resolve the others.

| Evidence observed | Possible response | Further check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Users value the benefit but struggle with setup | Improve setup within the same focus | Does the revised process remove the difficulty? |
| Interest is polite but no practical use follows | Revisit the need or customer group | Was the trial realistic and relevant? |
| Delivery requires repeated exceptions | Limit expansion and examine the process | Can the work be made repeatable? |
| Use and delivery both hold up | Consider a bounded next stage | What becomes harder at the next volume? |
| The original need has changed | Reconsider the objective | Does the opportunity still deserve priority? |

Treat these responses as reasoning prompts. The same observation can have different explanations, and a small trial may be inconclusive. An honest decision rule allows another focused test when the evidence is genuinely insufficient, with a reason and a limit.

## Worked example: choosing what deserves the next month

This is an illustration. A two-person Australian product business is exploring practical storage accessories. Its founders can allocate 80 hours over the next four weeks to development and testing after allowing for their other commitments. They are considering a compact organiser, an adjustable mounting accessory and a customised range.

Each option has a plausible customer story, but none has enough evidence to justify a broad release. Dividing 80 hours equally would provide about 26.7 hours per option. The founders estimate that each credible initial test needs 40 hours, including preparation, observation and review, so equal allocation would leave all three tests under-resourced.

They choose the compact organiser for the first focused test because they have access to people who encounter the relevant storage problem and can observe the existing workaround. This is a reason to prioritise learning, not a claim that the organiser is already the best commercial opportunity.

| Use of available time | Hours |
| --- | ---: |
| Observe the problem and confirm trial requirements | 16 |
| Build and prepare the trial units | 24 |
| Observe trials and collect feedback | 16 |
| Review findings and choose the next step | 8 |
| Reserve for unexpected work | 16 |
| Total | 80 |

The planned activities use 64 hours and the reserve accounts for the remaining 16. The founders defer both other concepts for the period and record what evidence would justify returning to them. This protects the test from being diluted by informal work on the alternatives.

The trial asks whether people can configure the organiser for a defined use without assistance and whether they choose to keep using it during the trial. It also records setup difficulties and requests outside the intended scope. The founders do not treat positive comments as equivalent to continued use or a purchase decision.

Suppose users repeatedly struggle with the same adjustment but otherwise use the organiser as intended. The next action is a bounded change to that adjustment, followed by another observation. It would be premature to interpret the difficulty as proof that the entire market is wrong, just as it would be premature to ignore the difficulty and prepare a larger release.

Now suppose the revised version works in use, but making each unit requires frequent hand correction. The customer test is encouraging while the delivery test remains unresolved. The founders can keep the chosen focus and redirect the next block of effort towards repeatable manufacture, with a new limit and review condition.

## Expand a working approach in manageable steps

Tracy argues for acting promptly when an advantage becomes available. For a small business, that principle needs to be considered alongside delivery capacity and uncertainty. A promising result deserves a timely decision, but the size of the next commitment should reflect what the result actually establishes.

**Expansion** means increasing the volume, scope or reach of an activity. These are different changes. More orders of an unchanged product, a new product variant and entry into another customer group can each introduce different requirements. Increasing all three together makes it harder to understand what causes any resulting difficulty.

Prefer a next stage that tests the most important remaining constraint. If production is the uncertainty, a modestly larger batch may reveal more than a broader marketing campaign. If support across distance is uncertain, a controlled trial in another location may be more informative than adding features.

Check the full chain from enquiry to ongoing use. An increase in completed units may create more packaging, dispatch, customer questions and replacement work. A process that appears efficient at the workbench may depend on unpaid or unrecorded effort elsewhere.

Keep existing commitments visible during the transition. Allocating every available hour to the new opportunity can weaken the service or reliability that made the business credible in the first place. The capacity decision should include those obligations rather than treating them as background work someone will somehow absorb.

## Coordinate the work around the promise

Focus needs cooperation across the tasks that shape a customer's experience. An offer promising simple ordering cannot be delivered by marketing language alone if product choices, order information and fulfilment still require repeated clarification.

Translate the proposed advantage into requirements for each part of the process. The person describing the offer needs accurate boundaries. The person preparing the product needs complete information. The person handling questions needs to understand the intended use and the exceptions the business can support.

A small team may have one person covering several of these roles. The connections still matter. Writing down the sequence can expose a gap that is hidden when the work lives entirely in the founder's memory.

Review the points where work changes hands. A clear completion condition at each stage helps prevent a local improvement from creating a problem somewhere else. Faster quoting is only helpful if the resulting orders contain what delivery requires.

## Applying focus to an Australian small business

Geography can change the scope of a useful focus. A product that works well for nearby users may face different packaging, freight or support demands when supplied over longer distances. Treat those as conditions to investigate rather than assuming that local success establishes national readiness.

Limited access to specialist skills or equipment can also shape the sequence. If a prototype depends on an occasional production slot, coordinate customer observation and design decisions around that constraint. Starting more concepts will not create more access to the scarce resource.

For a solo founder, separate exploration time from the work required to maintain ordinary commitments. A clear decision to spend the next available block on one uncertainty can be valuable even when the block is modest. Focus is about the consistency of the allocation, not the size of the organisation.

Keep customer concentration in view as well. Serving a well-defined problem is different from depending on one buyer. If one participant dominates the early feedback, investigate whether the need is shared before turning that participant's preferences into the whole product direction.

## Common mistakes when choosing a focus

One mistake is to narrow around an industry label without understanding the actual buying situation. A niche description can sound precise while combining customers with very different needs. Use observed problems and requirements to test whether the proposed group belongs together.

Another is to use flexibility as a reason to switch direction whenever a new idea appears. Changing the route after learning something material is different from abandoning a test before it has answered its question. Record what changed in the evidence when making a change in priority.

A third is to confuse being unusual with being useful. A surprising product may attract attention without solving a problem that matters. Examine what people do with it, what they give up to adopt it and whether the benefit persists after the novelty passes.

Finally, avoid making expansion the automatic response to any positive signal. A successful demonstration, a workable delivery process and a sustainable business each require different evidence. Build the next stage around the unanswered question that could most affect the decision.

## Questions to ask

- Which resource currently limits our ability to make progress?
- What specific customer problem deserves concentrated attention?
- Which useful difference can we demonstrate rather than merely describe?
- What will we defer while this test or commitment is active?
- What evidence would justify continuing, changing or stopping?
- Which new constraint would appear if the next stage succeeded?

## Bringing it together

Focus gives a worthwhile opportunity enough attention to develop. Flexibility preserves the ability to respond when the evidence changes. Both become practical through bounded commitments, visible trade-offs and decisions tied to customer behaviour and delivery performance.

Concentrate on a question or promise you can test properly, then use what you learn to decide the next allocation. When an advantage appears, develop it at a pace the business can support and keep checking the assumptions that made it worth pursuing.
