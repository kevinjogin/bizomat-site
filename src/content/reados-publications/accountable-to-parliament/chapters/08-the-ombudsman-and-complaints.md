A complaint asks an agency to account for one decision to one person. Some complaints go on to the Commonwealth Ombudsman, who can examine the agency's administration from outside, require documents and answers, and report on what the agency did wrong. By then, how the agency handled the complaint the first time is part of what is examined.

This is an illustration. In July 2027 the applicant ranked 32nd in Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) complained to NRIO. It had narrowly missed the funding line, then watched the Minister fund two applications ranked below it, both against officials' recommendations. In August it took its complaint to the Ombudsman. This chapter follows it, including one reply NRIO would rather not have sent.

## Complaints start at the agency

The agency should be the first place a complaint is resolved: it holds the records and can put right what went wrong. The [Ombudsman Act 1976](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A01611/latest/text) builds this in. Where a person has not complained to the agency first, the Ombudsman may decide not to investigate until they have (s 6(1A)). Where they have, the Ombudsman may wait until the person says the agency gave no redress, or not enough (s 6(1B)).

The Act does not tell an agency how to run its own complaints process, and neither do the other laws in this book. What follows is better practice, so read it as "should". An agency should acknowledge a complaint quickly, with a date for a full reply, and give it to someone who had no part in the decision. The reply should come from the record: what was decided, by whom and on what basis, and what can and cannot be changed. It should give the person their own information and nobody else's, and set out the options if they are still not satisfied, including the Ombudsman.

This is an illustration. NRIO's complaints coordinator, who had no part in the round, received the complaint on Monday 5 July 2027, acknowledged it the next day and promised a full reply by Monday 19 July. The reply went that day. It explained that the Minister decides RCFP grants and may approve projects officials did not recommend, and that such decisions are reported and published. It gave the applicant its own scores, rank and reasons, and the review options. It did not guess at the Minister's reasons, apologise for the decision or promise anything for Round 2.

This is an illustration. The ANAO later found that complaints about Round 1 had arrived through several channels and were not recorded in one place, and recommended that they be (Chapter 6).

## What the Ombudsman investigates

Subject to the Act, the Ombudsman "shall investigate action, being action that relates to a matter of administration", taken by a Department or a prescribed authority, when someone complains about it, and may investigate such action on the Ombudsman's own motion (s 5(1)). "Prescribed authority" is defined in section 3. This is an illustration. The case treats NRIO as one, so its administration is within the Ombudsman's reach.

That qualification matters. Section 6 lets the Ombudsman decline to investigate, or stop, in several cases: for example, where the complainant became aware of the action more than 12 months before complaining (s 6(1)(a)), or where an investigation is not warranted in all the circumstances (s 6(1)(b)(iii)).

Before deciding, the Ombudsman may make preliminary inquiries of the agency's principal officer, to work out whether the Ombudsman is authorised to investigate, or whether to decline (s 7A). A preliminary inquiry is the agency's first chance to show its records and its attitude. Treat it with the same care as a formal notice.

This is an illustration. The applicant learned the outcome in June 2027 and went to the Ombudsman in August, well inside 12 months, after complaining to NRIO first. In any case, whether to investigate was the Ombudsman's decision, not NRIO's.

## What the Ombudsman cannot investigate

Section 5(2) lists action the Ombudsman is not authorised to investigate. Three items matter most to an agency like NRIO:

- "action taken by a Minister" (s 5(2)(a));
- "action that constitutes proceedings in Parliament" for the purposes of section 16 of the Parliamentary Privileges Act 1987 (s 5(2)(aa));
- action about a person's employment in the Australian Public Service, such as promotion, termination of appointment, discipline or pay (s 5(2)(d)).

The list has other items, about judges, magistrates, intelligence agencies and some appointments.

The limit on Ministers is narrower than it looks. Action by a delegate of a Minister is not action by the Minister (s 5(3)). And an agency's action is not treated as the Minister's merely because it relates to something the Minister has done, proposes to do or may do personally (s 5(3A)).

This is an illustration. The Minister's decisions on Monday 31 May 2027, 31 grants worth A$9.98 million with 2 approved against officials' recommendations, were action taken by a Minister, so the Ombudsman cannot investigate them. NRIO's assessment of the 177 eligible applications, its ranking, its advice to the Minister, its records, its letters and its complaint handling were NRIO's own action, even though all of them relate to those decisions. The Ombudsman can investigate every one. The CEO's evidence at Estimates on Wednesday 2 June 2027 was "the giving of evidence before a House or a committee", part of proceedings in Parliament under section 16(2)(a) of the Privileges Act, so it is outside too.

## How an investigation runs

Before investigating, the Ombudsman must tell the agency's principal officer (s 8(1)). An investigation "shall be conducted in private", in whatever manner the Ombudsman thinks fit (s 8(2)), and the Ombudsman may make any inquiries the Ombudsman thinks fit (s 8(3)).

The Ombudsman must not report opinions critical of an agency unless the principal officer and the officer principally concerned have had opportunities "to appear" and make submissions before the investigation ends (s 8(5)). And if the Ombudsman proposes to require information under section 9 and has not yet told the responsible Minister about the investigation, the Ombudsman must do so (s 8(7A)(b)).

Section 9 is the formal power. By written notice, the Ombudsman may require a person to give information in writing signed by them, to produce documents, or to attend and answer questions, within the period or on the date the notice sets (s 9(1) and (2)). The usual reasons for holding back do not apply. A person is not excused because complying would breach another law, might incriminate them, would disclose legal advice given to an agency, or "would be otherwise contrary to the public interest" (s 9(4)). The Act does provide for an Attorney-General's certificate on matters such as security or Cabinet deliberations (s 9(3)); an agency's wish to keep something quiet is not one of them. Refusing or failing to comply when required is an offence, unless the person has a reasonable excuse (s 36).

The date in a notice is the Ombudsman's. If you cannot meet it, ask for more time before it passes.

## The duty to assist

Section 32 was added to the Act in 2025. Section 32(1) says the principal officer of a Department or prescribed authority "must ensure" that it uses its best endeavours to assist the Ombudsman. Section 32(2) puts the duty on every officer, who "must use the officer's best endeavours to assist the Ombudsman in the performance of the Ombudsman's functions."

Both are "must". The Act does not define "best endeavours", so ask how your response would look to someone reading the file later. The Code of Conduct applies too. A request from the Ombudsman is a request for information made for official purposes, and section 13(9) of the Public Service Act says an APS employee "must not provide false or misleading information" in response to one.

## Reports and what follows

When an investigation of a complaint is complete, the Ombudsman must give the complainant and the agency particulars of it (s 12(3)).

Section 15 applies where the Ombudsman forms the opinion that the agency's action, for example, "appears to have been contrary to law", was "unreasonable, unjust, oppressive or improperly discriminatory", was based on a mistake of law or fact, or "was otherwise, in all the circumstances, wrong" (s 15(1)(a)). It also applies where reasons should have been given but were not (s 15(1)(c)(ii)). If the Ombudsman also thinks something should be done, such as rectifying the effects or changing a practice, the Ombudsman must report to the agency (s 15(2)). The report must give reasons and may include recommendations (s 15(3)). The Ombudsman may ask the agency what it proposes to do, within a specified time (s 15(4)), and the agency may comment on the report (s 15(5)). The Minister gets a copy (s 15(6)).

If adequate and appropriate action is not taken within a reasonable time, the Ombudsman may inform the Prime Minister (s 16(1)), and may then send a report to both Houses, including the agency's comments (s 17). The Ombudsman may also make a public statement in the public interest, but not one critical of an agency that has not had its section 8(5) opportunity (s 35A).

Three practices follow. Treat an Ombudsman's recommendations like audit recommendations, with agreed actions in the register (Chapter 6). Write any comments under section 15(5) with Parliament in mind, because a report to both Houses must include them. And a non-corporate entity's annual report must give particulars of any report on its operations given by the Ombudsman during the year, with its response (PGPA Rule s 17AG(3)).

## When it goes wrong: a late, defensive reply

This is an illustration. The Ombudsman's letter of Tuesday 17 August 2027 asked the CEO three things by Tuesday 31 August: for NRIO's complaint file, how the applicant's application had been assessed and ranked, and whether NRIO had advised the Minister about the two applications funded against its recommendations.

Because the letter was about a complaint, it went to the complaints coordinator, who asked Regional Programs for the facts. Nobody put its date in the unit's register of deadlines, and Regional Programs was busy with the inquiry submission, lodged on Thursday 2 September (Chapter 4).

The reply went on Tuesday 7 September, signed for the CEO by the Director, Regional Programs: 7 days late, with no request for more time. It ran to one page. It said the complaint was "in substance about the Minister's decision", which the Ombudsman is not authorised to investigate, and described it as an attempt to reopen a lawful decision. It attached the July reply and nothing else, and did not answer the second or third question.

The cost came quickly. The legal point was wrong: NRIO's assessment, advice and complaint handling were its own action, whatever their link to the Minister's decisions (s 5(3A)). On Wednesday 15 September the Ombudsman told the CEO it would investigate and served a section 9 notice for what NRIO had not given, and more. Because a section 9 requirement was proposed, the Minister was told of the investigation (s 8(7A)(b)). NRIO had sent the Minister's office no alert, and the office asked why. The work now had to fit around the annual report, due to the Minister on Friday 15 October, and the inquiry hearing on Thursday 21 October. And the letter stayed on the Ombudsman's file.

Whether that letter met the duty to use best endeavours to assist (s 32) is a question nobody at NRIO wanted to be asked.

Nobody meant to obstruct anything. A dated letter went to a busy area, outside the system that tracks dates, and was answered by the people whose work was in question. NRIO changed three things:

- all correspondence from the Ombudsman goes to Clare's unit the day it arrives, and its date goes in the register with the committee deadlines;
- the program area drafts the facts, the unit checks every statement against the record and against what NRIO has already said, and the CEO signs;
- if a date cannot be met, the unit asks for more time before it passes.

## Exercise: NRIO's response to the notice

This is an illustration. The section 9 notice, dated Wednesday 15 September 2027 and addressed to the CEO, requires the following by Wednesday 6 October 2027:

1. all records of the assessment of the applicant's application, including scores, moderation notes and its rank;
2. the brief to the Minister recommending grants, with the ranked list and all attachments;
3. any record of advice NRIO gave the Minister or the Minister's office, between the brief and the decisions, about the two applications approved against officials' recommendations;
4. the decision record signed on Monday 31 May 2027;
5. NRIO's file on the complaint, including the reply of 19 July and the letter of 7 September;
6. a written statement of how NRIO decides what to tell unsuccessful applicants about grants approved against officials' recommendations.

Plan NRIO's response. Check the date, set a timetable, name a source and owner for each item, outline the covering letter, and say who signs and what else NRIO should do.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

**The date.** The notice allows 21 days. September has 15 days left after the 15th (16 to 30 September), and 21 − 15 = 6, so the date is Wednesday 6 October 2027. Clare plans to send on Friday 1 October, five days early, so that a question from the CEO or a missing record does not put the date at risk. If one item cannot be found in time, she asks for more time for that item before 6 October and sends the rest.

**Each item.**

1. From the grants system: the applicant's scores, moderation record and rank, 32nd of the 177 applications assessed. Owner: Sam Reid.
2. The brief and ranked list exactly as sent, with every attachment. Owner: Sam Reid.
3. Whatever the file holds, as it is. A file note made after the event goes with the date it was written and says when the contact took place. If a contact was not recorded, the response says so. Nobody creates, rewrites or backdates a record to fill a gap. Owner: the Director, Regional Programs.
4. The decision record. The Minister's decisions are outside the investigation (s 5(2)(a)), but the record bears on NRIO's advice and letters, and the Ombudsman has asked for it. Owner: Sam Reid.
5. The complaint file, including the 7 September letter. Owner: the complaints coordinator.
6. A short statement of what unsuccessful applicants are told, where approvals against advice are published, and who decides what goes in a letter. It describes the practice and does not defend the Minister's decisions. Owner: the complaints coordinator, cleared by the Director, Regional Programs.

**The consistency check.** Every number must match what NRIO has already told Parliament: 186 applications received, 9 ineligible and 186 − 9 = 177 assessed, as in the CEO's correcting letter of Friday 4 June 2027; 31 grants worth A$9.98 million, 2 of them approved against officials' recommendations. Clare checks the response against that letter, the answers to the questions on notice and the inquiry submission. A response that said "all 186 assessed" would repeat the error the CEO corrected.

**The covering letter.** It answers each numbered item in order, lists every attachment, names Clare as NRIO's contact and offers to meet. It acknowledges that the 7 September reply was late and withdraws its statement that the complaint was in substance about the Minister's decision, because NRIO's own administration is a matter for the Ombudsman (s 5(3A)). It does not characterise the applicant, speculate about the Minister's reasons, or hold anything back because it is sensitive (s 9(4)).

**Who signs.** The CEO. The notice is addressed to the CEO, and information under section 9(1)(a) is given in writing signed by the person required to give it. The CEO is also the principal officer who must ensure NRIO uses its best endeavours to assist (s 32(1)).

**What else.** The Minister's office gets its alert on day one. And Clare notes for the 2027–28 annual report that any report the Ombudsman gives on NRIO during the year must appear there, with NRIO's response (PGPA Rule s 17AG(3)).
