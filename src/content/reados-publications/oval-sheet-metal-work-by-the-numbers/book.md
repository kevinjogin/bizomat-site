## About this book

An oval is the shape a round vessel takes when it must be longer than it is wide: a trough that fits along a fence, a bath that fits a person lying down, a duct that fits a shallow ceiling, a hood over a long machine. Ovals look like ellipses, and on a drawing they are often called ellipses, but most oval sheet metal work is not elliptical at all. It is a **four-centre oval**: two tight arcs at the ends and two flat arcs along the sides, each struck from its own centre and meeting its neighbours smoothly. That construction is what makes ovals practical in sheet metal, because every part of it is a circle, and every part of a flared oval is part of a cone.

This book works through oval work with numbers. It starts with the shapes and how to construct a four-centre oval exactly, then calculates girths, areas and capacities, develops a flared oval vessel as a set of part cones, places its seams and makes its bottom and rim, deals with flat ovals, and finishes with material, nesting and checks. Each chapter calculates with one running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for sheet metal workers, fabricators, apprentices and drafters, and for engineers who check oval vessels and ducts. It assumes school algebra and trigonometry and some familiarity with round pattern development; READOS's Sheet Metal Patterns by the Numbers covers radial line development of cones, which this book builds on.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional job. This is an illustration. A rural fabricator has been asked for galvanised steel stock-water troughs, oval in plan so that cattle can drink along both sides and the trough sits along a fence line. Each trough is 1,800 × 900 mm across the top and 450 mm deep, measured on the mean line of 1.6 mm galvanised sheet, with walls flaring outward by 75 mm all round, so the bottom is 1,650 × 750 mm. The oval is a four-centre oval with an end radius of 300 mm at the top. The walls are made in four pieces, two ends and two sides, joined by vertical seams; the bottom is a flat oval plate; the rim is rolled over a steel rod.

The fabricator, the dimensions, the prices and the mistakes are invented. None describes KEVOS® work or a real business.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first: they set out the four-centre oval and its construction, which every later chapter uses. Chapters 3 and 4 deal with lengths and volumes, Chapters 5 to 7 with the flared vessel's patterns and joints, and Chapters 8 to 10 with flat ovals, material and checks. Values are given to 0.1 mm so that the arithmetic can be followed; oval work is usually marked to the nearest half millimetre.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The geometry in this book is exact, and every calculation was checked by running it. Seam, rim and bottom allowances are illustrations, to be calibrated with trial pieces. A trough full of water is heavy: the running case's holds about 440 kg of water to its fill line, and a vessel of that kind must be designed and supported for its full load and for the animals or people around it. Drinking-water and stock-water vessels also have material and coating requirements that depend on their use. This book teaches layout and fabrication geometry. It is not a design for any vessel, and qualified review governs real work.
