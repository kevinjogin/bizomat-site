On most building, fit-out and installation projects, one date matters more than any other: **practical completion**. It is the point at which the owner takes over the works, the contractor stops being exposed to delay damages and a series of financial and legal consequences switch over at once. It is also one of the most argued-about moments in any project. Owners worry about accepting unfinished work. Contractors worry about being held hostage by trivial defects. Both sides tend to treat practical completion as the end of the job, when in many respects it is the start of a different phase.

That phase is usually called the **defects liability period**, a defined time after practical completion during which the contractor must return to fix defects and the owner holds some security to make sure it does. Many people believe that when the period ends, the contractor's responsibility for defects ends too. In general, it does not.

This article explains, in general terms, what practical completion usually means, what changes when it is reached, how the defects liability period and retention work, what happens at final completion and why liability for defective work can continue long after the paperwork says the project is closed. It is general information, not legal advice. The precise effect of these stages depends on the contract, and on statutes that differ between states, so check both.

## What practical completion usually means

There is no single legal definition that applies to every contract. The contract's own definition governs, and the wording varies considerably. Most definitions share some common elements. Practical completion is typically reached when:

- the works are complete except for minor defects and omissions that do not prevent the works from being reasonably capable of being used for their intended purpose;
- the contractor has carried out any tests the contract requires, and the results are satisfactory;
- documents the contract requires at that stage have been provided, such as operation and maintenance manuals, as-built drawings, warranties and certificates of compliance; and
- any other stated conditions have been met, such as statutory approvals for occupation.

Two features are worth noting. First, practical completion does not require perfection. A short list of minor defects is normal and expected. Second, many contracts make documents and approvals part of the definition. A building can be physically finished but not practically complete because the occupation certificate or the fire system certification has not been obtained.

Many Australian contracts are based on standard forms, such as the Australian Standard general conditions of contract including AS 4000. These forms are frequently amended, sometimes heavily, so read the definition in your actual contract rather than assuming the standard form applies unchanged.

### Who decides

Practical completion is usually certified by someone named in the contract, such as a superintendent, project manager, architect or the owner itself. The contract normally sets out a procedure: the contractor gives notice that it believes practical completion has been or will be reached, the certifier inspects within a set time, and either issues a certificate or provides reasons and a list of what remains to be done.

Where the certifier is an agent of the owner, it is often required to act honestly and fairly in making certifying decisions, even though it is engaged by the owner. A certifier who withholds practical completion for reasons outside the contract's definition creates dispute risk for the owner.

## What changes at practical completion

Practical completion matters because a cluster of consequences typically attach to it at once. The table summarises the most common, but every contract differs.

| Consequence | Before practical completion | After practical completion (typically) |
|---|---|---|
| Possession and control | Contractor controls the site | Owner takes possession and use |
| Risk of loss or damage | Usually with the contractor | Usually passes to the owner, subject to the contract |
| Insurance | Contractor's works insurance often responds | Owner's own insurance must be in place |
| Liquidated damages for delay | Accrue after the date for completion | Stop accruing |
| Retention or security | Fully held | Often reduced, commonly by half |
| Defects liability period | Not yet started | Starts |
| Time bars and notices | Contract-specific | Some claims may need to be made by or soon after this point |
| Operation and maintenance | Contractor's responsibility | Owner's responsibility, with the contractor fixing defects |

The transfer of **risk and insurance** is the consequence most often overlooked. An owner that takes possession without arranging insurance may be exposed if a fire, flood or theft occurs in the first week. The same goes for operations. Once the owner is using the asset, it is responsible for operating and maintaining it correctly, and damage caused by misuse or poor maintenance is not a construction defect.

**Liquidated damages** usually stop at practical completion. That is why contractors push to achieve it, and why owners are sometimes tempted to delay certification. Delaying certification without a contractual basis is risky. The [what is a day of delay worth](/reados/collections/valuing-time-delay-and-uncertainty/what-is-a-day-of-delay-worth/) article explains how owners can think about the real value of time, which helps set sensible liquidated damages rates in the first place.

## Defects at practical completion

Almost every project reaches practical completion with a list of defects and omissions, often called a **punch list** or **defects list**. Handling this list well prevents many disputes.

- **Separate minor from major.** Items that prevent the works being used for their purpose generally prevent practical completion. Cosmetic items, minor adjustments and small omissions generally do not.
- **Make the list specific.** "Paint touch-ups throughout" is an invitation to argue. "Touch up scuffed paint to north wall of meeting room 2" is not.
- **Agree a timetable.** The contractor needs access, and the owner needs the works to be usable. Agree when and how the remaining items will be done.
- **Record the condition.** Photographs and a joint inspection at practical completion help distinguish construction defects from damage caused later by occupants.

## The defects liability period

After practical completion, most contracts provide a **defects liability period**, sometimes called a defects correction period. Twelve months is common for building and fit-out work, but the period is whatever the contract says, and different parts of the works may have different periods.

During this period, the contract typically:

- requires the contractor to rectify defects that appear or are notified, at its cost, within a reasonable time or a stated period;
- gives the contractor a right, and an obligation, to return to site to make good;
- allows the owner, if the contractor fails to rectify after notice, to have the work done by others and recover the cost, often from the retention or security; and
- may restart or extend the period for rectified items.

The contractor's right to return is valuable to both parties. It allows defects to be fixed by the party that knows the work, usually at lower cost than bringing in someone new. An owner that bypasses the process and engages another contractor without giving notice may struggle to recover the full cost.

### Defect notices that work

A defect notice should identify the defect precisely, where it is, when it was observed, why it is believed to be a defect (for example, by reference to the specification or a standard), any interim measures taken, and the time allowed for rectification. Photographs help. Vague or late notices are a common reason claims become difficult.

### Telling defects from maintenance problems

Not every problem after handover is a defect. A defect is a failure of the work to comply with the contract. Wear and tear, damage by occupants, problems caused by the owner's own changes and failures due to lack of maintenance are generally not defects. Disputes often turn on this distinction, which is why maintenance records, operating manuals and the condition record at practical completion matter.

## Retention and security

Most construction contracts give the owner some form of **security** for the contractor's performance, commonly:

- **cash retention**, where the owner withholds a percentage of each progress payment, often up to a capped amount;
- **bank guarantees** or **insurance bonds**, where a financial institution undertakes to pay the owner on demand up to a stated amount; or
- a combination.

Security is commonly reduced at practical completion, with the balance released after the defects liability period, provided the contractor has rectified notified defects. The contract governs when and how the owner can call on security. Calling on a bank guarantee without a contractual basis can lead to urgent court applications to restrain the call.

Some states have legislation affecting retention money in parts of the construction industry, for example requirements to hold retention in trust in certain circumstances. If your business holds or provides retention, check the rules in the state where the work is done.

Contractors should track the release dates of their own retention and security. Retention that is never claimed is a surprisingly common way for profit to disappear.

## Final completion and the final certificate

At the end of the defects liability period, many contracts provide for a **final certificate** or final payment process. The contractor submits a final claim, the certifier assesses it and remaining security is released. Some contracts give the final certificate a conclusive effect, limiting later claims, subject to exceptions such as fraud or claims already notified. Others do not. Understanding what the final certificate does under your contract matters, because it can affect the ability of either party to raise later claims.

## Liability does not end with the period

The most widespread misunderstanding about the defects liability period is that it limits the contractor's liability. In general, it does not. The period gives the contractor the right and obligation to return and fix defects on a defined process. It does not, by itself, end the contractor's liability for breach of contract if defective work is discovered later.

In general terms:

- **Contract claims** for defective work can often be brought until the relevant **limitation period** expires, which for many contract claims is six years from the breach, although periods and starting points vary by jurisdiction and contract type, and some contracts executed as deeds attract longer periods.
- **Latent defects**, those not reasonably discoverable at handover, can surface years later. Building legislation in some states sets long-stop periods for building actions.
- **Residential building** work is subject to **statutory warranties** under state home building legislation, which typically run for several years for different categories of defect and cannot be contracted out of.
- **Negligence** claims may sometimes be available to later owners in limited circumstances, although the law on recovering pure economic loss for building defects is complex and has narrowed for commercial buildings.

A contract can limit or exclude some of this liability by express terms, within the limits of the law, but the defects liability clause alone usually does not do so. ## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The project, figures and events are hypothetical.

A small manufacturer engages a builder to fit out a new production and office area for $420,000. The contract provides for 5% retention, capped at $21,000, with half released at practical completion and half at the end of a twelve-month defects liability period. Liquidated damages are $1,200 per day after the date for completion.

The builder gives notice of practical completion on day 98, three days after the date for completion. The superintendent inspects and finds two problems: the fire-door hardware certification is outstanding, and the compressed-air line is not pressure-tested. Both are required by the contract's definition of practical completion. The superintendent also lists forty minor items. It certifies practical completion on day 104, once the certification and test results are provided. Liquidated damages accrue for nine days: $1,200 × 9 = $10,800. At practical completion, $10,500 of retention is released and the owner's property insurance takes over.

In month ten, a section of floor coating begins lifting near the wash-down bay. The owner gives a defect notice with photographs, the specification reference and the maintenance record showing the approved cleaning chemicals were used. The builder returns, investigates and finds inadequate surface preparation. It rectifies the coating, and the contract extends the defects period for that area.

Two years later, the owner discovers that a concealed section of drainage was laid with insufficient fall, causing intermittent flooding. The defects liability period has ended and the final retention has been released. The owner's rights are not necessarily gone. It may still have a claim for breach of contract within the limitation period, but it must now prove the defect and the loss, and it no longer holds any security.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Read the definition before the end.** Know what your contract requires for practical completion, including documents and approvals, well before you get there.
- **Plan the handover.** Arrange insurance, operating staff, maintenance contracts and a joint inspection before taking possession.
- **Make defect lists precise.** Specific, located, photographed items reduce argument.
- **Use the process.** Give written defect notices and allow the contractor its contractual opportunity to rectify.
- **Keep maintenance records.** They help show whether a later problem is a defect or a maintenance failure.
- **Diary the dates.** Owners should diary defects liability expiry dates and inspect before they pass; contractors should diary retention and security release dates.
- **Do not treat the defects period as the end of liability.** Keep project records for at least as long as claims could be brought.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming practical completion means perfect completion.** Minor defects are normal and do not usually prevent it.
- **Taking possession without insurance.** Risk often transfers at practical completion.
- **Withholding certification for reasons outside the contract.** This creates dispute risk for the owner.
- **Bypassing the contractor's right to return.** Engaging others without notice can reduce what the owner can recover.
- **Confusing defects with damage or wear.** Without condition records and maintenance history, the argument is hard to resolve.
- **Letting security lapse unnoticed.** Owners lose their security and contractors lose money when dates are missed.
- **Destroying records too early.** Liability can outlast the defects period by years.

## Questions to ask

- What exactly must be done, tested and handed over before practical completion can be certified?
- Who certifies practical completion, and what process and timeframes apply?
- What transfers at practical completion under our contract: risk, insurance, security, operations?
- How long is the defects liability period for each part of the works, and can it restart?
- How must defect notices be given, and what happens if the contractor does not rectify?
- What security is held, and when and how is it released?
- What does the final certificate do under this contract?
- How long should we keep project records, given the limitation periods that may apply?

## Bringing it together

Practical completion is a transfer point, not a finish line. It typically hands possession, risk, insurance and operation to the owner, stops liquidated damages, reduces security and starts the defects liability period. The defects period gives the contractor a right and obligation to return and fix defects under a defined process, backed by retained security. It does not, by itself, end liability for defective work, which can continue for years under contract, statute and, in limited cases, negligence.

Treating the end of a project as a structured transition protects both parties. Define practical completion clearly, prepare for the handover, manage defects through the contract's process, track security dates and keep records long enough to deal with the defects that only time reveals.
