One of the world's largest technology companies earns most of its revenue from advertising, much of it paid by small and medium businesses. Yet its advertising platform became complex, and many small business owners found it hard to understand. Rather than simply advertising its advertising, the company built a website full of free content: research, practical guides and case studies showing how businesses of every size had used advertising to grow. Business owners who learned from that content became more confident, and more of them advertised.

The lesson applies far beyond technology. **When you help customers understand their problems and make better decisions, freely and without demanding their contact details first, you build trust, and trust becomes demand.** There is no free lunch, of course: the content serves the business. But it serves the business by genuinely serving the customer first.

Business trainers summarise the qualities that make content and brands effective with the acronym **THINK**: Trustworthy, Helpful, Inspiring, Necessary and Kind. This article explains each quality, shows how to apply them to content for small businesses, including technical and industrial firms, and sets out a practical content plan.

## Why helpful content works

- **Customers research before they buy.** Business buyers in particular often investigate problems and options online long before contacting a supplier.
- **Teaching demonstrates expertise.** Explaining a problem clearly shows you understand it better than competitors who only describe their products.
- **Helpful content earns attention without paying for every view.** Articles, guides and videos keep working long after they are published, through search, sharing and links.
- **It builds trust before the first conversation.** Prospects arrive already believing you know what you are talking about.
- **It improves decisions.** Customers who understand their options make better choices, buy more confidently and are less likely to be disappointed.

## The THINK test

Before publishing any piece of content, test it against five qualities.

### T: Trustworthy

Is the content accurate, honest and balanced?

- Base claims on evidence, and cite sources where appropriate.
- Acknowledge limitations and trade-offs, including when your product is not the right choice.
- Avoid exaggeration and hype.
- Keep content current, and update or remove outdated material.

Some of the most trusted brands in the world are trusted because they have kept their promises for generations. Content builds the same trust when it consistently tells the truth.

### H: Helpful

Does the content solve a real problem the reader has?

- Start from customers' questions, not from what you want to say.
- Give practical, usable guidance: steps, checklists, examples, calculations and templates.
- Make it complete enough to be genuinely useful on its own.

A useful test: would the reader be better off after reading it, even if they never buy from you?

### I: Inspiring

Does the content motivate the reader to act or think differently?

- Share real stories of problems solved, with permission.
- Show what is possible, and how others achieved it.
- Explain the "why" as well as the "how".

Inspiration is not hype. It comes from showing credible examples of progress that readers can relate to.

### N: Necessary

Is the content about something that genuinely matters to the reader?

- Focus on important, recurring problems rather than trivial topics.
- Address the questions customers ask most often, and the mistakes they make most often.
- Cover topics that affect their costs, risks, compliance, safety, quality or growth.

Content about necessary topics earns attention because readers need it.

### K: Kind

Is the content respectful of the reader's time, intelligence and circumstances?

- Write clearly and plainly, without jargon or condescension.
- Make content accessible: readable on mobile, with clear headings and descriptive links.
- Do not trick readers with misleading headlines or hidden sales pitches.
- Respect privacy: do not demand personal details for basic information.

## Where to start: customers' questions

The best content topics come from questions customers actually ask. Collect them from:

- **Sales and enquiry conversations.**
- **Customer service and technical support.**
- **Quotes that did not proceed**: what did customers not understand?
- **Search data**: what people search for in your field.
- **Industry forums and professional groups.**
- **Trade show conversations.**

Keep a running list, grouped by stage of the customer's journey:

| Stage | Typical questions | Content that helps |
|---|---|---|
| Recognising a problem | "Why does this keep failing?" "Is this normal?" | Explanatory articles, diagnostic checklists |
| Exploring options | "What are the ways to fix this?" "What do they cost?" | Comparison guides, cost explainers, case studies |
| Choosing a supplier | "What should I ask suppliers?" "How do I compare quotes?" | Buying guides, specification templates, FAQs |
| Using the product | "How do I get the best results?" | How-to guides, maintenance guides, videos |

### Answer the questions competitors avoid

Some of the most valuable content addresses questions suppliers often avoid: what things cost, when a cheaper option is good enough, what can go wrong and how to compare suppliers. Answering these honestly sets you apart. Customers are searching for these answers anyway. If you provide them clearly and fairly, you become the trusted source, even when the honest answer is occasionally that the customer does not need your most expensive option.

## Content formats

Choose formats that suit your customers and your capabilities:

- **Practical articles and guides**, which explain problems and solutions in depth.
- **Case studies**, which show how real customers solved problems.
- **Checklists and templates**, which readers can use directly.
- **Calculators**, such as cost, sizing or payback calculators.
- **Short videos**, which demonstrate processes or products.
- **Webinars and workshops**, which allow questions and build relationships.
- **Email newsletters**, which deliver useful content regularly to people who have chosen to receive it.
- **Technical notes and standards explainers**, particularly valuable in engineering and regulated industries.

## Content for technical and industrial businesses

Technical businesses are often well placed to produce helpful content, because their expertise is deep and their customers' problems are specific. Examples:

- A fabrication business explains how design choices affect fabrication cost, with examples.
- A maintenance contractor publishes guides on inspection requirements and common failure modes.
- An engineering consultancy explains how to prepare a clear brief, or how to compare design options.
- A component supplier publishes selection guides and installation tips.

Keep confidential client information out of content, and get permission before naming customers or sharing project details.

## A practical content plan

### 1. Choose your focus

Pick three to five core topics where you have genuine expertise and your customers have pressing questions.

### 2. Set a sustainable rhythm

Consistency matters more than volume. One genuinely useful article or video a fortnight, sustained for a year, usually achieves more than a burst of activity followed by silence.

### 3. Assign ownership

Someone must own the plan. Subject-matter experts can contribute through short interviews, with someone else drafting, so busy technical staff are not overwhelmed.

### 4. Apply the THINK test before publishing

Check each piece against the five qualities. If it fails one, fix it before publishing.

### 5. Distribute

Publish on your own website first, because it is an asset you control. Then share through email, professional networks, social media and conversations with customers. The article on [owning your website and digital presence](/reados/collections/business-systems-security-and-it-decisions/owning-your-website-and-digital-presence/) explains why your own site matters.

### 6. Measure

Track:

- Visits and time spent on content.
- Search rankings for important topics.
- Enquiries that mention or follow content.
- Newsletter sign-ups.
- Sales conversations where prospects refer to your content.

### 7. Improve and update

Refresh popular content regularly, expand pieces that perform well and retire outdated material.

## THINK beyond content: brands that pass the test

The THINK qualities apply to whole brands, not just individual articles. Trainers who use the model point to long-established businesses that customers trust across many product categories, and to companies whose founders built followings by teaching and helping people long before selling to them. The pattern is consistent:

- **Trustworthy**: they keep promises over many years, so customers buy new products from them with confidence.
- **Helpful**: their products and services make customers' lives easier, and their support solves problems quickly.
- **Inspiring**: their stories, often of modest beginnings and persistence, give customers a reason to care.
- **Necessary**: they meet needs customers have every day, not passing whims.
- **Kind**: their staff treat customers with respect, especially when something goes wrong.

Small businesses can apply the same test to their customer service, sales conversations, invoices, website and social media. Every interaction either builds or erodes trust.

## Turning expertise into content efficiently

The people with the deepest expertise are usually the busiest. A practical process:

1. **Collect a question** from the running list.
2. **Interview the expert** for 15 to 20 minutes, recording the conversation with permission. Ask how they would explain the issue to a customer, what mistakes customers make and what examples illustrate the point.
3. **Draft the piece** from the interview, written for the customer, not for other experts.
4. **Have the expert review it** for accuracy.
5. **Edit for clarity and apply the THINK test.**
6. **Publish and share**, crediting the expert where appropriate. Experts often enjoy seeing their knowledge recognised.

This process can produce a strong piece of content for less than an hour of the expert's time.

## Gating content: when to ask for details

Some businesses require readers to provide contact details before accessing content. The THINK approach suggests making most content freely available, because demanding details for basic information breaks trust and reduces reach. Reserve sign-up requirements for substantial resources, such as detailed templates, tools or webinars, where readers clearly see the value of exchanging their details. When you collect personal information, handle it lawfully and transparently, and send marketing emails only to people who have consented, with an easy way to unsubscribe, as Australian spam laws require.

## A worked example

A small industrial filtration company relies on cold calls and trade shows for new business. Its owner notices that customers repeatedly ask the same questions: how to choose filter ratings, how often to change filters and why filters fail early.

The company decides to answer these questions publicly. Over a year, it publishes 24 practical articles, a filter selection guide, a simple calculator estimating the cost of premature filter failure and four short videos showing correct installation. Each piece passes the THINK test: accurate and balanced (including advice on when cheaper filters are adequate), genuinely useful, illustrated with real customer stories, focused on costly problems and written plainly.

Within eighteen months, the articles rank well for many relevant searches, maintenance managers mention them in sales conversations and inbound enquiries have tripled. Several large customers say they chose the company because its content showed it understood their problems.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing about yourself** instead of the customer's problems.
- **Thin content** that says little of practical value.
- **Inconsistency**: publishing a burst of content, then nothing for months.
- **Hiding everything behind sign-up forms.**
- **Exaggerated claims**, which undermine trust and can breach consumer law.
- **Never measuring** whether content leads to enquiries.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will giving away knowledge reduce demand for our services?** Usually the opposite. Most customers who read detailed guidance realise the problem is complex and prefer to engage an expert who clearly understands it.

**How long should content be?** Long enough to be genuinely useful. Some questions need a short answer, while others need a thorough guide. Structure longer content with clear headings so readers can find what they need.

**How do we know if content is working?** Look beyond page views. The strongest signals are enquiries that mention your content, prospects who arrive already understanding their problem, shorter sales conversations and customers who share your guides with colleagues. Ask new customers how they found you, and record the answers.

**What if competitors copy our content?** Some will. Keep publishing, keep improving and keep your content grounded in real experience and examples that only you can provide. Original, regularly updated expertise is very hard to imitate convincingly.

**Can we use AI tools to write content?** They can help with drafting and editing, but content must be checked carefully for accuracy and should reflect your genuine expertise. Generic, unchecked content fails the trustworthy and helpful tests.

## Summary

Helpful content builds trust by teaching customers before you sell to them. Test every piece against THINK: trustworthy, helpful, inspiring, necessary and kind. Start from customers' real questions, organised by their journey, and choose formats that suit them, from practical guides and case studies to calculators and videos. Set a sustainable rhythm, assign ownership, publish on your own website first, measure results and keep improving. Gate only substantial resources, and handle personal information lawfully. Content that genuinely serves customers becomes one of the most durable marketing assets a business can build.
