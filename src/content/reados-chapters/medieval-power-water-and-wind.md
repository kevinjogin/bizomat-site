When people think of the history of power, they usually begin with steam. Long before the first steam engine pumped water from a mine, however, Europe and much of the wider world had already undergone a power revolution of a quieter kind. Waterwheels and windmills turned millstones, drove hammers, worked bellows, sawed timber, fulled cloth and drained land. In many regions, they were the most powerful machines people had ever seen, and they shaped where communities grew, how work was organised and who held economic power.

This earlier revolution is easy to overlook because it unfolded slowly, over centuries, and because its machines were built from timber and stone rather than iron. Yet it established many of the mechanical principles later industry depended on: converting rotation into useful motion through gears, cams and cranks; locating production where energy was available; managing shared natural resources; and treating power infrastructure as a business in its own right.

This article traces, in general terms, how water and wind power developed and spread, the mechanisms that put them to work, the economic and social arrangements around them, their early Australian history and the lessons they hold for energy decisions, site selection and shared infrastructure today. It is a general historical overview.

## Ancient beginnings

Water-powered mills were known in the ancient world. The Roman architect Vitruvius described a water-powered grain mill in the first century BC, and archaeologists have found substantial Roman mill sites, including a large complex in southern France where a series of waterwheels ran down a hillside, each driving millstones. Yet in many parts of the ancient world, abundant human and animal labour limited the incentive to adopt water power widely.

## The medieval spread of watermills

In medieval Europe, water power spread dramatically. A famous indication of its extent comes from England's Domesday Book, compiled in 1086, which recorded thousands of mills, around 5,600 by commonly cited counts, across the country. Most ground grain, but over the following centuries water power was applied to an expanding range of tasks:

| Application | What the mill did | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| Grain milling | Turned millstones to grind flour | Freed enormous amounts of labour |
| Fulling | Drove hammers that pounded woollen cloth to clean and thicken it | Transformed textile finishing |
| Iron working | Powered bellows and heavy trip hammers | Larger furnaces, more and better iron |
| Sawing | Drove reciprocating saws | Faster timber production |
| Paper making | Pounded rags into pulp | Supported the growth of printing and records |
| Ore crushing and pumping | Crushed ore and lifted water | Enabled mining |

The key insight was that a source of rotating power could be adapted to many different tasks through relatively simple mechanisms.

## Turning rotation into work

Waterwheels and windmills produce rotation. Most useful tasks need something else: a different speed, a change of direction or a repeated up-and-down motion. Medieval millwrights developed the mechanisms to provide it:

- **Gearing**, using wooden gears with pegs or cogs, changed speed and direction, for example turning the slow horizontal axle of a waterwheel into the faster vertical rotation of a millstone.
- **Cams** on a rotating shaft lifted hammers or stamps and let them fall, converting rotation into repeated blows for fulling, forging and crushing.
- **Cranks**, increasingly common in later centuries, converted rotation into reciprocating motion, for saws and pumps.

These mechanisms are among the oldest examples of a modular approach to machine design: a common power source, adapted to different uses by standard elements. The cam described in modern machine design texts, and in the [power screws and linear motion](/reados/collections/machine-elements-and-mechanical-design/power-screws-and-linear-motion/) article, descends directly from these medieval applications.

## Better wheels: from experience to experiment

Waterwheels came in several forms. **Undershot** wheels were driven by water flowing beneath them and suited fast streams with little fall. **Overshot** wheels received water at the top, using its weight as well as its motion, and suited sites with greater fall. **Breastshot** wheels received water part-way up.

For centuries, millwrights chose and built wheels by experience. In the 1750s, the English engineer John Smeaton carried out systematic experiments with model waterwheels and showed that overshot wheels used water far more efficiently than undershot wheels. His work is an early example of using measured experiment to improve machine design, a theme also explored in the [steam power and the business of improvement](/reados/collections/engineering-history/steam-power-and-the-business-of-improvement/) article.

## Windmills

Windmills appeared in north-western Europe in the late twelfth century. Early **post mills** mounted the whole mill body on a central post, so the miller could turn it to face the wind. Later **tower mills** kept the body fixed and turned only the cap carrying the sails, allowing larger and more robust mills.

Wind power was especially valuable where rivers were slow or absent. In the Low Countries, windmills drained land, pumping water from low-lying areas to create farmland, and powered sawmills that supplied timber for shipbuilding. Wind, however, was less predictable than water, and mills had to cope with storms, calm periods and changing directions.

## Tide mills

In some coastal areas, mills used the tides. A **tide mill** trapped water in a pond behind a gate as the tide rose, then released it through a waterwheel as the tide fell. Tide mills were operating in parts of Europe in the medieval period. They offered a predictable energy source, since tides follow a known timetable, but their working hours shifted with the tides, so the miller's day was set by the moon rather than the sun.

## The limits of water and wind

Water and wind power had real limitations, and the ways people managed them are instructive:

- **Variable supply.** Rivers ran low in drought and flooded in wet seasons. Wind dropped, gusted and changed direction. Mills stood idle when nature did not cooperate.
- **Storage.** Millponds behind weirs stored water so a mill could run for hours even when the stream was low, an early form of energy storage that smoothed an intermittent source, much as batteries smooth solar generation today.
- **Site constraints.** Good sites, with sufficient fall and reliable flow, were limited, and they were often far from markets or raw materials.
- **Transmission.** Mechanical power could be carried only short distances, by shafts, belts and ropes, so the work had to come to the power source.

These limits explain why steam, and later electricity, which could be generated anywhere and transmitted over long distances, were so transformative. They also explain why modern renewable energy systems pay so much attention to storage, transmission and matching demand to supply.

## The millwright: an early mechanical engineer

Building and maintaining mills required a skilled trade: the **millwright**. Millwrights designed and built waterwheels, sails, gears, shafts and mill machinery, mostly in timber, and kept them running. They dressed millstones, re-cutting the grooves that ground the grain, replaced worn gear teeth and adjusted machinery for changing conditions. Their work combined carpentry, metalwork, hydraulics and practical mechanics, and many historians regard millwrights as forerunners of the mechanical engineering profession. When the first engineering societies formed in the eighteenth century, several of their leading figures had worked as millwrights or on mill machinery.

## Mills as businesses and as power

Mills were valuable assets, and their ownership carried economic and social power. In much of medieval Europe, lords owned mills and required tenants to grind their grain there, taking a share of the grain as a toll. Mill rights were bought, sold and fought over.

Water power also created conflicts. Weirs and dams that served mills could block navigation and fish passage, flood upstream land or deprive downstream users of water. Legal arrangements developed to allocate water rights and resolve disputes, early forms of governance for a shared natural resource.

## From mills to factories

When mechanised textile production began in the eighteenth century, water power provided the energy. Early factories, such as the water-powered cotton mills of Britain in the 1770s, were built beside rivers with suitable falls. Factory towns grew where water power was available. Only later, as steam engines became efficient enough, could factories be located near coalfields, ports and labour markets rather than rivers.

## An Australian chapter

Water and wind power were part of early colonial Australia. Settlers built windmills and watermills to grind grain where rivers, wind and supplies allowed. One of the best known survivors is the Old Windmill in Brisbane, built by convicts in 1828 and now regarded as the oldest surviving building in Queensland. Later, the windpump became a familiar sight across rural Australia, lifting groundwater for stock and homesteads across vast areas without mains power.

## Lessons for energy, location and infrastructure

### Energy shapes location

Medieval mills had to be where the water or wind was. Early factories had to be by rivers. Today, the location and timing of renewable energy, such as rooftop solar or regional wind, again shapes where and when energy-intensive work is best done. The [learning curves and zero fuel cost](/reados/collections/engineering-history-modern-era/learning-curves-and-zero-fuel-cost-solar-wind-and-batteries/) article explains how cheap renewable generation is changing the economics of energy.

### One power source, many uses

The cam, gear and crank allowed one power source to serve many industries. Modern equivalents include electric motors, compressed air and software platforms. Investing in flexible capabilities that can serve many tasks often pays better than single-purpose solutions, especially when future needs are uncertain.

### Shared resources need governance

Water rights disputes are an early version of modern questions about shared infrastructure: electricity networks, water, roads and data. Using a shared resource well requires clear rules, fair allocation and ways to resolve conflicts before they become disputes.

### Infrastructure can be a business model

Medieval mill owners earned tolls on others' use of their infrastructure, much as utilities, platforms and infrastructure owners do today. Control of the means of production remains a source of economic power.

### Storage makes intermittent energy dependable

The millpond turned an irregular stream into a dependable working day. Batteries, thermal storage, compressed air receivers and simply scheduling flexible work into periods of plentiful energy play the same role today. Before adding generation, it is often worth asking whether better storage or scheduling would make existing supply more useful.

### Measurement improves machines

Smeaton's experiments showed that systematic measurement could improve centuries-old designs. Many businesses run equipment and processes "the way they have always been run" without measuring their efficiency, and simple measurements of energy use, throughput and losses often reveal improvements as significant as Smeaton's.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The business and figures are hypothetical, and solar output and tariffs vary by location and contract.

A small Australian powder-coating business installs a 100 kW rooftop solar system, which generates an average of about 400 kWh a day. Initially, the business uses about 60% of that energy on site, because its curing oven often runs in the late afternoon and early morning, and exports the rest to the grid at a low feed-in rate.

Like a medieval miller arranging work around the flow of the river, the owner reschedules the oven's main batches to run between about 10 am and 3 pm, when solar generation peaks. On-site use of solar energy rises to about 85% of generation. The additional 25% of generation used on site, about 100 kWh a day, replaces grid electricity that would have cost about $0.25 per kWh instead of earning a feed-in credit of about $0.05 per kWh, a difference of $0.20 per kWh. That is worth about 100 × $0.20, or $20 a day, roughly $5,000 over 250 working days a year, without any new equipment. The owner then looks at whether a modest thermal store, such as better oven insulation and a heated parts buffer, could extend the benefit into cloudy periods, much as a millpond extended the miller's working day when the stream ran low.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Match energy-intensive work to energy availability**, such as solar generation periods or off-peak tariffs.
- **Look for flexible capabilities** that serve many tasks.
- **Understand your dependence on shared infrastructure** and the rules that govern it.
- **Measure the efficiency** of long-standing equipment and processes.
- **Consider location** in light of energy, water and logistics.
- **Use storage and scheduling** to make intermittent energy more useful.
- **Keep the skills to maintain your equipment**, as millwrights did, rather than relying entirely on outside help.

## Questions worth considering

- When and where is energy cheapest for our business, and does our work schedule reflect it?
- Which of our capabilities could serve more than one product or customer?
- Which shared resources do we depend on, and how could changes in their rules affect us?
- When did we last measure the efficiency of our oldest equipment?
- How would changes in energy cost or availability affect where we locate future operations?
- Could storage or better scheduling make better use of the energy we already generate or buy?

## Bringing it together

Long before steam, water and wind power transformed medieval economies. Watermills and windmills spread across Europe and later to colonies such as Australia, applying rotating power to grinding, fulling, forging, sawing, paper making, mining and drainage through gears, cams and cranks. Mills shaped settlement, created wealth and power for their owners and raised early questions about governing shared resources. Their lessons, that energy shapes location, that flexible mechanisms multiply value, that shared resources need governance and that measurement improves even ancient designs, remain directly relevant as businesses adapt to a new era of renewable energy.
