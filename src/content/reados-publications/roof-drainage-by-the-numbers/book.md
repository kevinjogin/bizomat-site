## About this book

Roof drainage looks like simple sheet metal: a channel along the eaves, a pipe down the wall. It is also a small hydraulic system that must carry a short, intense storm off a large area without letting any of it into the building, and keep doing so when leaves block an outlet or the gutter has expanded on a hot afternoon. When it fails, the damage is inside the building: wet stock, ceilings, switchboards and insulation, usually found the morning after a storm.

This book works roof drainage through by numbers. It starts with the design storm and how intensity, duration and probability are chosen, turns a roof catchment into a flow, checks eaves gutters, outlets and box gutters against that flow, plans overflow for the rarer storm, deals with materials and thermal movement in long metal gutters, and finishes with setting out, testing, inspection and maintenance. Each chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It assumes basic arithmetic and some familiarity with sheet metal work. The hydraulics are explained from the start.

## The running case

The book follows a fictional **light industrial warehouse, W-40**, 40 m long and 24 m wide, with a gable roof pitched at 5° and its ridge along the 40 m length. Each roof slope covers 40 m by 12 m in plan. At the rear, the roof drains to an eaves gutter. At the front, facing the street, a parapet rises 1.0 m above the roof and the roof drains to a box gutter behind it, discharging through the parapet into rainheads at each end.

The design rainfall intensities used throughout are **180 mm/h** for a 5-minute storm with a 5% annual exceedance probability and **250 mm/h** for a 5-minute storm with a 1% annual exceedance probability. This is an illustration. They are round teaching numbers, not values for any real location. All dimensions, flows, prices and results are invented for teaching and do not describe a KEVOS® client, supplier or project. Money is in Australian dollars, excluding GST.

## How to use the book

Chapters 1 and 2 turn rain into flow. Chapters 3 to 6 size the parts that carry it and the overflow that protects the building when they cannot. Chapter 7 covers the metal itself, and Chapter 8 the work on site and the years after. The geometry of making gutter corners, stopends and other profiled sheet metal is beyond this book's scope.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The supplied source, a 1922 American treatise on architectural sheet metal work, informed only the range of subjects. The methods here are standard hydraulics and current Australian practice, with every calculation shown. The hydraulic estimates are teaching tools. Real roof drainage must be designed and installed to the National Construction Code and AS/NZS 3500.3 or another approved method, by people licensed for the work where the law requires it, using the design rainfall for the actual site from the Bureau of Meteorology. Working on roofs is high-risk work and must be planned under the work health and safety law that applies.
