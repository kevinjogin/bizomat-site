Anyone who has walked a trade show knows the feeling. A robot arm moves a part from a conveyor to a chuck in one smooth arc, the door closes, the spindle starts, and the arm is already reaching for the next blank. A screen on the stand shows every machine in a model factory, green, amber or red, updating as you watch. It is hard not to want one. The wanting is not about cost per part or delivery dates. It is about the machine itself: the precision, the coordination, the way every part serves the whole.

Adam Smith described this feeling more than two and a half centuries ago, and drew a lesson from it that is still useful to anyone approving a purchase. This chapter sets out his observation, introduces the job shop whose proposals the book follows, and gives a simple test for separating what a contrivance is from what it is for.

## Smith's watch

In the fourth part of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Smith turns to a puzzle about usefulness. We praise things for being useful, he says, but if we look closely, what we often admire is not the end they serve but their fitness to serve it: the neat arrangement of means to an end, valued for its own sake.

His example is a watch. A person who is particular about watches will despise one that loses a couple of minutes a day. They will sell it cheaply and pay many times as much for one that loses barely a minute in a fortnight. Yet the only purpose of a watch is to tell us the time so that we are not late, and the same person is often no more punctual than anyone else. What drew them was the perfection of the machine, not the punctuality it was supposed to produce.

Smith widens the observation to the small conveniences people collect: cases and instruments and gadgets of what he called frivolous utility, each beautifully fitted to some minor purpose, carried about at more trouble than they save. And he applies it to people's whole lives. Much of the effort people put into getting rich, he argues, is driven not by the comforts riches bring but by the imagined beauty of a well-ordered life full of such contrivances.

Charles Griswold, writing in the *Cambridge Companion to Adam Smith*, draws out the point that matters most here: for Smith, the usefulness people admire is often usefulness as functionality, the satisfying way a thing works within an orderly system, rather than the actual good it does. That admiration is a product of the imagination, and like all products of the imagination it can lead judgement astray.

## Smith did not think it was simply foolish

It would be easy to read Smith as mocking the watch collector. He is partly doing that. But he also argues that the same love of order and contrivance is what drives people to improve their tools, their land and their institutions. It keeps industry in motion. A society of people indifferent to well-made things would make fewer of them.

That two-sided view is what makes Smith useful to an engineering business. The love of a well-made machine is not a weakness to be stamped out; it is part of why engineers are good at their work. But it is a motive, not an argument. When it is allowed to decide what the business buys, it produces watches that keep perfect time for people who are no more punctual than before.

## The case: a job shop and four proposals

This is an illustration. A precision machining job shop employs twelve people. It runs two CNC lathes, three CNC machining centres, a coordinate measuring machine and a small fitting area. Its work is turned and milled parts in batches from a handful to a few hundred, for makers of mining equipment, food machinery and pumps. Batches average about 40 parts. The shop's on-time delivery over the last year was **78%**, its typical lead time about four weeks, and two customers have complained about late orders.

The owner is a mechanical engineer who reads every machine-tool magazine and goes to every trade show. In one year, four proposals reach his desk:

- **A robot loading cell** for the main CNC lathe, quoted at **A$185,000** installed, promising lights-out running and freeing the lathe operator for other work.
- **A scheduling and machine-monitoring system** with live dashboards on every machine and a large screen in the office, to be built by a local software developer for about **A$60,000** plus support.
- **A complete set of standard work instructions** for all of the shop's recurring job types, written by the owner over a summer and to be introduced across the shop in one go.
- **An upgrade to the coordinate measuring machine**, offered by a sales representative at **A$95,000**, with a probing head and software that measure to about a tenth of a micron.

Each is a real contrivance, well designed and fitted to its purpose. Each appeals to an engineer's sense of how things should be. And each is described first by what it is and only vaguely by what it is for. That is the signature of the love of system.

## The failure case: the five-axis machine

The owner has made this kind of decision before. Three years earlier the shop bought a five-axis machining centre for about **A$420,000**. The case for it was that five-axis machining would let the shop win complex work it could not do on three-axis machines, and finish parts in one set-up instead of several.

The machine is impressive and well used. But a review of its job logs shows how it is used. Of about **2,400 running hours** last year, about **190 hours**, roughly **8%**, involved simultaneous five-axis motion or tilted set-ups that a three-axis machine could not have done. The rest was three-axis work that the shop's older machines could have done, but the five-axis machine was newer, faster to set up and pleasant to use, so work flowed to it. The complex work it was bought to win amounted to two customers and a few small orders a year.

The machine was not a disaster. It is productive, and its speed has value. But the case for it, winning complex work, was barely realised, and the shop paid about A$420,000 for a capability it uses 8% of the time. A three-axis machine with a good tilting fixture would have done most of the work for perhaps half the price.

### How it is detected

The shop detected the gap only after three years, and only because the owner asked a direct question of the job logs: how many hours used the capability we paid for? That question should have been asked before the purchase, as a forecast, and checked six months after, as a measurement. A purchase case built on a capability should state how much of that capability the business expects to use, and the expectation should be checked.

### How it is corrected

The owner cannot un-buy the machine, but he can change how the next proposal is judged. He writes one rule on the whiteboard above his desk: **state the end before the means, and price the end.** Every proposal, whatever its appeal, must say what result it will change, by how much, under the shop's actual work, and what that result is worth.

## A test for any contrivance

The rule becomes a short sequence the shop applies to each of the four proposals in the chapters that follow.

```figure
type: steps
title: Judging a contrivance by its end
caption: This is an illustration. The test is deliberately plain. Its purpose is to move the discussion from what the proposal is to what it will do for the business's actual work.
- Name the end | the result the business needs: on-time delivery, cost per part, lead time, safety, quality
- Price the end | what a unit of that result is worth, in money or in a measure everyone agrees on
- Describe realistic use | the shop's actual batch sizes, product mix, skills and hours, not the demonstration
- Estimate the result at realistic use | how much of the end the proposal delivers
- Compare with simpler means | what a cheaper or less elegant option would deliver
- *Decide, and measure afterwards | check the delivered result six months later
```

The test does not forbid elegant solutions. A robot cell that genuinely delivers the end at a sensible price passes it easily. What the test does is take the appeal of the contrivance out of the argument, so that it can be enjoyed afterwards, if the purchase is made, rather than relied on beforehand.

## Recognising the love of system in a proposal

Some features of a proposal are useful warning signs, not because they are wrong, but because they suggest the means is being admired more than the end.

- **Description by capability rather than result.** "Measures to a tenth of a micron" rather than "lets us accept parts we currently cannot inspect".
- **Superlatives about the system.** Real-time, fully integrated, lights-out, complete, state of the art. These describe the contrivance.
- **Benefits stated without a baseline.** "Better delivery" without the current delivery figure or the target.
- **Demonstrations on ideal work.** A robot loading the same simple part for an hour, when the shop's batches are small and varied.
- **The proposer's evident delight.** Including the owner's own. Delight is a good sign about the proposer and no sign at all about the proposal.
- **A solution looking for a problem.** The proposal arrives before anyone has described the problem it solves, often after a trade show or a supplier visit.

The proposer's delight deserves the most attention, because the person most susceptible to the love of system is often the one with the authority to approve. Smith's watch collector did not need a salesman to persuade him; he persuaded himself.

## Why engineers are especially prone

Engineers are trained to admire fitness. Their education rewards the elegant solution, the optimised design and the system in which every part has a reason. Those are exactly the qualities Smith said people admire for their own sake. The trait that makes an engineer good at designing a fixture can make the same engineer a poor judge of whether the business needs a robot.

Smith adds another motive that is easy to recognise in any industry. People want to be admired, he argued, and much of the effort to acquire fine things is really an effort to be looked at with approval. A shop with a robot cell and a wall of live dashboards impresses visiting customers, suppliers and other engineers. That is a real benefit, and occasionally a commercial one: a customer may be reassured by an evidently modern shop. But it should be named as what it is, and priced if it is to count, rather than hidden inside a claim about labour cost or delivery.

Suppliers know all of this. Demonstrations, trade-show stands and brochures are designed to show the contrivance at its most beautiful, on work chosen to suit it. There is nothing dishonest in that; it is selling. The buyer's job is to bring the conversation back to the buyer's own work.

## Who applies the test

The test works best when the person applying it is not the person proposing the purchase. At the job shop, the owner asks the production supervisor and the office manager to review each proposal against the six steps, with the proposer in the room to answer questions but not to chair. The reviewers have no stake in the contrivance's beauty and a strong stake in the shop's delivery and costs. The review takes an hour for a small purchase and an afternoon for a large one. It has already changed one decision, as Chapter 3 shows.

## Exercise: name the ends

Rewrite each of the four proposals as a statement of the end it should serve, including a measure and a baseline that the shop could collect. For example: "Raise on-time delivery from 78% to 90% within a year."

**Worked answer.** One reasonable set:

- **Robot loading cell.** "Reduce the labour cost of loading the main lathe by an amount to be measured, and raise the lathe's productive hours, from a baseline of the current loading hours per week and the lathe's current running hours."
- **Scheduling and monitoring system.** "Raise on-time delivery from 78% to 90%, and cut typical lead time from about four weeks to three, within a year."
- **Standard work instructions.** "Reduce first-off rejects and rework on recurring jobs from their current rate, measured over three months, and shorten the time for a new operator to run a recurring job unsupervised."
- **Measuring machine upgrade.** "Enable reliable acceptance decisions on parts the shop currently cannot measure with adequate confidence, and identify how many such parts the shop makes each year."

Two things stand out from the rewriting. First, every end needs a baseline the shop does not yet have: loading hours, rework rates, the number of parts with very tight tolerances. Collecting those baselines is the first job. Second, two of the proposals may serve the same end. The scheduling system and the robot both claim to improve delivery, and the following chapters ask which, if either, actually would.
