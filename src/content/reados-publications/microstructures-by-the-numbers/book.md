## About this book

A metallographic report turns a polished, etched slice of metal into numbers: a pearlite fraction, a grain size number, a nodule count, a porosity percentage, an inclusion rating, a hardness. Those numbers decide whether a casting is accepted, whether a heat treatment worked and whether a failure was the material's fault. Yet each one comes from a flat section through a three-dimensional structure, from a handful of microscope fields chosen by a person, and from counting rules that change the answer if they change.

This book teaches the arithmetic that lies behind those numbers, which is mostly stereology and counting statistics, so that a reader can ask for the right measurement, judge how precise it is and act on it sensibly. It starts with what a section shows and how to sample a part. It then works through phase fractions by point counting, grain size by counts and intercepts, and the correction of particle sizes seen in sections. It applies these to graphite shape in ductile iron, to porosity, to non-metallic inclusions and to microhardness traverses, and ends with the uncertainty of the whole chain and the decisions made from it.

It is for mechanical, materials and quality engineers, laboratory technicians and supervisors, and the buyers who order castings and forgings to a microstructure requirement. It assumes algebra, logarithms, a little probability and the vocabulary of steels and cast irons. Specimen preparation and etching are described only where they change the numbers. Each chapter works the running case with real numbers, includes one plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional laboratory. This is an illustration. Plant P makes centrifugal pumps and runs a small metallography laboratory with one technician, a metallurgical microscope with a camera and image analysis software, a Brinell tester and a microhardness tester. The laboratory checks two purchased parts.

- **The pump casing**, a ductile (spheroidal graphite) iron casting ordered to grade EN-GJS-500-7, with a 20 mm wall and a 32 mm sealing flange. The plant's drawing adds microstructure requirements: nodularity at least 80%, at least 100 nodules per mm², pearlite no more than 40% of the matrix, porosity on the sealing face below a stated limit, and a hardness of 170 to 230 HBW.
- **The pump shaft**, a quenched and tempered 42CrMo4 (AS 1444 grade 4140) steel bar, 40 mm in diameter, with an induction-hardened bearing journal. The drawing requires a prior austenite grain size of ASTM 5 or finer, a stated inclusion cleanliness, and an effective hardened depth on the journal.

The story begins when a batch of casings leaks through the sealing flange on the plant's pressure test, and a shaft from a new supplier fails its journal hardness check. The laboratory is asked whether the material is at fault.

The plant, its suppliers and every measurement and result are invented. None describes KEVOS® work, a real company or a real supplier's product. The drawing requirements are illustrative choices, not values taken from any standard.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 to 3 in order: they set out what a section shows, how to sample and the point-counting method that later chapters build on. Chapters 4 and 5 cover grain size. Chapters 6 to 9 deal with particles, pores and inclusions, where the sectioning effect matters most. Chapter 10 covers hardness traverses, and Chapter 11 the uncertainty of microstructure numbers and the decisions made from them.

WORKOS tools are linked where they perform a step of the method, with a note of exactly what each one calculates and where its model stops. Where a tool solves a related problem rather than the chapter's own, the text says so.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The relations used here are the established ones of quantitative metallography and stereology: Delesse's principle, the planimetric and intercept grain size relations, Wicksell's corpuscle relations and extreme-value statistics. Each chapter states the assumptions they need, chiefly that the structure is sampled without bias and, for some relations, that particles are spheres. Test standards are named by number and scope only; their procedures, charts and acceptance values govern real testing and are not reproduced.

Metallographic results feed decisions about pressure-containing castings, rotating shafts and failure investigations, some of them safety-critical. Real acceptance, failure analysis and specification work are matters for competent, accredited laboratories and qualified engineers working to the applicable standards and contracts. This book is educational. It explains how the numbers are made and how far to trust them; it is not a test procedure, a specification or a substitute for accredited testing.
