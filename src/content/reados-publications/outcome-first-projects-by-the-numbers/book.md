## About this book

Most projects arrive as a request for a thing: a new machine, a building, a system, a plant. The thing is easy to describe, price and order, and the project quickly becomes the job of delivering it. Whether it was the right thing is decided later, if at all, when the money has been spent. Starting from the outcome reverses the order. The purpose comes first, written as a few measurable benefits with their value; then the measures, the baselines and the targets; then the causes of the gap; and only then the options, the requirements and the scope, each kept only if it traces back to a target.

This book works through that sequence with numbers. It begins with the difference between a deliverable and an outcome, and between ends and means. It turns the outcome into measures using the goal–question–metric method, measures a baseline that will stand up to scrutiny, and sets targets from the purpose rather than from last year's figure. It then works back from the gap to its causes, maps the benefits to the business changes and enabling changes they need with a benefits dependency network, derives and compares options, traces every requirement and scope item to a target, and holds acceptance testing and benefit tracking to the same measures. Each chapter follows one running case, includes a plausible error and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for owners, managers, sponsors, engineers and finance staff in small and medium Australian businesses. It assumes spreadsheet arithmetic and nothing more. [From Approval to Benefits](/reados/collections/from-approval-to-benefits/) covers what happens to benefits after a project is approved; this book covers the definition work that comes before approval and decides what is worth approving.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional decision. This is an illustration. A family-owned commercial laundry in a regional New South Wales city, run by its second generation, washes, dries, irons and delivers about **60,000 kg** of linen a week over six production days for a group of four private hospitals and 22 hotels and serviced apartments. Its plant is a bank of eight washer-extractors, dryers and two flatwork ironer lines, one of them from 2009. The owners ask for **a new plant**: a tunnel washer with a press and new dryers, a 600 m² extension to house it, RFID linen tracking and refurbished amenities, about **A$6.12 million excluding GST**.

Working back from what the business needs reveals a different problem. The hospital contract, more than half the laundry's volume, is to be re-tendered within a year, at a lower price and with a requirement for **98% of deliveries on time and in full**, against the laundry's measured **91.2%**. The outcome the owners need is expressed in four measures: on-time-in-full delivery, cost per kilogram, and water and energy per kilogram. The book builds those measures, finds where the gap really comes from, and shows how an option derived from the outcome, costing about **A$2.44 million**, meets all four targets where the requested plant meets two.

The business, its customers, its people and every number in the case are invented. None describes KEVOS® work, a real business or a real project. All amounts are Australian dollars excluding GST.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first: the outcome and its value are the yardstick for everything after them. Chapters 3 to 5 turn the outcome into measures, baselines and targets, and are best read in order. Chapters 6 and 7 work back from the gap to causes and to the changes that would close it. Chapters 8 to 10 derive the options, test the scope and requirements, and carry the measures into acceptance and benefit tracking. Every calculation was checked by running it, and the KEVOS® tools linked in the chapters reproduce the capacity and option arithmetic.

This book is one of a series of KEVOS® Library books on how projects are defined, decided and delivered, and it links to the others where their subjects meet. *Demand and Benefit Forecasts by the Numbers* covers how large and how certain the benefits defined here are likely to be. *Front-End Planning by the Numbers* places outcome definition within the wider planning done before a project is committed. *Stage Gates and Stopping Rules by the Numbers* covers the decision points, such as the case's later gate on a tunnel washer, at which the outcome is used to continue, change or stop. *Reference Class Forecasting by the Numbers* corrects the cost, schedule and benefit estimates that the options in this book rely on.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The methods are established, and the sources are listed with what each was used for. Much of the published guidance on benefits comes from government and from information systems research, written for larger organisations than most readers work in; the book uses its principles, not its procedures, and says where a small business can do with less. The case's prices, costs, rates and performance figures are teaching values, not market data, and a real decision needs its own measurements, quotes and advice. Commercial laundries handling hospital linen work under infection-control requirements, the applicable Australian Standard, work health and safety laws and their customers' contracts; nothing here replaces those or the competent people responsible for them. This book is general information about defining and deciding projects. It is not financial, legal, engineering or investment advice.
