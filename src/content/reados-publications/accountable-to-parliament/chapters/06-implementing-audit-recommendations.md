Agreeing to an audit recommendation takes one line in a letter. Implementing it takes months, several people and often money nobody budgeted for. In the gap, attention moves on, and a year later a committee, an auditor or a Senator asks what changed. The answer has to be on the record, and it has to be true.

This is an illustration. The ANAO's report on Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) was tabled in June 2028. It made four recommendations, and NRIO's comments, printed in the report, agreed all four (Chapter 5). Oscar Hale, the Audit Liaison Officer, keeps NRIO's recommendations register. The Director, Regional Programs, owns the actions, and Sam Reid, who manages the RCFP, does most of the work. NRIO's audit committee meets quarterly, and at every meeting it will ask what has been done and how NRIO knows.

## Agreeing, and saying why

An agency has three honest responses to a recommendation. It can agree. It can agree with a qualification, saying exactly what it will do instead of, or as well as, what was recommended. Or it can disagree, with reasons and evidence. Whichever it chooses, the response is printed in the report under s19(8) of the [Auditor-General Act 1997](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05248/latest/text), so it has to stand up in public.

Agreement is a commitment, not a courtesy: once the response says "agreed", Parliament, the Auditor-General and the public can hold the agency to it. Before the accountable authority signs, the people who will do the work should record whether it can be done, what it will cost, who owns it and by when.

Disagreeing is legitimate when the reasons are sound: a recommendation may misread the program, cost more than the risk it treats, or be overtaken by a change already made. Say so plainly, with evidence. What fails is the half-agreement: "agreed in principle", or "agreed, subject to resources", with no action and no date. It reads as agreement and works as a refusal.

This is an illustration. NRIO agreed all four RCFP recommendations because each described a weakness it accepted and could fix within its own budget and authority. The CEO's letter gave, for each, a one-sentence reason and the first action with a date.

## Action plans with owners and dates

An action plan turns a recommendation into work that someone can be asked about. It names an accountable owner senior enough to commit resources, and an action officer who does the work. It breaks the recommendation into actions that can be seen when done, such as an approved procedure or a changed system report, each with a date, a cost and any dependency.

Two parts are easy to leave out. The first is the closure test: write down at the start what evidence will show that the recommendation has been done, so that nobody redefines "done" later. The second is an interim control for any weakness that is live now. A six-month fix leaves six months of exposure, and something should cover it.

This is an illustration. The Director, Regional Programs, approved the plan for Recommendation 2 in June 2028. It had four steps:

- **From Mon 3 Jul 2028, an interim control.** Sam's team checks every RCFP figure against the grants system before clearance, and initials the check.
- **By Thu 31 Aug 2028, the fix.** One reconciled data set, a procedure for keeping it, and a change to the standard report that briefing staff use, so that it draws on that set.
- **September to November 2028, operation.** Three months in which every RCFP figure comes from the reconciled set.
- **By Fri 15 Dec 2028, closure.** Oscar traces a sample of the figures used in those three months back to the reconciled set, and the Director signs.

## Evidence of closure

Close a recommendation when there is evidence that its weakness has been fixed. Evidence comes at three levels:

- **Designed.** A document exists: a procedure, a template, a system specification.
- **Implemented.** The document is in use: staff are briefed and the system has changed.
- **Operating.** Records over a period show that the new way works, and someone independent of the work has checked a sample.

A plan is not evidence at any level, and a procedure alone is evidence of design only. Close on evidence of operation wherever there has been an occasion to operate. Where none can arise before the due date, close on evidence of implementation, say so in every report, and set a check for the first real use.

Decide in advance who closes an entry. A sound rule is that the action officer proposes closure and attaches the evidence, someone independent of the work checks it against the closure test, the accountable owner signs, and the audit committee is told at its next meeting.

This is an illustration. NRIO adopted that rule after the failure described below, with Oscar as the independent checker. Recommendation 1 concerns advice on funding decisions, and none is expected before its due date. NRIO will close it on implementation: an updated procedure, a file-note template in the grants system and a record that every RCFP officer has been briefed. The entry will carry a flag that stays open until Oscar has checked the first ten records of advice in any further round.

```figure

title: Evidence that can close a recommendation
caption: This is an illustration of NRIO's closure test. The evidence is filed with the register entry, so that anyone can check it later.
- It meets the closure test written in the plan | not a test redefined at the end
- Records show the change operating over a period | for example, three months of figures traced to the reconciled set
- Someone independent of the work has checked a sample | at NRIO, the Audit Liaison Officer
- The accountable owner has signed the closure | with the date and the evidence reference
- x A plan, a draft or an approved procedure alone | evidence of design, not of operation
- x An email saying "done" | an assertion, not evidence
- ? Was it closed on implementation only? | then a flag stays open until the first real use is checked
```

## The recommendations register

A recommendations register is the single list of every recommendation an agency has accepted, from the ANAO, internal audit and other reviews, with its status. For any entry it should show the recommendation in full, the response as printed, the owner and action officer, what has been done and what is left, the due dates, the risk rating while open, and the evidence and who verified it. Keep any revised due date beside the original: a register that overwrites its own dates hides every delay.

The register is also how the audit committee sees whether controls are improving. Under s17(2) of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text), the committee's functions "must include reviewing the appropriateness of the accountable authority's" financial reporting, performance reporting, system of risk oversight and management, and system of internal control. Open audit recommendations are evidence on the last two. Chapter 7 covers the committee in full.

This is an illustration. Oscar's register holds the four ANAO recommendations alongside those from NRIO's internal audits. Since the change described below, an entry's status can move forward only on evidence.

```figure
type: steps
title: The statuses of an entry in NRIO's register
caption: This is an illustration. "Implemented" and "Verified" are separate statuses, so a report shows what has been checked and what has only been claimed.
- Agreed | the response as printed in the tabled report
- Planned | owner, actions, dates, costs and the closure test approved
- Under way | actions started; interim control in place if the weakness is live
- Implemented | the action officer attaches evidence and proposes closure
- * Verified | Oscar checks the evidence against the closure test
- Closed | the accountable owner signs; the audit committee is told
```

## Rating the risk of an open recommendation

Due dates do not say which open recommendation matters most today. A risk rating does. It asks how likely it is that the weakness causes a problem before the recommendation is closed, and how bad that problem would be.

This is an illustration. NRIO rates open recommendations on its 5 × 5 risk matrix, using fictional descriptors written for this purpose. For likelihood, that the weakness causes a problem before closure:

- 1, rare: not expected, because no occasion for the weakness to show is expected before the due date;
- 2, unlikely: could happen, but is not expected, because occasions are rare or a control covers the gap;
- 3, possible: could happen in any quarter;
- 4, likely: expected, because the weakness is in weekly use and has caused a problem before;
- 5, almost certain: happening now.

For consequence, in the accountability category:

- 3, moderate: a complaint upheld, or a late or incomplete answer to an applicant, the Ombudsman or a committee;
- 4, major: wrong or unsupported information given to the Minister or Parliament, or a funding decision that cannot be explained from the record.

Scores from 1 to 6 are Low, 7 to 14 Medium and 15 to 25 High.

The [Risk Rating (5 × 5 matrix)](/tools/risk-score/) tool does the same arithmetic: likelihood (1 to 5) times consequence (1 to 5), banded Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14 and High 15 to 25, with a reminder to use your organisation's own descriptors. Use it to check a rating, but rate against your agency's scales.

This is an illustration. When the report was tabled, Recommendation 2 was the most urgent. RCFP figures go into briefs, answers and reports every week, and a figure given at Estimates had already needed correction, so likelihood is 4. A wrong figure given to Parliament is consequence 4. The score is 4 × 4 = 16, High. The interim check from Mon 3 Jul 2028 makes a wrong figure leaving NRIO unlikely, but cannot change what happens if one does: likelihood 2, consequence 4, so 2 × 4 = 8, Medium.

```figure

title: Rating Recommendation 2 while it is open
caption: This is an illustration. The score sorts open recommendations for attention. It does not measure how much risk is left, and 8 is not half the risk of 16.
formula: Risk score = Likelihood × Consequence
- Likelihood | 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain), that the weakness causes a problem before closure
- Consequence | 1 to 5 on NRIO's descriptors; 4 is wrong information given to Parliament
- Bands | Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14, High 15 to 25
step: At tabling, with figures in weekly use and one already corrected: 4 × 4 = 16, High
step: With the interim check from Mon 3 Jul 2028: 2 × 4 = 8, Medium
```

The lower score holds only while the manual check is done every time, so it is no reason to slow the fix. Record both factors, not just the score, and re-rate when either changes.

This is an illustration. NRIO's fictional rule sets the reporting rhythm from the rating: a High open recommendation goes to the CEO every month, and every open recommendation, whatever its band, goes to each quarterly meeting of the audit committee.

## Follow-up by Parliament and in the annual report

Chapter 4 set out the duties of the Joint Committee of Public Accounts and Audit. One of them is "to examine all reports of the Auditor-General" tabled in each House ([Public Accounts and Audit Committee Act 1951](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1951A00060/latest/text), s8(1)(c)). Another is to report to both Houses on matters in those reports, "with any comment it thinks fit" (s8(1)(d)). The Act leaves it to the committee to decide which reports to look at closely and how to follow them up.

If the committee takes evidence, it may summon a person to give evidence and produce documents (s13(1)) and take evidence on oath or affirmation (s10(1)). It must generally take evidence in public (s11(1)), although it can take evidence it considers secret or confidential in private, and must do so if the witness asks (s11(2)). Giving false evidence on oath or affirmation before the committee is an offence (s18). An official who appears should be able to say, from the register, where each recommendation stands.

The committee cannot direct the Auditor-General (s8(1A)), but the Auditor-General must have regard to the audit priorities it sets (Auditor-General Act s10(a)) and may conduct a performance audit "at any time" (s17(1)). A later audit could look at the same program again, including what became of NRIO's agreed recommendations.

Under s17AG(3) of the PGPA Rule, the annual report must include information on "the most significant developments during the period in external scrutiny of the entity, and the entity's response to that scrutiny". That includes reports by the Auditor-General.

This is an illustration. The RCFP report was tabled in June 2028, inside the 2027–28 reporting period, so NRIO's 2027–28 annual report, due to the Minister by 15 October 2028 under PGPA Act s46(2), has to cover it and NRIO's response. What it says about progress should match the register.

## When it goes wrong: closed on a signed procedure

This is an illustration. On Thu 31 Aug 2028 the Director, Regional Programs, signed the procedure for the reconciled RCFP data set. That afternoon Recommendation 2 was marked closed in the register, with the signed procedure as its evidence. Under the rule NRIO used at the time, an entry closed when its accountable owner said so, and Oscar recorded it. The interim check stopped the next day, and the papers for the audit committee's next quarterly meeting reported Recommendation 2 as implemented.

In late September, Clare's unit drafted the external scrutiny section of the 2027–28 annual report, which said Recommendation 2 had been implemented. Before the draft went to the CEO, Oscar tested the claim. He traced the 12 RCFP figures used in briefs and draft answers since 1 September back to their sources. Four of the 12, one in three, came from the old standard report, which had not yet been changed to draw on the reconciled set. The plan's second step was incomplete. All 4 figures happened to be right; the next might not have been.

The cost was contained, but only just. The annual report statement was rewritten before it went to the Minister, so nothing wrong reached Parliament. But the audit committee had been told something untrue and had to be told so. For about four weeks neither the complete fix nor the interim check had been in place. The entry was reopened, its closure date moved from Fri 15 Dec 2028 to Wed 31 Jan 2029 and shown beside the old one.

The plan itself was sound: closure needed three months of operation, checked by Oscar. The failure was a register rule that let an owner close an entry on a document. NRIO changed the rule the same week. "Implemented" and "Verified" became separate statuses, only Oscar can mark an entry verified, and interim controls stay in place until an entry is closed. The interim check restarted the next working day.

## Exercise: rate and plan the four recommendations

This is an illustration. It is June 2028, and the report has just been tabled. Using NRIO's descriptors and bands above, rate each of the four recommendations while it is open. Then, for each, write the plan in one line: the main actions and dates, the closure test and the reporting rhythm. Put the four in order of priority. Recommendation 2 is rated above; include it.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. The four, in order of priority:

| No. | Likelihood × consequence | Band | Main actions and dates | Closure test and date | Reporting |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 4 × 4 = 16 (2 × 4 = 8 with the interim check) | High | Interim check from Mon 3 Jul 2028; reconciled set, procedure and changed standard report by Thu 31 Aug 2028 | Three months of figures, September to November, traced by Oscar to the reconciled set; closed by Fri 15 Dec 2028 | CEO monthly; audit committee quarterly |
| 3 | 3 × 3 = 9 | Medium | Complaint fields, response dates and a quarterly themes report set up by Fri 29 Sep 2028 | Complaints from October to December 2028 recorded with response dates; first themes report to the Director; closed by Wed 31 Jan 2029 | Audit committee quarterly |
| 1 | 2 × 4 = 8 | Medium | Procedure, file-note template and briefing of RCFP staff by Fri 29 Sep 2028 | Closed on implementation by Fri 15 Dec 2028; flag open until the first ten records of advice in any further round are checked | Audit committee quarterly, flag shown |
| 4 | 2 × 3 = 6 | Low | Lessons review approved by Fri 23 Feb 2029, or before any further round opens if that is sooner | Each change traced to updated guidelines or procedures; closed by Fri 30 Mar 2029 | Audit committee quarterly |

**Recommendation 2.** As rated above: 4 × 4 = 16, inside the High band of 15 to 25, and 2 × 4 = 8, Medium, while the interim check runs.

**Recommendation 3.** Complaints about Round 1 can arrive in any quarter: likelihood 3. A complaint lost or answered late is likely to be upheld, and may go to the Ombudsman, as the July 2027 complaint did in August 2027: consequence 3. So 3 × 3 = 9, inside the Medium band of 7 to 14.

**Recommendation 1.** Funding decisions come with a round, none is expected before the due date, and a decision outside a round is rare: likelihood 2. A decision that cannot be explained from the record is consequence 4. So 2 × 4 = 8, Medium.

**Recommendation 4.** No further round is expected before the due date, so likelihood is 2. A round that repeats avoidable weaknesses brings complaints that would be upheld: consequence 3. So 2 × 3 = 6, inside the Low band of 1 to 6.

**Order and rhythm.** The order is 2 (16), 3 (9), 1 (8) and 4 (6). Scores only sort, so watch the factors too. If a further round is announced before Recommendation 1 is closed, its likelihood rises to 3 and its score to 3 × 4 = 12, ahead of Recommendation 3. Recommendation 4's likelihood would rise to 3 as well, giving 3 × 3 = 9. Low does not mean dropped: every agreed ANAO recommendation goes to each audit committee meeting until it is closed on evidence.

**Dates.** Closures run from Fri 15 Dec 2028 to Fri 30 Mar 2029, within ten months of tabling; the High-rated recommendation is among the first and the Low-rated one the last.
