## About this book

Buildings carry a great deal of folded sheet metal that is not roofing: copings on parapets, fascias on canopies, barge cappings on gables, flashings where roofs meet walls and pipes pass through roofs, and the occasional curved fascia or domed finial. Each is a profile, a shape folded from a flat strip, and each has to turn corners, meet other profiles, keep water out and move with the seasons. A century ago, architectural sheet metal workers developed these shapes on the drawing board with dividers and straight-edges. Today most of the same geometry can be done with a calculator, which is faster, checkable and easy to record.

This book develops architectural sheet metal by numbers. It starts with a profile's girth and stretch-out, develops square mitres, mitres at other angles and raking mitres where a horizontal profile meets an inclined one, then turns to flashings at penetrations and walls, copings with their falls, joints, movement and fixings, and finally segmented curved work and gored domes. Every chapter works one problem through the running case, shows how an error is caught and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It assumes basic trigonometry and some folding experience. *Sheet Metal Patterns by the Numbers* covers pipes, elbows, branches and cones, and *Sheet Metal Parts by the Numbers* covers bend allowances in detail; this book links to them rather than repeating them.

## The running case

The book follows the street front of a fictional **light industrial warehouse, W-40**. Its 40 m front parapet, 200 mm thick, carries a folded steel coping. Over the entrance, a canopy 6 m wide projects from the facade, finished with a profiled fascia that turns the canopy's front corners and rises up the canopy's 15° gable ends as a raking barge. A 900 mm square roof hatch and a 150 mm flue pass through the main roof, and the canopy roof meets the facade wall under an apron flashing. A domed finial, 300 mm in diameter, caps a flagpole base on the parapet, and a curved version of the canopy is considered in the last chapter.

All dimensions, profiles, loads, prices and results are invented for teaching. This is an illustration. None of it describes a KEVOS® client, supplier or project. Material is 0.55 mm prepainted metallic-coated steel unless stated, and money is in Australian dollars, excluding GST.

## How to use the book

Chapters 1 to 4 build the geometry of profiles and their mitres, each depending on the one before. Chapters 5 and 6 cover flashings, Chapter 7 copings and cappings, and Chapter 8 curved and gored work. Each method is set out as a table of numbers that can be checked, cut and kept.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The supplied 1922 treatise informed the range of subjects. The methods here are expressed in modern terms with original examples. Weatherproofing details, fixings and wind resistance for real buildings must follow the National Construction Code, the roofing and cladding makers' instructions, the installation code and the wind loading standard, and be checked by a competent person. Roof and facade work is work at height and must be planned under the work health and safety law that applies.
