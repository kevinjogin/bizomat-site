Small engineering businesses usually start with generalists. A few skilled people do everything, each taking a job from drawing to delivery. It works well at low volume: everyone understands the whole product, jobs move without handovers, and holidays are easy to cover. Growth puts that arrangement under strain, and sooner or later someone suggests dividing the work.

This chapter introduces the running case, sets out where the gain from dividing work comes from, and estimates how many people the business would need with and without dividing it. It also shows the first trap: treating a projected gain as if it already existed.

## The pin factory

Adam Smith begins *The Wealth of Nations* with a small manufacture he had seen: pin making. A pin, he observes, passes through about eighteen distinct operations, from drawing out the wire to straightening, cutting, pointing, grinding the head end, making and fitting the head, whitening and papering. One worker making whole pins, unskilled in the trade, might make perhaps one pin a day and certainly not twenty. In a small workshop where ten workers divided the operations between them, Smith reports, they made upwards of forty-eight thousand pins a day.

He gives three reasons for the difference:

1. **Dexterity.** A worker who repeats one operation becomes much quicker and better at it.
2. **Time saved switching.** A worker who moves between operations loses time with each change: putting down one set of tools, picking up another, and getting back into the rhythm of a different task.
3. **Machines.** People whose attention is fixed on one operation are more likely to find a simpler way to do it, and to invent a tool or machine for it.

Smith's figures are an illustration from his own observation, not a measurement anyone could repeat today, and they are not a forecast for any other product. Their value lies in the three reasons, which can each be estimated for a real workshop. A business that knows how much of its labour is lost to switching, how quickly its people learn a new design, and how often a specialist would build a better tool, can predict its own gain. A business that relies on the pin factory's multiple will be disappointed.

He then spends the next two chapters on the conditions. The division of labour arises from people's disposition to trade, and it is limited by the extent of the market: there is no point dividing work so finely that a specialist has nothing to do for half the week. Later he adds that a stock of materials and tools must be built up before work can be divided, and, in Book V, that very narrow work dulls the judgement of the people who do it. Those qualifications matter as much as the pins, and this book gives each its own chapter.

## The case: from six benches to fifteen

This is an illustration. A regional stainless steel fabrication business makes hygienic benches for food processing plants. Three fabricators each build whole benches. A fabricator takes a job card and a drawing, cuts and folds the top and undershelf, fits up and welds the leg frame, polishes the welds and surfaces, assembles, inspects and wraps. The owner quotes, draws, buys materials and arranges freight.

Demand has been steady at about six benches a week. A distributor now offers a contract for a new bench design, the B-series, at fifteen a week for at least a year. The B-series has a folded top with coved corners, a welded box-section leg frame and an adjustable undershelf.

The owner times the first few B-series benches built by the generalists, as each fabricator would build them once they are familiar with the design. The planning figures per bench are:

- cut and fold the top and shelf: **100 minutes**;
- fit up and weld the leg frame: **180 minutes**;
- polish, assemble, inspect and wrap: **160 minutes**;
- switching between tasks, including setting up and putting away welding gear, changing abrasives and finding tools: **about 45 minutes**.

That is **485 minutes**, just over 8 hours, of labour per bench.

## How many people it would take

The [staffing need tool](/tools/staffing-need/) in BIZOMAT Tools converts a workload into a staffing requirement. It multiplies the units required by the labour minutes per unit to get workload hours, divides by each person's productive hours (available hours multiplied by an efficiency), and reports the full-time equivalents needed and the whole number of people. Its own guidance notes that rounding up is only a screen: skill mix, coverage, leave and variation also matter.

The business works about 250 days a year, so fifteen benches a week is about 750 benches a year. The owner allows 1,520 available hours a year per person, after leave, public holidays, training and toolbox meetings, and an efficiency of 85% for the time actually spent on bench work.

With generalists at 485 minutes a bench, the tool gives a workload of **6,062.5 hours**, 1,292 productive hours per person, and **4.69 full-time equivalents**: five people.

The owner then sketches a divided arrangement, which later chapters examine in detail. Three stations: cutting and folding; welding; polishing, assembly and inspection. Switching between tasks largely disappears, the welders get faster with repetition and a fixture, and the station times, including moving each bench on, add up to an estimated **372 minutes** a bench. At that figure, the tool gives **4,650 hours** and **3.60 full-time equivalents**: four people.

The gain is real but modest: about 23% fewer labour minutes per bench, and about one person over a year. This is typical of small businesses. Smith's pin factory produced an enormous multiple because pin making was broken into eighteen tiny operations and repeated thousands of times a day. A bench is built from a few substantial operations, fifteen times a week. The principle is the same; the size of the gain depends on how often each operation repeats.

## What dividing the work is not

Dividing work by operation is not the only way to split a growing workload, and the alternatives are worth naming because they are often confused with it.

- **Dividing by product.** Each fabricator takes one product line: one builds benches, another trolleys, another custom work. Each still does every operation on their products. This spreads the load but gains little of what Smith described, because each person still switches between operations all day.
- **Dividing by customer.** One person looks after the distributor, another the direct customers. Useful for relationships, but in the workshop it is the same as dividing by product.
- **Dividing by skill level.** A senior fabricator does the hard parts and an apprentice the easy ones. This is a real division of labour, often a good one, and it is a partial version of the station arrangement in this book.

The owner's station plan divides by operation, which is where the switching time, dexterity and machine-invention gains come from. It is also where the risks of narrow work and fragile coverage are greatest, which is why the later chapters weigh both.

### The first division is already in place

The business already divides one piece of work: the owner quotes, draws, buys and arranges freight, and the fabricators make. That separation of office work from workshop work is the first division of labour in almost every small business, usually made without anyone calling it that. It has the same gains and costs as any other: the owner has become quick at quoting, but nobody else can do it, and when the owner is away, quotes wait. Chapter 8 returns to that office work when the contract increases it, and shows how a specialised role can create queues.

## What generalists do well

Before reorganising, it is worth recording what the current arrangement does well, because a station layout can lose it. Each generalist knows the whole bench, so they catch a fold that will not suit the leg frame before the frame is welded. Each bench has one owner, so quality problems have a name attached and are fixed quickly. There are no handovers, so no bench waits between stations. And any fabricator can cover for any other.

Smith himself noticed that the country labourer, doing a variety of work, often showed more judgement than the specialised workman. The owner does not need to agree with Smith's harsh language about narrow work to see the practical point: the station arrangement should keep some of these strengths deliberately, through cross-training, rotation and shared inspection, rather than lose them by accident. Chapter 9 sets out how.

## Where the gain actually comes from

The table makes the sources of the gain visible, and each one has conditions attached.

- **Switching time** is the largest single item: about 45 minutes a bench. It disappears only if each person really stays at one station, which needs a steady flow of work to that station. Chapter 3 values it.
- **Welding time** falls from 180 to 116 minutes, through learning and a fixture. Learning needs repetition, and the fixture needs someone to design it, which a dedicated welder is more likely to do. Chapters 2 and 4 examine both.
- **Cutting and folding** gains a few minutes from dexterity. Polishing and assembly change little in total, but Chapter 6 shows that their placement matters for balance.

None of these gains appears on the day the stations are created. Learning takes many repetitions, fixtures take weeks to design and prove, and new habits take time to settle. That leads to the first failure case.

## The failure case: counting the gain before it exists

The owner's first plan was straightforward. Sign the contract for fifteen benches a week from the first week, reorganise into three stations with the existing three fabricators plus one new hire, and rely on the 372-minute estimate.

In the first four weeks the business delivered between nine and eleven benches a week. The welding station was the problem. The welder was still learning the B-series frame, the fixture did not yet exist, and the polishing station kept running out of work and then being swamped. Overtime filled part of the gap, at higher cost; the distributor's first two deliveries were short.

The plan had treated a projection as a fact. The 372 minutes was the figure the stations should reach once learning and the fixture were in place. In the first weeks the real figure was much closer to the generalists' 485, and for the welding station worse, because one person now carried all the early-unit learning on the new design.

### How it is detected

The cheapest detection is to measure. From the first week, the owner records the actual minutes per bench at each station from job cards and a simple start and finish log. Plotted weekly against the projected figures, the gap shows which station is behind and by how much. Waiting for missed deliveries to reveal the problem costs a customer's patience.

### How it is corrected

The correction is a **ramp plan** agreed with the distributor: nine benches a week in the first month, twelve in the second, fifteen from the third, with the dates tied to measured station times rather than to the calendar alone. A contract welder covers part of the welding load for the first six weeks, and the fixture is given priority. The owner also stops treating 372 minutes as a single number and starts treating it as three targets, one per station, each with its own evidence.

## Exercise: before the contract

Before the distributor's offer, the owner had considered dividing the work at a lower volume of ten benches a week, about 500 a year. Using the staffing need tool with the same available hours and efficiency, how many people would each arrangement need at 500 benches a year? What does the comparison suggest?

**Worked answer.** For generalists at 485 minutes, the tool gives a workload of **4,041.7 hours** and **3.13 full-time equivalents**, which rounds up to four people with 0.87 of a person spare. For stations at 372 minutes, it gives **3,100 hours** and **2.40 full-time equivalents**, three people with 0.6 spare.

At ten benches a week the divided arrangement saves a whole person on paper, because the generalists' requirement sits just above three. But the comparison relies on the same projected 372 minutes, which depends on enough repetition at each station to achieve the learning and keep each person busy. Chapter 5 asks whether ten benches a week is enough for that. The tool's answer is a useful screen, and a reason to check the conditions, not a reason to reorganise.
