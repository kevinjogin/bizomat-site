## About this book

A sheet metal fitting starts as a flat shape on a sheet. Cut it out, roll or fold it, close the seam, and it becomes a pipe end cut to sit on a sloping roof, an elbow made of five short pieces, a branch that meets its main without a gap, a reducer, a hood or a transition from a fan's rectangular outlet to a round duct. The flat shape is the pattern, and setting it out is the skill that sits underneath all of that work. Get the pattern right and the fitting goes together with a mallet and a few rivets. Get it wrong and no amount of hammering will close the gap.

This book sets out patterns with numbers. Every chapter shows the geometry by the traditional drawing method and then calculates the same lines, so that a pattern can be set out on the bench, in a spreadsheet or checked against a CAD unfold. It works through the three methods of development, parallel line, radial line and triangulation, in the order they are usually learned, then adds the seam, edge and flange allowances that turn a net pattern into one that can be made, and finishes with checking, nesting and keeping patterns.

It is for sheet metal workers and apprentices, fabrication drafters, and engineers who order or check ductwork, hoods and fittings. It assumes school algebra and trigonometry. Each chapter is worked through with one running case, includes one plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional job. This is an illustration. A joinery workshop is extending its dust extraction system and has asked a local sheet metal shop to make a new branch line. The fittings are made from 0.8 mm galvanised steel sheet. Duct sizes are inside diameters, because each piece must slip over the spigot of the next, and patterns are developed on the mean line of the metal.

- A **transition** from the fan's 300 × 200 mm rectangular outlet to a Ø250 round duct, 350 mm long.
- A **Ø250 main duct** passing through a roof pitched at 20°, with its end cut to match.
- A **90° segmented elbow** in Ø250, five pieces on a 375 mm centreline radius.
- A **Ø150 branch** meeting the Ø250 main square, and a second Ø150 branch meeting it at 45°.
- A **concentric reducer** from Ø250 to Ø200, 200 mm long, and an **eccentric reducer** from Ø200 to Ø150, 250 mm long, flat along one side.
- A **conical hood** for a sander, Ø450 at the mouth and Ø150 at the neck, with its mouth cut on a 20° slant to sit against the machine's guard.

The workshop, the shop, the mistakes and the dimensions are invented. None describes KEVOS® work or a real business.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first: they set the conventions used everywhere else, including what a girth is, why patterns are developed on the mean line and how cut lines are numbered. After that, the chapters on each kind of fitting can be read in any order, although triangulation in Chapter 7 is easier after radial line development in Chapter 5. Chapter 9 on allowances applies to every pattern in the book.

Calculated values are given to 0.1 mm, more precision than a scriber can mark, so that a reader checking the arithmetic can see where each number came from. On the bench, a pattern marked to within about 0.5 mm is good work for 0.8 mm sheet.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The geometry of development is exact, and every calculation in this book was checked by running it. Seam widths, wire sizes and other allowances are illustrations: the right values depend on the machines, the sheet and the specification for the job, and the book shows how to calibrate them with trial strips.

Ductwork for dust extraction is part of a system that controls a health hazard, and in some cases a fire or explosion hazard. Its design, airflow, materials and installation are matters for a competent designer and for Australian work health and safety requirements, including Safe Work Australia's guidance on wood dust and the specification for the job. This book teaches the geometry of patterns. It is not a design for any duct system.
