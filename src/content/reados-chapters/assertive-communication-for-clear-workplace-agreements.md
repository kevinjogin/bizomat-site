An unclear agreement can begin with a small hesitation. You accept a deadline without explaining the work already promised, hint that information is missing or postpone feedback because the conversation may be uncomfortable. The immediate interaction stays pleasant while the unresolved problem moves into the delivery schedule.

The management text *Australian management essentials* describes assertiveness as expressing your position directly while respecting other people. That principle is useful for requests, boundaries and everyday feedback. The method below turns it into a practical way to reach clear agreements, with attention to evidence, decision authority and the possibility that a reasonable conversation may still end without agreement.

## Define assertiveness through behaviour

**Assertiveness** is communicating your needs, observations and position clearly while recognising that other people have legitimate needs and perspectives. It is expressed in what you say and how you handle the response. It does not require a forceful personality or a particular speaking style.

**Aggressive communication** tries to secure an outcome through intimidation, insults or disregard for the other person's position. **Passive communication** leaves an important need or boundary unexpressed. These descriptions concern behaviour in an interaction; they are not permanent labels for the person involved.

An assertive response states the issue, explains its relevance and identifies the next action or decision. It allows the other person to add information and disagree. Being direct does not require assuming that your first interpretation is correct.

The purpose is a more accurate working agreement. You may reach a compromise, maintain a boundary or discover that a different person must make the decision. A conversation can be handled well even when the requested outcome is unavailable.

## Prepare the outcome and the boundary

Before a difficult discussion, write down what you want the conversation to resolve. A useful outcome might be an agreed priority, a complete specification or a change in how feedback is provided. Keep it narrow enough that the other person can respond to it.

A **boundary** is a limit on what you can commit to or accept in a particular situation. It may concern available capacity, your decision authority or the scope of an existing agreement. Establish what is fixed and where you have room to negotiate.

Separate the preferred outcome from the underlying need. You may prefer a document by Tuesday because you need time to review it before a Thursday decision. If the document cannot be ready, there may be another way to obtain the essential information. Explaining the need creates room for alternatives without abandoning the objective.

Check your authority as well. You can describe a capacity problem and propose options, but you may not be entitled to move another team's deadline unilaterally. Knowing who can decide prevents a clear conversation from ending with an unauthorised commitment.

## Start with observations rather than accusations

An **observation** describes something that can be examined, such as a missing field or a missed agreed date. An **interpretation** explains what you think it means. Confusing the two can turn a practical issue into an argument about motives.

Describe the event and its effect on the work. A request arrived without the required dimensions, so preparation could not begin. That gives the other person something specific to confirm or correct. Saying that they never care about detail makes a broader claim and provides little direction.

Use your own perspective where appropriate. Explain what information you have and what you need rather than claiming to speak for everyone. Ownership of your statement makes its basis clearer, but adding the word I does not make an accusation constructive by itself.

Allow for an explanation you have not considered. The requirement may have been unclear, the date may have changed elsewhere or the person may have sent information through another channel. Clarifying those facts is part of resolving the problem, not a retreat from being direct.

## Make requests specific enough to answer

A **request** asks another person to take an action or provide a decision. State what you need, when it is needed and why the timing matters. Leave enough room for the person to tell you whether it is possible.

Avoid hints that depend on the recipient inferring your priority. Mentioning that the report would be useful soon may be interpreted as a general preference. Identifying the section needed for a scheduled review makes the request actionable.

| Unclear approach | More useful approach | What becomes explicit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hint that work is late | Identify the agreed date and ask for the current position | The commitment being discussed |
| Ask for something soon | Name the required action and relevant deadline | The timing and deliverable |
| Say a process is frustrating | Describe the repeated event and its effect | The practical problem |
| Ask someone to communicate better | Specify the update needed and when | The behaviour to change |

After making the request, listen to the answer. If the person cannot meet it, explore the constraint before repeating the same demand. You may need a smaller deliverable, a different sequence or a decision about which priority takes precedence.

## Decline or renegotiate without leaving ambiguity

A clear refusal identifies what cannot be agreed and, where useful, why. It does not need a long defence, but it should give enough information for the other person to understand the practical limit. Keep the explanation factual and relevant.

Where you have discretion, offer alternatives you can actually deliver. You might be able to complete a smaller part by the requested date or the full task later. An alternative that is equally unrealistic only postpones the same conversation.

Avoid an ambiguous agreement intended to reduce immediate discomfort. Saying you will try can be appropriate when the uncertainty is explicit, but it can also be heard as a commitment. State whether the date is confirmed, provisional or not achievable under current conditions.

If the request comes from someone who controls priorities, explain the trade-off and ask them to choose. This is different from claiming that your existing workload can never change. The issue is which commitment moves when another is added.

## Listen without surrendering the point

Listening means trying to understand the other person's information and reasoning. It does not require accepting every claim or withdrawing a legitimate concern. Summarise what you have heard, then identify what remains unresolved.

Ask questions that help distinguish the underlying need from the proposed solution. A person requesting a complete report may actually need one figure for a decision. A customer asking for immediate delivery may have a fixed event date that makes partial delivery useful.

The guidance in [Open and closed questions: when to use each, and why it matters](/reados/collections/communication-and-professional-presence/open-and-closed-questions/) can help structure this part of the discussion. Open questions explore the situation; focused questions confirm the details on which an agreement depends.

Return to the original issue when the conversation drifts. Do so by restating the decision required rather than repeating a slogan or ignoring new information. Consistency is useful when it preserves the purpose of the discussion; rigidity is less useful when the facts have changed.

## Give feedback about work and consequences

**Feedback** is information about an action or result that can help someone understand or improve their work. Effective feedback describes a specific event, its impact and the change needed. It should be detailed enough that the recipient can recognise what you are referring to.

Choose a setting that allows a proper response. Everyday corrective feedback is usually easier to discuss without an unnecessary audience. If an immediate intervention is needed to prevent a problem, address the immediate work and arrange a fuller discussion later.

Distinguish a one-off error from a recurring pattern and explain the evidence for either. Avoid words such as always and never unless they are literally justified. Overstatement gives the recipient a reason to argue with the description instead of examining the issue.

Agree what happens next. That may involve clearer instructions, a revised handover or a check on the next attempt. If the expected improvement depends on resources or authority the person lacks, address those conditions rather than treating the problem entirely as attitude.

## Receive feedback without deciding too quickly

When you receive criticism, first identify the specific claim. Ask for an example if the statement is broad. A clear example lets you examine the work, while a vague judgement about your personality may need to be translated into an observable behaviour.

You can acknowledge an effect without agreeing with every explanation offered. Perhaps an update arrived too late, but the claim that you deliberately withheld information is unsupported. Separate the timing problem from the interpretation of intent and respond to each accurately.

If the feedback identifies a mistake, say what you will correct and when. If relevant information is missing, explain what needs checking before you respond fully. An immediate answer is not always the most careful answer.

Do not use a request for evidence to avoid all responsibility. The purpose is to understand the issue well enough to act. Once the relevant facts are clear, make the next step equally clear.

## Worked example: resolving competing deadlines

This is an illustration. A project coordinator in a small Australian business has 12 available work hours before Thursday after existing fixed commitments. Two agreed tasks require eight hours for a customer summary and four hours for supplier information. A colleague requests another six-hour presentation for the same deadline.

The total proposed work is 18 hours: eight plus four plus six. Against 12 available hours, that leaves a six-hour gap. Agreeing to all three without changing anything would create an unsupported commitment.

The coordinator explains the capacity position and asks what the presentation must achieve. The colleague needs three decision points for an internal meeting, rather than a polished deck. Preparing a concise outline is estimated to require two hours.

Even with that reduced scope, the total is 14 hours. The coordinator therefore brings two options to the person authorised to set priorities: move two hours of supplier work to the following period, or obtain two hours of help from someone with the necessary context. The calculation remains visible instead of disappearing behind an expression of willingness.

| Option | Work before Thursday | Consequence to agree |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Accept all original requests | 18 hours | Exceeds available capacity by six hours |
| Reduce presentation to an outline | 14 hours | Still exceeds capacity by two hours |
| Outline plus deferral of two supplier hours | 12 hours | Supplier timing must be revised |
| Outline plus two hours of suitable support | 12 coordinator hours, two support hours | Support must be available and briefed |

The decision-maker chooses the deferral after checking that the supplier task can move. The coordinator confirms the revised deliverables and dates with the affected people. The agreement is clear about both the new work and the commitment changed to accommodate it.

This conversation does not depend on winning an argument. It depends on accurate information, a specific request, alternatives and a decision by someone with authority. If the estimates later change, the coordinator must update the agreement rather than treat the initial calculation as permanent proof of capacity.

## Record the agreement and the remaining uncertainty

A useful conversation can still lead to confusion if participants remember different outcomes. Confirm the action, owner, timing and any condition that remains unresolved. Keep the record proportionate to the importance of the commitment.

Distinguish a decision from an option that was merely discussed. If the group considered moving a deadline but did not obtain approval, say that the change remains pending. This protects people from planning around a possibility that was never confirmed.

When there is no agreement, record the specific unresolved point and the next route for deciding it. That may be another discussion after information is gathered or a review by the appropriate manager. A clear unresolved issue is more useful than a false impression of consensus.

Follow through on your own part. Assertive wording loses practical value if the promised update or action does not occur. The quality of the agreement depends on what people can rely on after the conversation ends.

## Account for power and communication differences

The same wording can have different effects depending on who is speaking and who controls the work. A manager's request may be heard as an instruction even when presented casually. Make the decision status clear and allow people to describe constraints without treating that information as disloyalty.

Do not make eye contact, a firm voice or quick responses compulsory tests of assertiveness. People differ in culture, disability, language and preferred communication methods. A clear written response or time to prepare may support a better discussion.

If a conversation becomes threatening or abusive, focus on ending the immediate interaction safely and using the appropriate support or reporting channel. Assertiveness is not an obligation to remain in an unsafe exchange or to resolve every conflict personally.

This article concerns ordinary communication and work agreements. Formal employment, safety or legal matters need the relevant procedures and current guidance from an appropriate adviser or authority; a conversational technique does not replace those requirements.

## Practise on manageable situations

Choose a recurring, relatively low-stakes situation for initial practice. Clarifying an incomplete request or confirming a meeting outcome can help you develop the structure before using it in a more difficult discussion. Prepare the observation, impact and request in advance.

Afterwards, review whether the other person understood the issue and whether the next action became clearer. Do not assess the attempt solely by whether they agreed with you. Their response may reflect constraints that your wording cannot remove.

Ask a trusted colleague to review a draft or rehearse a conversation if that would help. Focus on whether the wording is accurate, specific and respectful. Avoid memorising a script so rigidly that you stop listening when the real discussion takes a different direction.

As the method becomes familiar, use less preparation for routine exchanges while retaining care for consequential ones. The aim is to make direct communication an ordinary part of coordinating work, rather than a performance reserved for major conflict.

## Common mistakes that weaken the conversation

One mistake is explaining so extensively that the request disappears. Provide the facts needed for the decision, then state what you need. Additional detail can follow if it is relevant to the response.

Another is using polite language to conceal an ultimatum you cannot authorise. Be clear about your own limit and the decision that belongs to somebody else. Respectful wording does not make an unsupported threat appropriate.

A third is repeating your position without considering new information. The text's advice to remain clear is useful, but clarity should coexist with learning. Change your view when the facts justify it and explain the reason.

Finally, avoid treating compromise as automatically desirable. Some limits cannot be traded away in a casual conversation. Identify which parts are negotiable and use the appropriate decision process for the rest.

## Questions to ask

- What specific issue should this conversation resolve?
- Which observations support my description of the problem?
- What do I need, and which parts are open to negotiation?
- Does the other person have information that could change my view?
- Who has authority to approve the resulting commitment?
- What action, owner and timing should we confirm afterwards?

## Bringing it together

Assertive communication makes a position clear while preserving room for evidence, disagreement and workable alternatives. It helps people understand the real limits and decisions behind a request.

Prepare the purpose, describe the facts, make a specific request and listen to the response. Finish with an accurate agreement or a clear next step for resolving what remains open.
