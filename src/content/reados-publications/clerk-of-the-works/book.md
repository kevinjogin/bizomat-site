## About this book

Geoffrey Chaucer is remembered as a poet. For most of his adult life he was also a working official of the English crown. From 1374 he was controller of customs in the port of London, required to keep the rolls of his office in his own hand. From July 1389 to June 1391 he was clerk of the king's works, responsible for repairs and building at Westminster Palace, the Tower of London and a string of royal manors. He bought materials, paid workers, carried cash and accounted for every penny to the Exchequer. In 1390 he was robbed on the road while carrying the king's money.

His poetry is full of the same world. The Canterbury pilgrims include a reeve who kept accounts no auditor could fault, a manciple who bought so shrewdly that he outwitted the lawyers he served, a host who drew up the rules of a contest and appointed himself judge, and a canon's yeoman who had spent years on experiments that never worked. The Knight's Tale describes a theatre built so that no spectator blocks another's view. And Chaucer wrote an instruction manual for a ten-year-old.

This book uses that material to teach the owner's side of a small construction project. It is written for owner's representatives, clerks of works, project engineers and managers of small organisations who commission work they cannot build themselves. It assumes you can read a budget and a simple drawing. It assumes no knowledge of Chaucer or Middle English: every quotation is short and explained.

## How to use the book

Read the chapters in order, because the case runs through them from the brief to the final account. Each chapter opens with a piece of Chaucer, states what it teaches in modern terms, and applies it to the case with real numbers. Every chapter ends with an exercise and a worked answer. Where a BIZOMAT tool performs a calculation the chapter uses, the text links to it and says what it does and does not do.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A regional agricultural society in New South Wales is replacing its 1950s timber grandstand with a 480-seat steel grandstand: 12 rows of 40 seats under a roof, with a kiosk and toilets beneath. The society's approved budget is A$1,180,000 excluding GST, made up of a head contract with a builder of A$1,020,000, seats bought directly by the society for A$86,400, design and certification fees of A$48,000 and services connections of A$25,600. GST is extra at 10%. The society appoints a clerk of works, a part-time engineer, to act on its behalf, and the book follows that person's work over a 26-week build.

The society, the builder, the suppliers, the site and every figure are invented for teaching. They are not real organisations, and they are not BIZOMAT work, clients or claims.

## An independent treatment

Chaucer's works are quoted from public-domain editions, and the facts of his working life are taken from the published records and the National Archives of the United Kingdom, cited so that you can check them. *The Cambridge Companion to Chaucer*, supplied for this book, was read for orientation and is credited; its text, structure and examples are not reproduced. The book uses Chaucer as a lens on practice. It makes no claim about what Chaucer intended, and where scholars disagree, for example over who wrote the short poem to Adam the scribe, it says so.

A grandstand is a public building with real safety consequences. In Australia its design, classification and approval are governed by the National Construction Code and state law, construction work is regulated under work health and safety laws, and the contract will usually follow a standard form such as AS 4000. This book is educational. It is not engineering, legal or contractual advice. Structural design, sightline and egress design, certification and contract administration on a real project belong to qualified, registered people working within the applicable law, standards and contract.
