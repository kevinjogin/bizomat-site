A question taken on notice is a promise made in the hearing room: the agency will answer in writing later. The written answer is evidence to the committee. Committees commonly publish answers, and Senators read them beside the transcript and against everything else the agency has said. A late answer invites a sharp question at the next hearing. A vague one invites three more. An answer that contradicts the transcript, or another answer, can become the story.

This is an illustration. NRIO's Budget estimates hearing ended on Wednesday 2 June 2027 (Chapter 2). The committee asked 46 questions on notice, 19 of them about Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP), and set Friday 16 July 2027 as its date for answers. That date, like every hearing date and deadline in the case, is fictional. Lucy Tran tracks the questions. Clare Whitfield is accountable for getting every answer to the committee, accurate and on time, after the Minister's office has cleared it.

## Where questions on notice come from

Questions reach an agency in two ways. A witness takes a question on notice at the hearing rather than guessing (Chapter 2), or a Senator puts a written question through the committee afterwards. Either way, the committee sends the questions and sets the date for answers.

The date is the committee's, and it can differ from one committee and one hearing to the next. Read the committee's instructions every time: the date, the format, how answers are lodged and what to do if one will be late. This book gives no real deadline.

An answer is information given in response to a request made for official purposes, so the Code of Conduct's duty not to provide false or misleading information applies to the people who prepare it (subsection 13(9) of the [Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text); Chapter 1). Treat that duty as reaching everyone who supplies a fact for an answer, not only the person who approves it. An answer can be accurate in every word and still mislead, if it leaves out the fact that changes the picture.

## Log every question the day it arrives

A register is the single list that says, for every question, what was asked, who owns it, where it is and when it is due. A spreadsheet is enough; a trail of emails is not.

This is an illustration. NRIO's register has these fields.

| Field | What Lucy records |
|---|---|
| Number | NRIO's own number, Q01 to Q46, and the committee's reference |
| Words | The question exactly as asked, from the transcript or the committee's list |
| Source | Taken on notice at the hearing, with the transcript page, or a written question |
| Owner | One named person, and any contributors |
| Related record | Hearing evidence, briefs, earlier answers and correspondence on the same subject |
| Dates | Internal dates for the draft, each clearance and the Minister's office; the committee's date |
| Status | Allocated, drafting, content cleared, read across, approved, with the office, lodged |

On the evening of the hearing Lucy reconciled Ravi Menon's log from the table with her own and entered every question. By Wednesday 9 June 2027 the committee had sent its list of NRIO's 46 questions, and Lucy had matched each one to the register.

Copy each question exactly: a question paraphrased on day one is answered wrongly on day thirty. Until the transcript is out, mark the words provisional. If a question can be read two ways, answer the words and say how you have read them.

## One owner for each answer

Every question has one owner: a named person in the area that holds the facts. The owner drafts the answer, finds the evidence and answers for its accuracy. The parliamentary unit owns the process, not the facts. It chases dates and checks quality and consistency, but never writes content the owner has not confirmed.

When a question spans two areas, name one lead owner and list the others as contributors. Two owners means no owner. Send each owner the exact words, the related record and the dates in one message, and ask them to confirm within a day that the question is theirs.

## Triage with a priority score

Every question will be answered by the committee's date. Triage decides the order of work: which answers are drafted first, get senior time early and go to the Minister's office first.

Some questions should not be ranked at all, because their place at the front is obvious. This is an illustration. Lucy took four out of the scoring. Q03 and Q04 ask how many Round 1 applications were received, found ineligible and assessed: the subject of the CEO's evidence corrected by letter on Friday 4 June 2027 (Chapters 2 and 10). Q08 and Q09 ask why the Minister approved the two grants against officials' recommendations. The reasons are the Minister's to give, so the office needs NRIO's facts early.

The [Work Priority Score](/tools/priority/) puts the rest on one scale. It rates impact, urgency, risk if delayed and effort, each from 1 to 5, and weights them 35, 30, 20 and 15 per cent. Effort is reversed as ease, 6 minus effort, so smaller jobs score higher. The weighted sum is divided by 5 and multiplied by 100: HIGH at 70 or more, MEDIUM from 45 to 69, LOW below 45. The tool keeps mandatory safety, legal and compliance work outside the scoring, the same logic that sets aside questions whose place is already fixed.

This is an illustration. NRIO wrote down what each rating means for questions on notice, so everyone scores the same way.

- **Impact:** how much the answer matters to the committee and to confidence in the program.
- **Urgency:** how soon work must start, given how long the facts take to find and how many people must clear them.
- **Risk if delayed:** the harm of a late or rushed answer, such as a misleading figure or an issue the Minister learns of too late.
- **Effort:** 1 for a figure already in a published report, 5 for new analysis across many files.

Q07 asks, for each grant approved against officials' recommendations, the amount, the date of the decision and what officials had recommended. NRIO rated it impact 5, urgency 4, risk if delayed 4 and effort 3.

```figure

title: Scoring Q07 with the Work Priority Score
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's ratings for Q07. The score orders the drafting work; every question is still answered by the committee's date.
formula: Score = (0.35 × impact + 0.30 × urgency + 0.20 × risk if delayed + 0.15 × (6 − effort)) ÷ 5 × 100
- Impact, urgency, risk if delayed | each rated 1 to 5 on NRIO's descriptions
- Effort | rated 1 to 5, then reversed as ease: 6 − effort
- Bands | HIGH 70 or more; MEDIUM 45 to 69; LOW below 45

step: = 1.75 + 1.20 + 0.80 + 0.45 = 4.20
step: 4.20 ÷ 5 × 100 = 84, HIGH
```

The score informs judgement; it does not replace it. Clare can move a question, with the reason in the register. Compare scores only between questions rated by the same people on the same descriptions, and record the four ratings as well as the score.

## Drafting an answer that answers the question

The best answers are short, direct and plain.

- **Answer the question asked, in the first sentence.** If it asks "how many", start with a number. Use the question's own terms.
- **Answer every part,** in the same order and with the same numbering.
- **Define every figure:** what it measures, the date or period, whether it includes GST, and its source.
- **Give facts, not argument.** Words such as "rigorous" and "robust" claim quality without evidence, and invite a question asking for it.
- **Leave policy views to the Minister.** Explain the policy as the government has stated it and flag the question to the Minister's office (Chapter 2).
- **Say what you cannot give, and why.** Never leave part of a question out without saying so.

Sometimes information should not be released, such as personal information about applicants, or material the government may want to withhold in the public interest. Withholding is never the drafter's decision, and never done by omission. The Senate's own orders set out how a claim to withhold information is made. Raise it with your parliamentary unit at once, so the legal area and the Minister's office can consider it in time.

## Clearance and the Minister's office

Clearance is a chain of checks, each by a person who can see something the others cannot. Each step should be quick, because the committee's date does not move.

```figure
type: steps
title: How an answer is cleared at NRIO
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's clearance chain is fictional. Your agency's chain, and its arrangements with the Minister's office, will differ.
- Owner drafts | the answer, with the evidence attached and every figure sourced
- Content clearance | the Director, Regional Programs, the CFO or Clare, by group, confirms the facts
- Read-across | Lucy checks format; Clare checks the answer against the record and every related answer
- CEO approval | the CEO approves the answer for NRIO
- Minister's office | the office clears the batch; any change it proposes comes back to the owner and Clare
- * Lodged | NRIO lodges the cleared answer with the committee
```

The Minister's office clears answers because the Minister answers to the Parliament for the portfolio (Chapter 1). Most changes it proposes are improvements. Test each one as you tested the first draft: is it accurate, and does it still answer the question? If a change would make an answer inaccurate or misleading, the owner says so in writing to the parliamentary unit, which raises it with the office and, if it is not resolved, with the agency head. Wording and emphasis can be negotiated. Accuracy cannot. Keep the exchanges appropriately confidential, as subsection 13(6) of the Public Service Act requires for dealings with a Minister's staff, and record in the agency's files what changed and why.

This is an illustration. The Minister's office asked NRIO for five working days to clear each batch.

## Planning back from the committee's date

Plan backwards from the committee's date, not forwards from the hearing. Start with the day the last batch must reach the Minister's office and work back to the date each draft is due.

Send answers in batches. Batches spread the office's load, bring problems to light early and let answers be lodged as they are cleared. Group them by topic as well as priority, so answers that share facts are checked together.

Leave a margin: a plan whose last step lands on the committee's date has no room for one slow clearance. If an answer will still be late, tell the committee before its date passes, through the channel its instructions name, with the reason and the date the answer will come. A late answer with notice is a problem. A late answer without notice looks like avoidance.

## Consistency with what has already been said

Every answer joins an existing record: the transcript, any correction already sent, published guidelines and reports, media statements and earlier answers. Senators read them together. An answer that contradicts the record is a problem even if it is right, because the agency must then say which one is wrong.

This is an illustration. Lucy keeps a settled facts sheet for the RCFP, cleared by the Director, Regional Programs. It holds each figure once, with its definition and source. Every RCFP answer takes its numbers from the sheet, and a new figure goes on the sheet before it goes in an answer. Grant amounts exclude GST, as they do throughout the RCFP's records.

| Fact | Figure |
|---|---|
| Round 1 applications received | 186 |
| Found ineligible, and not assessed | 9 |
| Assessed | 177 (186 − 9) |
| Grants approved by the Minister on Monday 31 May 2027 | 31, worth A$9.98 million |
| Approved against officials' recommendations | 2: Corran Bay Progress Association Inc., A$140,000, and Yarrowmere Showground Committee Inc., A$260,000; A$400,000 in all |
| Evidence corrected | The CEO's letter to the committee of Friday 4 June 2027 |

Before an answer goes to the CEO, Clare reads it against the sheet, the transcript and every other answer on the same topic, including any already lodged. That read-across is quick when answers on one topic travel in one batch, and unreliable when they are scattered.

## When it goes wrong: two answers that disagreed

This is an illustration. At NRIO's previous Estimates appearance, before the settled facts sheet and the read-across existed, two Senators asked about consultants. One asked what NRIO had spent on consultants in 2025–26. The finance team answered from NRIO's accounts: A$1.42 million. The other asked for the value of consultancy contracts NRIO had entered into in 2025–26. The procurement team answered from NRIO's contract records: A$1.90 million.

Both figures were right. One was money paid during the year; the other was the value of contracts signed during the year, part of it payable later. Neither answer said so. They were cleared by different managers and lodged in different batches, and nobody at NRIO read them side by side. A Senator's office did.

At the next hearing a Senator asked which figure was true and where the difference had gone: A$1.90 million − A$1.42 million = A$480,000. The CEO could not reconcile them at the table, and NRIO wrote a third answer to explain the first two. Nothing was wrong except the presentation, yet it cost hearing time, a further answer and a transcript suggesting NRIO did not know its own numbers. A genuine error presented that way would have looked like concealment.

Three controls would have caught it, and NRIO now uses all three. Every figure in an answer states what it measures and for what period. A settled facts sheet holds each figure once, with its definition. And one person reads every answer on a topic together, earlier answers included, before any goes to the CEO. Q30 in this round, on spending on consultants in 2026–27, goes through all three.
