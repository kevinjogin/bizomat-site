Communication is often treated as a talent people either have or lack. In reality, it is a skill, like riding a bicycle or typing. A child learning to ride falls repeatedly, keeps trying and eventually rides without thinking. Communication works the same way: with deliberate practice, anyone can improve markedly.

Good communication matters at every level of business. Leaders communicate vision and expectations. Engineers explain technical issues to customers. Salespeople listen and explain. Managers give feedback and resolve conflict. People who communicate well build relationships, share ideas, delegate responsibility and manage teams more effectively.

This article sets out a practical 30-day plan with exercises across four channels: verbal communication, written communication, non-verbal communication and intrapersonal communication, meaning the way you talk to yourself. It also explains how to read other people's body language carefully, without falling for popular myths.

## Four channels of communication

- **Verbal**: spoken words in conversations, meetings, presentations and calls.
- **Written**: emails, messages, reports, proposals and documentation.
- **Non-verbal**: body language, facial expressions, gestures, posture, eye contact and tone.
- **Visual**: appearance, dress and the visual materials you use.

Communication also happens at two levels:

- **Interpersonal communication**: between two or more people, such as a manager and an employee, a supplier and a customer, or colleagues.
- **Intrapersonal communication**: your conversation with yourself, including self-talk, planning, rehearsing, reflecting and evaluating.

Communication scholar Leonard Shedletsky has described intrapersonal communication using the same elements as communication between people: a source, a receiver, a message, a channel, feedback, an environment, a context and interference. When you rehearse a presentation in your head, criticise yourself after a meeting or calm yourself before a difficult call, you are both sender and receiver.

## Week 1: Speaking clearly

### Exercise 1: Talk to the mirror (daily, 5 minutes)

Stand in front of a mirror and talk to yourself about anything, while maintaining eye contact with your reflection. It does not need to make sense. The goal is to become comfortable speaking while looking someone in the eye. Many people find their confidence improves noticeably within ten days.

### Exercise 2: Read aloud (daily, 5 minutes)

Read a newspaper or magazine article aloud, slowly and clearly. Pay attention to pronunciation and pace. Some voice coaches suggest reading with a pencil held lightly between the teeth for a minute, then reading normally, to make your articulation more deliberate.

### Exercise 3: Tongue twisters (daily, 2 minutes)

Practise tongue twisters such as "She sells seashells on the seashore" or "Red lorry, yellow lorry". They improve clarity and control of speech.

### Exercise 4: Reduce filler words

Record yourself explaining something for two minutes, such as your job, a project or a product. Count the filler words: "um", "ah", "like", "you know", "so". Fillers distract listeners and weaken your message.

To reduce them:

- **Pause instead.** A short silence sounds more confident than a filler.
- **Breathe** before starting each point.
- **Keep sentences short.**
- **Rehearse** important conversations and presentations.

Record yourself again at the end of the week and compare.

## Week 2: Listening and responding

### Exercise 5: Listen, then summarise

In at least one conversation a day, listen without interrupting, then summarise what the other person said before responding: "So what you're saying is..." This builds active listening, which differs from hearing. Hearing happens automatically. Listening requires full attention. The article on [listening, the most under-trained skill](/reados/collections/communication-and-professional-presence/listening-the-most-under-trained-skill/) explains why this matters so much.

### Exercise 6: Research before speaking

Before a meeting with a new customer, a presentation or a difficult conversation, spend time researching the topic and the people involved. Knowledge builds confidence. When you understand a subject well, you speak about it more clearly and handle questions better.

### Exercise 7: Appreciate and reciprocate

Each day, sincerely compliment or thank someone for something specific: a useful idea, good work, help received. "Thanks for catching that error in the drawing. It saved us a lot of rework." Genuine appreciation builds relationships and makes conversations warmer.

### Exercise 8: Practise everyday social phrases

If you are shy, practise everyday phrases aloud until they feel natural: "Hello, how are you today?", "Thanks for your help", "Can I help you with anything?", "What do you do?" Rehearsed openings make it easier to start conversations at events and meetings.

## Week 3: Writing simply

### Exercise 9: Write a paragraph a day

Write one paragraph daily on any topic: your industry, a news story, a problem you solved, a lesson you learned. Daily writing builds fluency and command of language.

### Exercise 10: Edit for simplicity

Take something you wrote recently, such as an email or report, and cut it by a third without losing meaning. Remove repeated words, unnecessary qualifiers and long sentences. Clear, brief writing is more effective than long, complex writing.

### Exercise 11: Check tone

Before sending an important email, reread it as the recipient. Does it sound abrupt, defensive or unclear? Would you be comfortable if it were forwarded to others? Avoid jokes and sarcasm in professional writing, because tone is easily misread.

### Exercise 12: Keep a collection of good writing

Save examples of writing you find effective: emails, proposals, articles, phrases and quotes. Refer to them when writing. Over time, your collection becomes a valuable resource for presentations, articles and emails.

### Exercise 13: Review before sending

Make it a habit to review important messages before sending them, checking grammar, punctuation, names, numbers and attachments. Use "please" and "thank you". A short review prevents embarrassing errors and gives you a chance to improve how you put your point.

## Week 4: Body language and self-talk

### Exercise 14: Posture practice

Practise sitting and standing in front of a mirror. Sit upright with your feet on the floor and your hands relaxed on your lap or the table. Stand with your weight balanced and hands at your sides, rather than in your pockets. Walk with your head up, looking ahead.

### Exercise 15: Notice your habits

Talk to yourself in a mirror or on video and notice your habits: touching your face, fidgeting, crossing your arms, looking away, slouching. Pick one habit to reduce this week.

### Exercise 16: Notice how emotions show physically

Several times a day, notice what you are feeling and how it shows in your body: tense shoulders when stressed, clenched hands when frustrated, slumped posture when tired. Awareness of your own signals helps you manage the impression you give, especially in presentations and difficult conversations.

### Exercise 17: Positive social signals

Practise the basics: a genuine smile, nodding to show you are listening and a firm, friendly handshake where appropriate. These simple signals communicate warmth and attention.

### Exercise 18: Manage self-talk

Self-talk shapes confidence and performance. In stressful moments, deliberately calm yourself: "Slow down. Breathe. You've prepared for this." When negative thoughts arise, such as "I'll make a fool of myself", replace them with balanced alternatives: "I know this topic well, and people want to hear what I have to say."

Use intrapersonal communication productively:

- **Rehearse** important conversations and presentations, reviewing and improving them in your head or aloud.
- **Reflect** after interactions: what went well, and what would you do differently?
- **Pause before reacting** to provocations, giving yourself time to choose a response.
- **Build calm** through practices such as meditation, exercise or quiet reflection.

## Reading body language carefully

Understanding others' body language helps you notice whether people are engaged, confused, uncomfortable or disagreeing. But popular claims about body language are often overstated. Use these principles.

### Look for clusters, not single signals

A single gesture means little. Crossed arms may signal defensiveness, or simply that the person is cold or comfortable. Several signals together, such as crossed arms, leaning back, little eye contact and short answers, are more meaningful.

### Know the person's baseline

People differ. Some naturally avoid eye contact, fidget or speak quietly. Notice changes from someone's normal behaviour rather than judging against a general rule.

### Consider context and culture

Eye contact, personal space, touch, gestures and facial expressions vary between cultures and individuals. Direct eye contact is expected in many Australian business settings but may be considered disrespectful in some cultures. Neurodivergent people may communicate non-verbally in different ways.

### Treat signals as questions, not conclusions

If someone seems disengaged or uncomfortable, check rather than assume: "I sense this might not be quite what you were expecting. What are your thoughts?"

### Common signals and their usual meanings

| Signal | Often suggests | But could also mean |
|---|---|---|
| Leaning forward, nodding | Interest, engagement | Difficulty hearing |
| Leaning back, crossed arms | Reservation, defensiveness | Comfort, feeling cold |
| Frequent face touching | Nervousness, discomfort | Habit, an itch |
| Looking at the phone or watch | Distraction, time pressure | An expected call |
| Relaxed hands, open posture | Confidence, openness | |
| Clenched fists, tense jaw | Anger or stress | Concentration |

## Visual communication

Your appearance communicates before you speak. Dress appropriately for the setting: formal for a corporate interview or board presentation, practical and clean for a site visit, neat casual for many office settings. Grooming matters: tidy hair, clean nails and clean clothes. The visual materials you use, such as slides, documents and your website, also communicate, so keep them clear, consistent and professional.

## Common mistakes when practising

- **Practising only in your head.** Speaking skills improve through speaking aloud, not just thinking about it.
- **Trying to fix everything at once.** Focus on one or two habits each week.
- **Avoiding recordings.** Hearing yourself is uncomfortable but is the fastest way to notice habits.
- **Copying someone else's style.** Learn techniques from good communicators, but keep your own voice.
- **Stopping after the 30 days.** The plan builds habits, and habits need maintaining.

## Practising as a team

These exercises also work for teams. A manager might ask each team member to give a two-minute update at the weekly meeting without filler words, rotate who summarises decisions at the end, or review one another's customer emails for clarity. Practising together normalises feedback, builds shared standards and makes improvement visible. Keep feedback specific and kind: "Your summary at the start made the email easy to follow" teaches more than "Good email".

## Tracking progress

At the end of 30 days, compare:

- Your first and last recordings: filler words, pace and clarity.
- The length and clarity of your emails.
- How comfortable you feel starting conversations and speaking in meetings.
- Feedback from a trusted colleague who has observed you.

Choose the exercises that helped most and keep them as ongoing habits.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much time does the plan take each day?** Around 15 to 20 minutes for the exercises, plus applying the habits in conversations and emails you would have anyway. Most of the practice happens inside normal work.

**I'm an introvert. Will this change my personality?** No. Introverts are often excellent communicators, because they listen well and think before speaking. The plan helps you express yourself clearly when you choose to, not become someone else.

**What if English is my second language?** The same exercises work, and reading aloud and daily writing are especially valuable. The article on [business English for non-native speakers](/reados/collections/communication-and-professional-presence/business-english-for-non-native-speakers/) adds useful phrases for common workplace situations.

## A worked example

A maintenance planner at a manufacturing plant is technically excellent but struggles in management meetings: she speaks quickly, uses many filler words and her emails are long and hard to follow. Her manager suggests the 30-day plan.

In week one, she records herself explaining the weekly maintenance schedule and counts 23 filler words in two minutes. By the end of the week, after daily practice, she is down to eight. In week two, she starts summarising what others say before responding, which reduces misunderstandings with production supervisors. In week three, she rewrites her weekly maintenance email, cutting it from 600 words to 250 with a clear summary at the top. Supervisors start reading it. In week four, she practises calming self-talk before presenting to senior management.

After a month, her manager notices that she presents with more confidence, and senior managers begin asking her to brief them directly.

## Summary

Communication is a skill that improves with deliberate practice. Over 30 days, practise speaking clearly through mirror work, reading aloud, tongue twisters and reducing fillers. Build listening by summarising before responding, research before important conversations and express appreciation. Write a paragraph daily, edit for simplicity, check tone and review before sending. Practise posture and positive social signals, notice your habits and manage self-talk constructively. Read others' body language carefully, through clusters, baselines and context, and treat signals as questions rather than conclusions.
