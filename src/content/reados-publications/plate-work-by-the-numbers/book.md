## About this book

Plate is sheet that has become thick enough to matter. In thin sheet, a pattern developed on the inside or the outside is out by a fraction of a millimetre, and nobody notices. In 6 mm plate rolled into a 2.4 m shell, the same choice is worth 19 mm of girth, the plate springs back to three times the radius it was rolled to, a hole cut round on the flat is too narrow once rolled, and a cone's mean line is not where a sheet worker would put it. Plate work is pattern development with the thickness left in.

This book works through those numbers in the order a plated structure is made: why thickness changes the pattern, rolled shells and their seams, rolling and springback, conical shells, flat-sided hoppers, openings, rings and flanges, marking out and nesting, fit-up and roundness, and finally the plate list, mass and lifting. Each chapter calculates with one running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

It is for platers, boilermakers and their apprentices, fabrication drafters, and engineers who specify plate structures or check a fabricator's drawings and plate lists. It assumes school algebra and trigonometry and some familiarity with pattern development; the Library’s [Sheet Metal Parts by the Numbers](/reados/publications/sheet-metal-parts-by-the-numbers/) covers the folded-part side of the subject, and [Sheet Metal Patterns by the Numbers](/reados/publications/sheet-metal-patterns-by-the-numbers/) develops the same patterns for thin sheet.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional job. This is an illustration. A stockfeed business has ordered a storage bin from a plate fabrication shop. The bin is a vertical cylinder of 2,400 mm inside diameter and 4,800 mm high, made from two 2,400 mm courses of 6 mm mild steel plate, standing on a conical hopper of 8 mm plate whose walls slope at 60° to the horizontal and which discharges through a Ø400 outlet. A 75 × 75 × 6 angle ring stiffens the top of the shell, a flat 5 mm plate forms the roof, a Ø500 manway is set into the lower course, and a separate square loading hopper, 1,200 mm square at the top and 300 mm square at the bottom, feeds the roof inlet. The shop buys plate in 2,400 × 6,000 mm sheets and rolls on a three-roll machine.

The business, the shop, the dimensions, the yield strengths, the prices and the mistakes are invented. None describes BIZOMAT work or a real business.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 sets out the conventions, especially which line of the thickness each pattern is developed on. Chapters 2 to 4 cover the main curved plates, and Chapters 5 to 7 the parts that attach to them. Chapters 8 to 10 cover the shop work that turns patterns into a finished structure. Values are given to 0.1 mm so that the arithmetic can be followed; plate is usually marked to the nearest millimetre and fitted within the tolerances the drawing sets.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The geometry in this book is exact, and every calculation was checked by running it. The springback estimate in Chapter 3 is a published model for an idealised material, which describes the trend well and the exact value only approximately; plate is rolled to a template, not to a calculation.

Bins, hoppers and silos carry bulk loads that can be large, uneven and dynamic, and their failure can kill. Their structural design is governed by AS 3774 for loads, AS 4100 for steel structures and AS/NZS 1554.1 for welding, among others, and must be done and approved by a competent engineer. Pressure vessels are outside this book and are governed by AS 1210. Lifting, rolling and handling heavy plate are high-risk work under Australian work health and safety law. This book teaches layout and fabrication geometry. It is not a design for any structure, and its numbers must not be used for one.
