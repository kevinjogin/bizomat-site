Most people decisions in this book have no deadline set by law. A request for flexible working arrangements does. Under the Fair Work Act 2009, an employer must give an eligible employee who asks in writing a written response within 21 days, and the Act lists the only conditions under which the answer may be no. Leave and attendance carry rules too: some in the Act's National Employment Standards, more in your agency's enterprise agreement.

This is an illustration. Mei Wong, an APS 5 Client Services Officer, comes back from parental leave on Monday 4 January 2027. That morning she gives Hannah a written request to work part-time, three days a week, from Monday 1 February. The timing is hard. Daniel's former position stays empty until Tessa Morgan starts on Monday 15 February (Chapter 3), and January is when the section plans its leave for the year.

## Who can ask, and what a request must say

Section 65 of the [Fair Work Act 2009](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2009A00028/latest/text) (Compilation No. 73) lets an employee request a change in working arrangements if a circumstance in section 65(1A) applies and the change relates to it. The circumstances are that the employee is pregnant; is the parent of, or responsible for the care of, a child "of school age or younger"; is a carer; has a disability; is 55 or older; is experiencing family and domestic violence; or cares for an immediate family or household member who is. A note gives examples of changes: hours, patterns and location of work. Section 65(1B) adds that a parent returning after leave for the birth or adoption of a child "may request to work part‑time to assist the employee to care for the child."

An employee other than a casual must also have "at least 12 months of continuous service with the employer immediately before making the request" (s 65(2)(a)). The request must be in writing and set out the change sought and the reasons for it (s 65(3)). In the APS the employer's powers sit with the agency head (Public Service Act s 20(1)).

This is an illustration. Mei's request meets each test. Her son is one, so section 65(1A)(a) applies, and she has worked at NRIO for more than five years. (Section 22(4) of the Act counts service for this part in its own way; when service is close to 12 months, ask the People Branch.) Her email names the change (Tuesday to Thursday), the start date and the reason: her son's childcare place on those days. Hannah had checked before 4 January who answers. NRIO's procedure names the EL1 manager, and requires People Branch advice and the Director's agreement before any refusal is sent.

## Respond in writing within 21 days

Section 65A(1) is a requirement, not a guideline: "the employer must give the employee a written response to the request within 21 days." The Act says days, not working days. A request received on Monday 4 January must be answered by Monday 25 January 2027.

The response must do one of three things (s 65A(2)): grant the request; set out a different change that both people have agreed after discussion; or refuse and include what section 65A(6) requires. Calling a partial refusal an agreement does not make it one; the employee has to agree.

The Act does not require a meeting before a grant, but you should hold one: you learn what the request is for and what else might work. If a refusal is possible, the meeting is not optional, as the next section shows.

This is an illustration. Hannah acknowledged Mei's email the same morning, entered the date received and the deadline in the section's request register, and booked a meeting for Tuesday 12 January. She planned to reply by Friday 15 January, ten days early, so that Mei could confirm her childcare place and the team leaders could change the roster.

## Refuse only on reasonable business grounds

Section 65A(3) says the employer may refuse "only if" four conditions are met. The employer has discussed the request with the employee and "genuinely tried to reach an agreement" about changes that would accommodate the employee's circumstances. The two have not reached such an agreement. The employer has "had regard to the consequences of the refusal for the employee". And "the refusal is on reasonable business grounds".

Section 65A(5) gives examples of reasonable business grounds without limiting them: the arrangement would be too costly; there is no capacity to change other employees' arrangements to accommodate it; changing others' arrangements, or recruiting, would be impractical; or it would be likely to cause a significant loss in efficiency or productivity, or a significant negative impact on customer service. A note says the employer's circumstances, including its size, are relevant.

A refusal must also explain itself. Under section 65A(6) the response must give the reasons, set out the particular business grounds, explain how they apply to this request, set out other changes the employer would be willing to make or state that there are none, and "set out the effect of sections 65B and 65C". "Operational requirements" is not a ground explained. It is a label.

## Disputes start at the workplace

If the employer refuses, or 21 days pass with no written response, section 65B applies to a dispute about the request (s 65B(1)). The parties "must attempt to resolve the dispute at the workplace level" first (s 65B(2)). Then either may refer it to the Fair Work Commission (FWC), which must first try means other than arbitration unless there are exceptional circumstances (s 65B(4)(a)). Your agreement's dispute clause also applies: a note to section 65B says agreements must include one for disputes about the National Employment Standards.

If the FWC arbitrates, it may order further steps or, where there is no reasonable prospect of resolving the dispute otherwise, "an order that the employer grant the request" (s 65C(1)(e) and (f), (3)). It must take into account fairness between the two (s 65C(2)). The workplace discussion the Act requires after a refusal is the conversation that should have happened before it.

## What your enterprise agreement may add

The National Employment Standards are minimum standards that cannot be displaced (s 61(1)). They cover, among other things, flexible working requests, parental leave, annual leave, personal/carer's leave and public holidays (s 61(2)). Your agency's enterprise agreement builds on them, and this book gives no figures from any agreement.

Read its clauses on flexible work, part-time work, working from home and leave before you reply. An agreement may add rights or steps; ask the People Branch which apply. Working from home is a change in the location of work, so a request from an employee in a section 65 circumstance is a section 65 request, whatever the policy calls it. Other requests follow the agreement and your agency's policy.

## Test the roster with the numbers

A business ground has to be shown, not assumed. Hannah measured the effect of Mei's request with the [Team Workload](/tools/workload/) tool. It divides planned work hours by people times available hours per person, and reports AVAILABLE up to 85 per cent utilisation, FULL up to 100 per cent and OVERLOADED above that, with the buffer in hours.

This is an illustration. The work is the section's 2026–27 handling time (Chapter 2). The people are the six officer positions; the team leaders' time goes to supervision, rosters, checks and escalations. Each full-time officer gives NRIO's 1,275 productive hours a year, already net of leave, so leave is not taken out twice. The tool takes whole people and one figure for hours, so Hannah entered Mei at 0.6 through the average: 5.6 FTE × 1,275 = 7,140 hours, entered as 6 people at 1,190 hours.

Even at full strength the section is FULL, at 93.1 per cent, before service-level cover and the Monday morning peak (Chapter 2). Mei's request removes 0.4 × 1,275 = 510 hours a year and leaves a buffer of 20 hours: a real effect on customer service. Two other periods matter as much. In January, with Mei full-time and one position empty, five officers give 6,375 hours and utilisation is 7,120 ÷ 6,375 = 111.7 per cent. From 1 to 12 February, with Mei at 0.6 and the position still empty, 4.6 FTE give 5,865 hours, entered as 5 people at 1,173 hours, and utilisation is 121.4 per cent. Any other absence makes these worse.

This is an illustration. Hannah read the numbers against section 65A(5). Refusing would not fix January, overloaded with Mei full-time. Cost is a weak ground: a part-time officer is paid for part-time hours, so the money for the other 0.4 stays in the budget, and the People Branch confirmed that filling it through its own merit process was practical. At the meeting Hannah asked whether Monday could replace Thursday, for the Monday peak. Mei's childcare place is Tuesday to Thursday only and starts on 1 February. Those consequences weighed heavily, and Hannah granted the request as made.

Capacity became a separate resourcing question. A brief to the Director sought approval to fill the 0.4 and named the workload risk (Chapter 8). For the two weeks from 1 February, Marcus and Leila would take more calls and a knowledge-base review would move to March. The Director's answer is not yet known.

## Plan leave and handle attendance fairly

Paid annual leave is taken for a period agreed between employee and employer (s 88(1)), and "The employer must not unreasonably refuse to agree to a request by the employee to take paid annual leave" (s 88(2)). Your agreement adds the detail. Refusals are rarer, and easier to justify, when leave is planned as a team.

This is an illustration. In January Hannah asked everyone for leave plans to the end of June. The 1,275 productive hours already allow for leave, so the question is whether too much falls in the same weeks. The team agreed a rule: clashes in school holiday weeks are settled by agreement first, then in turn, with whoever missed out last time going first. Any refusal comes with the reason and other dates that would work.

Unplanned absence needs different care. Personal/carer's leave covers an employee unfit for work through illness or injury, and care or support for an immediate family or household member who is ill, injured or facing an unexpected emergency (s 97). Notice must be given "as soon as practicable (which may be a time after the leave has started)" (s 107(2)). If the employer requires it, the employee must give "evidence that would satisfy a reasonable person" (s 107(3)); an agreement may set the kind of evidence (s 107(5)). A note adds that the Privacy Act 1988 may regulate the personal information received.

This is an illustration. In March the leave records showed that one officer, whom this chapter does not name, had taken seven single days of unplanned personal/carer's leave in the 13 weeks from Monday 7 December 2026 to Friday 5 March 2027, five of them Mondays. Hannah did not treat the pattern as a finding. Every absence had been notified. She met the officer privately, said what the record showed and asked what would help. The officer had been taking a parent to weekly treatment. Hannah did not ask for medical details. She mentioned NRIO's employee assistance service, and explained that a carer may also request a change in working arrangements (s 65(1A)(b)), handled exactly as Mei's was.

If a pattern continues unexplained, or health may be affecting work, go to the People Branch. Steps such as a medical examination under the Regulations (s 11) are the agency head's decision, not the manager's.

## Keep decisions consistent

The APS Employment Principles describe an APS that "provides flexible, safe and rewarding workplaces" that are "free from discrimination, patronage and favouritism" (Public Service Act s 10A(1)(e) and (f)). Three habits make them visible in a team.

Keep a register of every request: date received, circumstance, change sought, deadline, discussion, outcome and reasons. It holds personal information, so keep it in the records system with access limited.

Assess each request on its merits, against the roster as it then stands. An earlier grant can change the numbers, and a lack of capacity to change other employees' arrangements is a listed ground (s 65A(5)(b)). It still has to be shown, and arriving second does not make a request less deserving.

Respect an arrangement once it is made. Mei is not on call on Mondays and Fridays. The Act gives employees a right to refuse "to monitor, read or respond to contact … outside of the employee's working hours unless the refusal is unreasonable" (s 333M(1)). This is an illustration. From February the weekly team meeting moved from Monday to Wednesday, a day everyone works. Development and acting opportunities, the subject of Chapter 10, should be open to part-time staff on the same terms as everyone else.

## When it goes wrong: the two-line refusal

This is an illustration. Consider the reply Hannah could have sent had January swallowed her. On Friday 22 January, day 18, with no meeting held, she emails Mei: "Unfortunately we can't support a three-day arrangement in the team at this time due to operational requirements. Happy to look at it again later in the year."

The email arrives within 21 days, and that is all it gets right. With no discussion and no attempt to agree, section 65A(3) does not allow a refusal at all. Nothing shows regard for Mei, whose childcare place starts in ten days. "Operational requirements" explains nothing. There is no other offer and no word on sections 65B and 65C. And NRIO's procedure required People Branch advice and the Director's agreement first.

Mei must give up the childcare place, use leave she was saving, or dispute the decision. A dispute means workplace discussions with trust already damaged, then possibly the FWC and an order. Hours go to the dispute, and the team learns how requests are treated. The refusal does not even fix the problem: with Mei full-time, January runs at 111.7 per cent anyway, because the cause is the empty position.

The controls are the ones in this chapter: a register with the deadline in it, a meeting before any decision, numbers behind any business ground, and no refusal without People Branch advice and the Director's agreement.

### Worked answer

The dates first. Mei's request arrived on Monday 4 January 2027. The last day is 4 + 21 = 25 January, Monday 25 January 2027; both are Mondays because 21 days is exactly three weeks. Hannah's response is dated Friday 15 January, 11 days after the request and 10 days before the limit (25 − 15 = 10).

The response is a grant under section 65A(2)(a). It need not contain grounds, alternatives or the effect of sections 65B and 65C, which section 65A(6) requires only for a refusal. It should be exact about what was agreed.

> **Friday 15 January 2027**
>
> Dear Mei,
>
> Thank you for your written request for flexible working arrangements of Monday 4 January 2027, and for meeting me on Tuesday 12 January. You asked to work part-time, three days a week, on Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays, from Monday 1 February 2027, to care for your son.
>
> I grant your request. From Monday 1 February 2027 you will work 0.6 of full-time hours, on Tuesdays, Wednesdays and Thursdays. The People Branch will confirm in writing how your hours, pay and leave are worked out under our enterprise agreement.
>
> As we agreed, Marcus will send you a short handover note each Tuesday morning covering Friday and Monday, and we will meet in the week of Monday 3 May 2027 to see how the arrangement is working. If your circumstances change, you are welcome to make a new request at any time.
>
> Hannah Lee
> Client Services Manager

A copy goes on Mei's file and the register entry is closed. The brief about the 0.4 is a separate document, because Mei's arrangement does not depend on its answer.

A refusal would have needed two sets of things. Before refusing, under section 65A(3): a discussion with Mei (the 12 January meeting); a genuine attempt to agree other changes, such as other days, a later start or a trial; no agreement reached; regard to the consequences for Mei, including her childcare place; and reasonable business grounds. In the response, under section 65A(6): the reasons; the particular grounds and how they apply, with the numbers (the buffer falling from 530 hours to 20, if no way to fill 510 hours could be found); any other changes NRIO would make, or a statement that there are none; and the effect of sections 65B and 65C in plain words: Mei may raise a dispute, it would first be discussed at the workplace, and it could then go to the FWC, which may order that the request be granted. On these facts the grounds would be hard to sustain, which is why Hannah granted the request.

Mei's new arrangement changes the roster; Chapter 10 turns to developing the people on it and making sure no role depends on one person.
