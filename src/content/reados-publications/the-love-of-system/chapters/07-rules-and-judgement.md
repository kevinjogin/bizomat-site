Chapter 6 ended with a warning against assuming that a procedure can capture everything. That raises the opposite question: what should a procedure capture? Every business needs some rules that are followed exactly, and every business has work that no rule can specify in advance. Getting the line between them wrong in either direction is expensive.

Smith gave a remarkably useful way of drawing that line. This chapter sets out his distinction between precise and loose rules, applies it to the job shop's standard work, and follows two failures: one where the shop wrote rules for everything, and one where it left to judgement a decision that needed a rule.

## Smith on general rules

Smith did not think people should decide every action afresh. In *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* he argues that we form general rules from experience: having seen many actions of a kind approved or disapproved, we arrive at a rule about that kind of action. We need such rules, he says, because in the moment our own interests and feelings distort our judgement. When we are angry, hurried or tempted, we see only brief and unreliable glimpses of how an impartial observer would view what we are doing. A rule formed in calmer times protects us from that distortion. Robert Shaver, in the *Cambridge Companion*, draws out how much weight Smith puts on this: a regard for general rules is, for Smith, what a sense of duty largely consists of, and the conduct of people without it cannot be depended on.

But Smith also insisted that rules differ in how precise they can be. The rules of justice, he argued, can be stated with great accuracy, like the rules of grammar: they say exactly what is required and admit few exceptions. The rules of the other virtues, such as prudence, generosity or gratitude, are more like the rules a critic gives for elegant writing: loose, general guidance that helps a skilled person but cannot tell them exactly what to do in each case. David Lieberman and Marcelo Dascal, in their chapters of the Companion, both note how firmly Smith held to this. He criticised the moralists of his day, the casuists, for trying to write exact rules for every circumstance of life, and judged their books as useless as they were tiresome. The attempt to make loose rules precise, he thought, produced neither good rules nor good judgement.

## Grammar and style in a job shop

The distinction maps neatly onto a workshop. Some of its rules are like grammar: there is a right answer, it can be stated in advance, and departures from it are errors. Others are like advice on style: they help a skilled person do well, but the right action depends on the case.

- Safety | Isolation before reaching into a machine; guards and interlocks; lifting limits | How to arrange a set-up for good access
- Quality | First-off approval before a batch runs; inspection frequency and acceptance criteria | How to adjust offsets to centre a feature in tolerance
- Traceability | Material certificates matched to the job; heat numbers recorded | Which supplier to use for an urgent order
- Process | Tool life limits on critical features; calibration due dates | Cutting data for a new material; the order of operations on a new part
- *Commercial | Approval limits for quotes and discounts | Pricing a complex job; when to decline an order
```

The left column has four features in common. The consequence of getting it wrong is serious. The right action can be specified in advance. People under pressure are tempted to depart from it in ways that favour themselves in the moment. And a departure can be seen and checked. Those are exactly the conditions Smith's argument about general rules addresses: rules protect people from their own hurried judgement where hurried judgement is most dangerous and least reliable.

The right column has the opposite features. The right action depends on details that cannot be listed in advance: the particular casting, the particular customer, the state of the machine that day. A precise rule there would either be wrong in many cases or so long that nobody could follow it. What helps is guidance: a range of cutting data with notes on when to go high or low, worked examples of past jobs, and a review by an experienced person.

## The failure case: rules for everything

After the success of the standard work pilot in Chapter 6, the owner sets out to make the sheets complete. Every contingency the operators mention is added: what to do if the casting is oversize, if the bar is from a different supplier, if the tool chips, if the coolant is low, if the customer's drawing revision has changed. Each addition is sensible. After two months the lathe's main sheet has grown from two pages to fourteen, with branches and cross-references.

Usage, recorded by the operators themselves, falls sharply. Operators read the first two pages, which hold the set-up and the checks, and ignore the rest. Two of the branches contradict each other. A new operator, told to follow the sheet, spends most of a morning reading it and still asks the lathe operator what to do.

This is Smith's casuistry in a workshop. The owner tried to turn guidance like style into rules like grammar, writing exact rules for circumstances that vary too much to be ruled exactly. The result was neither a reliable rule nor useful guidance.

### How it is detected

The signs are easy to see once looked for: documents growing longer with each revision, branches and exceptions multiplying, experienced people no longer reading them, and new people still needing to ask. A simple test is to ask an experienced operator which parts of a document they actually use. The parts they skip are usually guidance dressed as rules.

### How it is corrected

The sheet is split. **Two pages of rules**, the grammar, stay as the standard work: set-up sequence, workholding, critical tools and offsets, first-off and in-process checks, and safety steps. These are followed exactly and checked. **A separate guidance sheet**, the style, holds the cutting data ranges, notes on materials and suppliers, and three worked examples of awkward jobs. It is read when needed, discussed at the weekly meeting and updated by the operators. The fourteen pages become two of rules and three of guidance, and both are used.

## Training judgement

Moving a practice out of the rule book does not mean leaving it to chance. Judgement can be trained, and in a small shop the methods are simple. Worked examples on the guidance sheet show how experienced people handled real cases. A short review after an awkward job, asking what was decided and what happened, builds a shared sense of what good judgement looks like. Pairing a newer operator with an experienced one on unfamiliar work passes on the parts of judgement that are hard to write down. And the weekly meeting discusses decisions, not just results, so that a good call made under uncertainty is recognised even when luck went the other way.

None of these produces certainty. They produce people whose judgement is better informed and more consistent, which is what Smith thought general guidance was for: helping a skilled person do well in cases no rule could foresee.

## The second failure case: judgement where a rule was needed

The opposite error happens in the same quarter. First-off approval at the shop had always been a matter of judgement: an operator who was confident in a set-up would sometimes run a few more parts while the first was being measured, to save time. Usually it did no harm.

On a Friday afternoon, with a delivery due on Monday and the measuring machine busy, the lathe operator runs on after the first part of a batch of **120** shafts, confident in a set-up he has done many times. The first part, when measured, has the wrong chamfer angle: the program had been edited for another customer's version of the part and not reverted. By then the whole batch has been run. The batch is scrapped at a material and machine cost of about **A$3,400** excluding GST, and the delivery is late.

The operator is skilled and conscientious. That is precisely the point Smith made: under pressure, even good judgement gives way to the interests of the moment, and the moment's interest, getting the batch finished before the weekend, pointed the wrong way. First-off approval has all four features of a rule like grammar: the consequence of error is serious, the right action can be stated in advance, there is a strong temptation to skip it, and compliance can be checked.

### How it is detected

The detection is a review of what went wrong, carried out without blame. The question is not "who failed?" but "was this decision left to judgement in a situation where judgement is known to fail?" The answer is yes: first-off approval was being decided case by case at exactly the moments when pressure was highest.

### How it is corrected

The shop makes first-off approval a precise rule: **no batch runs beyond its first three parts until the first part has been measured and signed off.** The three parts allow the operator to keep the machine warm and the process stable while the first is measured. The rule is written on the sheet, checked at the morning meeting, and supported by giving lathe first-offs priority at the measuring machine, so that following the rule does not cost the constraint its time. The cost of following the rule is small; the cost of the judgement it replaced was A$3,400 and a late delivery.

## Deciding which is which

The two failures suggest a simple way to sort any practice. Ask four questions:

1. **How serious is the consequence of getting it wrong?**
2. **Can the right action be stated in advance for nearly every case?**
3. **Will people under pressure be tempted to depart from it?**
4. **Can compliance be seen and checked?**

caption: This is an illustration. Where the consequence is serious and the right action can be specified, write a precise rule and check it. Where the action depends on the case, train judgement and review it. Pressure to deviate pushes a practice towards a rule.
- Minor consequence, action varies | Leave to judgement; share examples
- Minor consequence, action specifiable | A simple standard; light checking
- Serious consequence, action varies | Trained judgement with review by an experienced person
- Serious consequence, action specifiable | A precise rule, followed exactly and checked
```

Practices in the top right quadrant, serious and specifiable, are rules like grammar. Practices in the left column are guidance like style, with the bottom left needing more training and review the more serious they are. Where people are under strong pressure to deviate, a practice should move towards a rule even if the right action is not perfectly specifiable, because, as Smith argued, that is when unaided judgement is least reliable.

## Rules and the love of system

The love of system pulls towards rules. A complete set of precise procedures is a beautiful thing, orderly and consistent, and it appeals to the same taste as the robot cell and the live dashboards. That is why the owner's first response to success was to write more rules. The discipline this chapter recommends is to write precise rules only where the four questions call for them, and to resist the temptation to extend them into territory where they become casuistry.

Judgement has its own risks, and the second failure showed them. But judgement can be improved, by training, by worked examples, by review of decisions and by the kind of guidance sheet the shop now keeps. Dascal's point in the Companion is that both kinds of rule are needed and neither can be reduced to the other. A business that tries to replace judgement with rules ends up with neither; one that leaves everything to judgement leaves its people exposed at the moments when they most need a rule.

## Exercise: sort six practices

Sort each of the following into precise rule or guided judgement, using the four questions, and give one sentence of reasoning for each.

1. Locking out the machine before clearing a chip jam inside the guard.
2. Choosing the depth of cut for roughing an unfamiliar alloy.
3. Recording the material heat number against each job.
4. Deciding whether to accept a rush order that would delay another customer.
5. Replacing the finishing insert on a critical bearing seat after a set number of parts.
6. Deciding the order of operations for a new part with a difficult feature.

**Worked answer.**

1. **Precise rule.** The consequence is injury, the right action is fully specifiable, and the temptation to skip it when a jam is small is strong.
2. **Guided judgement.** The right depth depends on the machine, the tool, the casting and the alloy; give a range and examples, and review the first part.
3. **Precise rule.** Traceability failures can be serious and costly, the action is simple and specifiable, and compliance is easy to check.
4. **Guided judgement**, with an approval limit. The right answer depends on the customers, the margins and the schedule; a rule can say who decides and above what value, not what to decide.
5. **Precise rule.** The consequence of a worn insert on a critical feature is a non-conforming part, the life can be set from experience, and there is pressure to stretch it near the end of a batch.
6. **Guided judgement with review.** The right sequence depends on the part; worked examples and a review by an experienced machinist before the first part is cut are worth more than a rule that would be wrong for the next part.
