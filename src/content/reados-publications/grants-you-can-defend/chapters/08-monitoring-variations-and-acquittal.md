Once a grant agreement is signed, public money can spend many months in other people's hands: volunteer committees, council project teams and their builders. Monitoring tells the agency, early enough to act, whether the money is doing what it was approved for. Variations let an agreement follow real events without becoming a different grant. Acquittal ends the grant with evidence rather than an assumption.

This is an illustration. By September 2027 the 31 Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) agreements were executed and the first instalments paid. Four officers could not watch 31 projects equally closely, and they should not try. This chapter follows Sam Reid and Ben Walker as they set monitoring by risk, decide requests to change agreements and close grants. It also shows how an unauthorised "yes" in an email surfaced sixteen months later.

## Monitoring in proportion to risk

Paragraph 14.12 of the [Commonwealth Grants Rules and Principles 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) (CGRPs) says a well-drafted agreement is not enough on its own. Officials should support it with "ongoing communication, active grants management and performance monitoring requirements, which are proportional to the risks involved".

Proportion cuts both ways. Paragraph 9.5 says "There are no mandatory acquittal or reporting requirements for grantees in the CGRPs", and asks officials to set the volume, detail and frequency of reporting in proportion to the risks and outcomes sought. Heavy reporting wastes a volunteer group's time. Light reporting of a struggling project puts public money at risk.

This is an illustration. The RCFP agreement fixes what every grantee reports: the mid-point progress report, the final report and acquittal, and a return on use for the 12 months after the works finish, which outlasts the grant. It also lets NRIO ask for information it reasonably needs and visit the site on reasonable notice. Ben rates each grantee after execution, the band sets the monitoring, and the rating is reviewed at each report and whenever something changes.

## Rating a grantee on NRIO's 5 × 5 matrix

This is an illustration. NRIO's risk framework multiplies likelihood (1 to 5) by consequence (1 to 5). Scores of 1 to 6 are Low, 7 to 14 Medium and 15 to 25 High. For every grant, the Regional Programs Branch rates one event, "the project is not delivered as agreed", against its own descriptors.

Likelihood:

- 1, rare: an experienced grantee, with funding secured and approvals in hand;
- 2, unlikely: a capable grantee with a firm quote and only routine items open;
- 3, possible: a first project of this size, or a known uncertainty in time, cost or scope;
- 4, likely: a key condition not met, such as no builder, a missing approval or an unfunded gap;
- 5, almost certain: the project is already off track.

Consequence:

- 1, minimal: a short delay within the completion date;
- 2, minor: delay past the completion date, or up to A$25,000 of grant at risk;
- 3, moderate: A$25,000 to A$100,000 at risk, or part of the benefit lost;
- 4, major: more than A$100,000 at risk, or the facility's main benefit lost;
- 5, severe: misuse of grant money, or harm to people.

The [Risk Rating (5 × 5 matrix)](/tools/risk-score/) tool does the same multiplication and applies the same three bands. Use it to check the arithmetic, but rate against the branch's descriptors.

This is an illustration. Ben rated Brindlemere Football Netball Club Inc. on his return from leave, after its agreement was executed in July 2027.

```figure

title: Rating Brindlemere in July 2027
caption: This is an illustration. The score ranks grantees for attention. Keep both factors on the file, because they say what kind of attention is needed.
formula: Risk score = Likelihood × Consequence
- Likelihood | 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain), on the branch's grant descriptors
- Consequence | 1 (minimal) to 5 (severe), on the branch's grant descriptors
- Bands | Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14, High 15 to 25

step: Risk score = 3 × 4 = 12, Medium
```

The likelihood is 3, not 2, because at signing the committee had written in a completion date of 30 June 2029 (Chapter 7). That was not misconduct, but it was evidence the club's own plan might not fit 18 months.

## Progress reports and site visits

This is an illustration. The mid-point report decides the second 40 per cent, so it must show enough to decide: progress against each deliverable, spending to date by the schedule's budget lines, photos, changes to timing or risks and, for councils, the council's own spending.

Ben assesses each report against the schedule and, at a sample of site visits, checks use baselines against source records (Chapter 2). Sam accepts the milestone under NRIO's instructions, which give the Grants Program Manager that power but not the power to vary an agreement. Nadia Haddad raises the payment and the CFO area releases it. The file records what was checked, what was queried and why the milestone was met.

Three habits make reports useful:

- read spending line by line against the schedule, because a report that is on budget overall can hide spending the agreement does not cover;
- treat any statement that NRIO "agreed" to something as a question, and find the Branch Manager's approved variation on the file;
- query at the mid-point, while a gap can still be fixed.

A site visit tests what a report says. Plan what to see and which claims to check, take photos, and file a dated, signed note within five working days. Grantees often ask for changes on site: listen, take the request back, and leave the answer to the Branch Manager.

## Variations: who approves and what cannot change

A variation changes the agreement: its dates, deliverables, budget lines, conditions or amount. This is an illustration. At NRIO only the Branch Manager may approve a variation to an RCFP agreement, in writing, within the A$500,000 delegation. Nobody else's agreement, however senior or well meant, changes the grant.

The CGRPs give the principles as better practice. Paragraph 14.13 says value with relevant money should be considered in recommending and approving a variation, and adds: "Variations should not be used as an alternative to appropriately planning a new grant opportunity." Paragraph 14.14 says agreements should generally be varied only where necessary, and should "not significantly change the scope of the initial agreement". Under 14.16, a pattern of variations should prompt officials to consider longer agreements or a new process.

In a competitive round, some changes cannot be made by variation at all. This is an illustration. Each grant was won on merit, against 176 other eligible applications, for the project described. A change that would have altered its score, ranking or amount is not an administrative matter.

```figure
type: decision
title: Can this RCFP variation be approved, and by whom?
question: Does the change keep the project, the money and the round's rules as they were when the Minister approved the grant?
- Adds money | Not by variation | New spending is the Minister's decision, after written advice, and no grant may exceed A$500,000
- Changes what was assessed | Not by variation | If it would have altered the score, the ranking or the amount, it is a merit question
- Breaks a rule every applicant faced | Not by variation | Completion within 18 months of the agreement; a council's 25 per cent minimum contribution
- Unclear | Hold the answer until it is clear | Ben assesses, legal advises on the agreement, and the grantee hears only that the Branch Manager will decide
- *Within the project, the money and the rules | The Branch Manager approves in writing | Value with relevant money considered (CGRPs 14.13), with the reasons on the file
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's rules are the fictional agency's own. Only the delegate's written approval varies an agreement; an officer's email does not.
```

This is an illustration. Two Round 1 requests show the difference. On Thursday 2 March 2028, Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. asked to move its mid-point report from 15 March to Friday 12 May 2028, after asbestos removal ran three weeks over. Nothing else changed. On Ben's assessment and Sam's recommendation, the Branch Manager approved it in writing, and Ivy Chen moved the A$168,000 instalment from April to June 2028, still in 2027–28. A date change within one financial year is a minor variation that need not go on GrantConnect (footnote 51; Chapter 9).

In November 2027 the Shire of Eastbrook's tenders came in A$60,000 above its estimate, and the council asked NRIO to add that amount. NRIO could not. The grant was already A$500,000, the maximum in the guidelines. Extra money would also be new spending the Minister had not approved, and Round 1 had only A$10.0 million − A$9.98 million = A$20,000 unallocated. The council funded the gap, raising its contribution from A$300,000 to A$360,000 of an A$860,000 project: 41.9 per cent, well above the 25 per cent minimum.

## Underspends

This is an illustration. Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. finished its workshop fit-out for A$79,000, A$6,000 under its A$85,000 grant. The RCFP agreement pays eligible costs actually incurred, up to the grant amount. So the question is not whether the shed keeps the A$6,000, but whether the money is spent on the project or not paid at all.

If, before its completion date, the shed asks to spend it on more of the equipment the schedule describes, the Branch Manager can consider a variation. Otherwise NRIO pays only what was spent. The shed had received A$34,000 + A$34,000 = A$68,000, so the final instalment is A$79,000 − A$68,000 = A$11,000 instead of A$17,000.

Holding 20 per cent back until the final report is approved is what turns an underspend into a smaller payment rather than a debt. Once the underspend is confirmed, close that part of the commitment, so the forecast stops showing money that will never be paid (Chapter 7).

## Misuse and recovering money

Not every problem in an acquittal is misconduct. A grantee that buys something the agreement does not cover, in good faith, needs a decision under the agreement. False invoices, or money that has gone where the grantee cannot explain, need NRIO's fraud process. Tell the two apart before you act, and when in doubt, treat it as the second.

Section 10 of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) requires the accountable authority to have mechanisms for detecting fraud and corruption, including confidential reporting, and for investigating or otherwise responding to it. Paragraph 15.5 of the CGRPs adds: "Officials should be aware of the procedures to follow when fraud, corruption or misappropriation is suspected." Report the suspicion through NRIO's process, keep the evidence as it is, do not question the grantee or alert anyone involved, and release no further payment without advice.

The CGRPs do not deal with recovering grant money; the duty comes from the PGPA Rule. Section 11 says the accountable authority of a non-corporate Commonwealth entity "must pursue recovery of each debt for which the accountable authority is responsible". There are three exceptions: the accountable authority considers recovery uneconomical, it is satisfied the debt is not legally recoverable, or the debt has been written off as authorised by an Act. The duty applies where the agreement makes an amount repayable as a debt to the Commonwealth, so check its terms with the legal area. Under NRIO's practice, not pursuing a debt is a recorded decision with reasons, made by the official its instructions name.

Recovery is not set-off. Section 64 of the PGPA Act is a separate power, for the Finance Minister to authorise setting off amounts owed to and by the Commonwealth. Ask the CFO area before netting amounts, and the legal area what the agreement lets NRIO withhold.

## Acquittal and closing the grant

Acquittal is the grantee's account of how the grant was spent, and NRIO's check of it. Paragraph 14.12 says well-documented arrangements for financial accountability are the basis of effective acquittal, and that "Reliable, timely and adequate evidence is required" to show the grant was spent as agreed. Officials should balance the stringency of acquittal against the risk and the cost of compliance.

This is an illustration. RCFP final reports and acquittals are due by 31 March 2029. Each shows what was delivered, evidence that the work is finished and any required approvals signed off, and a spending statement by budget line certified by the grantee's authorised officers. Councils add their own spending.

```figure
type: checklist
title: Before the final payment and closure
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's acquittal check. The question-marked item decides whether the matter goes to the fraud process.
- Deliverables match the schedule | photos, and the site visit note for Medium and High grantees
- Spending statement by budget line | certified by the grantee's authorised officers
- Every line matches the schedule or an approved variation | the Branch Manager's approval on the file, not a mention of one
- Council contribution shown | at least 25 per cent of eligible project costs
- Final instalment = eligible spending less instalments paid | never more than the 20 per cent in the schedule
- Commitment closed and closing letter sent | the forecast stops carrying money that will not be paid
- x Accepting "NRIO agreed" without the approved variation | only the Branch Manager's written approval varies an agreement
- ? Does anything suggest misuse rather than error | if so, NRIO's fraud process comes before any conversation with the grantee
```

Then close the grant: pay the final instalment or record why less is paid, close the commitment, write to the grantee, check every reportable variation is on GrantConnect (Chapter 9), and file the decision, its reasons and the evidence together. A file that ends with a payment and no closing note leaves the next reader guessing.

## When it goes wrong: the email that varied an agreement

This is an illustration. Brindlemere's change-room tenders came in on Friday 15 October 2027, A$41,000 under the budget in its schedule. On Friday 19 November the club's president emailed Sam asking to use the savings on "related improvements at the ground". Sam had worked closely with the club since the signing problem in July, and replied that day: fine in principle, keep it related to the facilities and show it in the reports. He held no power to vary agreements; NRIO's instructions give it to the Branch Manager. Sam did not see his reply as deciding anything, so it never reached the grant file.

The mid-point report arrived on Wednesday 19 April 2028. The change rooms were on track, and the report said NRIO had agreed the savings could go on related improvements. Ben flagged the sentence. Sam, remembering his email, accepted the milestone, and the second A$124,000 was paid. Between August and October 2028 the club spent A$24,000 on an electronic scoreboard, A$11,500 on a ride-on mower and A$5,500 on branded volunteer shirts and an opening-day event.

On Friday 16 March 2029, Ben's acquittal check matched every line of the club's statement against the schedule and found A$24,000 + A$11,500 + A$5,500 = A$41,000 spent on items it did not contain. On legal advice, the Branch Manager treated them as the variation request they should have been in November 2027, and declined it on Friday 13 April 2029. None was part of the change-room upgrade the panel assessed and the Minister approved, and the mower, shirts and event were not part of any facility.

Spending on the agreed project was A$310,000 − A$41,000 = A$269,000, and NRIO had paid A$124,000 + A$124,000 = A$248,000. The final instalment was A$269,000 − A$248,000 = A$21,000 instead of A$62,000.

No money had to be recovered, but only because 20 per cent was still held. The club had spent A$41,000 of its own money relying on an NRIO officer's written word. NRIO wrote to it with its reasons and an apology, its legal area advised on the club's position, and the matter was reported through NRIO's process for possible non-compliance with the finance law. Almost seventeen months had passed since the email, and nothing could be undone.

The controls are ordinary. A request to change an agreement goes on the grant file the day it arrives, and the officer's only answer is that the Branch Manager will decide. The officer who gave an informal answer does not accept the report that relies on it. A mid-point check that looked for the approved variation behind "NRIO agreed" would have stopped this in April 2028, months before the money was spent. Sam had joined from a policy role in mid-2026, and nobody had walked him through the branch's delegations. NRIO now does that in every new starter's first week.

## Exercise: monitoring four grantees

This is an illustration. On Monday 6 September 2027, Ben rates four grantees and proposes their monitoring to Sam. Use the branch's descriptors and NRIO's bands above.

| Grantee | Grant | What Ben knows |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. | A$420,000 | Fixed-price builder's quote; asbestos in the roof, its removal costed; the town's only public venue |
| Shire of Eastbrook | A$500,000 | Experienced council projects team; building approval granted in August 2027; A$300,000 contribution in the adopted budget |
| Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. | A$85,000 | Volunteer group; first grant above A$20,000; licensed electrician not yet engaged; key equipment on a 12-week order |
| Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. | A$140,000 | Builder withdrew in August 2027; new quote A$22,000 above the kitchen budget; no plan yet to fund the gap |

Rate each grantee, set its monitoring, and name one trigger that would change its rating.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

| Grantee | Likelihood | Consequence | Score and band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shire of Eastbrook | 1: experienced, funded, approval in hand | 4: more than A$100,000 at risk | 1 × 4 = 4, Low |
| Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. | 2: capable, firm quote, asbestos costed | 4: more than A$100,000 at risk; the town's only venue | 2 × 4 = 8, Medium |
| Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. | 3: first project of this size; electrician and equipment open | 3: A$85,000 is between A$25,000 and A$100,000 | 3 × 3 = 9, Medium |
| Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. | 4: no builder and an unfunded gap | 4: more than A$100,000 at risk | 4 × 4 = 16, High |

- **Eastbrook (Low).** The agreement's reports, and a call when each is due. Each report shows the council's own spending, because the 25 per cent minimum applied to every council applicant; Eastbrook's plan is A$300,000 of A$800,000, or 37.5 per cent. Trigger: any change to the council's budget for the project. A Low score with a consequence of 4 means watching the right thing, not watching nothing.
- **Kellerton (Medium).** A quarterly call, photos with each report, and a site visit before the mid-point report is accepted. Trigger: asbestos removal costing more than quoted, or the program slipping past the mid-point date.
- **Tallowood Ridge (Medium).** A quarterly call, starting with help on the report template, photos, and a site visit at completion. Trigger: no licensed electrician engaged by the end of October 2027.
- **Corran Bay (High).** A monthly update, a site visit before works start, and written evidence that a builder is engaged and the A$22,000 gap is funded before the mid-point report can be accepted. The Director hears at once, the Branch Manager within two working days, and the rating is reviewed monthly.

Corran Bay was approved against officials' recommendation, in the Minister's own electorate (Chapter 6). That history must not soften or harden its monitoring; the evidence sets it. Once the association signs a builder and shows the gap is funded, the likelihood falls to 2 and the score to 2 × 4 = 8, Medium, and its monitoring steps down. The same method for every grantee is what makes each rating defensible.
