Australia's workplaces are among the most linguistically diverse in the world. Many skilled engineers, technicians, tradespeople, managers and business owners speak English as a second, third or fourth language. Many are highly capable but feel held back because they are less confident speaking English in meetings, on the phone or in emails than in their first language.

Language confidence grows with practice and with a toolkit of reliable phrases for common situations. This article offers practical English phrases for everyday business situations, explains email conventions, describes some Australian workplace norms that can surprise newcomers and suggests how teams can communicate well across languages. It is written both for non-native speakers and for the colleagues and managers who work with them.

## Greetings

Greetings open every interaction. A smile and a clear greeting break the ice in any language.

| Situation | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Formal or first meeting | "Good morning." "Good afternoon." "Hello, how do you do?" "Pleased to meet you." |
| Everyday at work | "Hi, how are you?" "Morning!" "How's it going?" "How are you going?" |
| Seeing someone after a while | "Good to see you again." "Long time no see. How have you been?" |
| Leaving | "Have a good evening." "See you tomorrow." "Take care." "Have a good weekend." |

**Responding**: to "How are you?", a short reply is expected: "Good, thanks. How are you?" or "Not bad, thanks. And you?" It is a greeting, not a detailed question about your health.

**"How are you going?"** is a common Australian greeting. It means "How are you?", and a typical reply is "Good, thanks. You?"

## Introductions

- "Hi, I'm Priya. I'm the new project engineer."
- "My name is Wei. I look after quality in the plant."
- "I don't think we've met. I'm Ahmed, from the design team."
- "Nice to meet you. What do you do here?"
- "Where are you based?" or "Which team are you in?"

When introducing others: "Sam, this is Maria, our new estimator. Maria, Sam runs the workshop."

## Names and titles in Australia

Australian workplaces are generally informal about names. Most people use first names, including with managers, senior leaders and customers, often from the first meeting. Using "Mr" or "Ms" with a family name is more formal and is used mainly in very formal settings or until invited to use a first name.

If you are unsure, use the person's full name or "Hello" until you hear how others address them, or simply ask: "Should I call you Michael?"

Calling people "sir" or "madam" is uncommon in most Australian workplaces, although it is still used in some customer service settings.

## Asking for clarification

Not understanding something is normal in any language. Asking politely is professional, and far better than guessing.

- "Sorry, could you repeat that, please?"
- "Sorry, I didn't catch the last part."
- "Could you speak a little more slowly, please?"
- "What do you mean by...?"
- "Could you explain that another way?"
- "Just to check I've understood: you need the drawings by Friday, is that right?"
- "Can I confirm that in an email?"

**Paraphrasing**, repeating back what you understood in your own words, is one of the most useful habits in any workplace, whether or not English is your first language. It catches misunderstandings before they cause mistakes.

## Apologising and handling mistakes

- "I'm sorry. That was my mistake. I'll fix it today."
- "Sorry to bother you, but do you have a minute?"
- "Apologies for the delay in getting back to you."
- "Sorry, I was distracted. Could you say that again?"

In Australian workplaces, owning a mistake directly and proposing a fix is respected. Lengthy apologies are less common than a brief acknowledgement and action.

## Requests and offers

- "Could you please send me the latest drawing?"
- "Would you mind checking this before I send it?"
- "Can you help me with something, please?"
- "Do you need a hand with that?"
- "I'll be with you in a minute."
- "Sure, I'll do it this afternoon."
- "When could you get back to me?"

Words such as "please", "thank you" and "would you mind" matter. Direct requests without them can sound abrupt in English, even when that is not intended.

## Thanks and appreciation

- "Thank you. That's very helpful."
- "Thanks for your help with this."
- "That's very kind of you."
- "I really appreciate it."
- "Cheers." (informal, very common in Australia)

Replying to thanks: "You're welcome." "No problem." "No worries." "Any time."

**"No worries"** is perhaps the most Australian phrase of all. It means "you're welcome", "that's fine" or "don't worry about it", depending on the situation.

## Encouragement and congratulations

- "Congratulations on the promotion!"
- "Well done. That was a great presentation."
- "Keep up the good work."
- "I'm happy for you."
- "I know you can do it."

Supporting a colleague who is upset:

- "Don't worry, these things happen."
- "Don't take it to heart."
- "Is there anything I can do to help?"

## Meetings

| Purpose | Phrases |
|---|---|
| Asking for opinions | "What do you think?" "How does that sound?" "Any thoughts on this?" |
| Agreeing | "I agree." "That makes sense." "Sounds good." |
| Disagreeing politely | "I see your point, but..." "I'm not sure that will work because..." "Could we consider another option?" |
| Adding a point | "Can I add something?" "Just to build on that..." |
| Interrupting politely | "Sorry to interrupt, but..." |
| Checking actions | "So, who's doing what by when?" "Can we confirm next steps?" |
| Asking questions | "Do you have any questions?" "Can I ask a question?" |

**Polite disagreement** is expected and valued in many Australian workplaces. Direct but respectful disagreement, focused on the issue rather than the person, is usually welcome. If speaking up in a group feels uncomfortable, you can raise points with the meeting leader beforehand or afterwards, or put them in writing.

## Phone and video calls

Phone calls are often harder than face-to-face conversation because you cannot see facial expressions or lips.

- "Hello, this is Hana from GreenTech Engineering. Could I speak to John, please?"
- "Sorry, the line's not very clear. Could you say that again?"
- "Could you spell that for me, please?"
- "I'll send you an email to confirm what we discussed."

Following up important calls in writing protects everyone from misunderstandings.

## Email conventions

### Greetings

- **"Hi Sarah,"**: the most common in Australian business email, even with customers.
- **"Dear Sarah,"**: slightly more formal.
- **"Dear Sir or Madam,"**: only when you do not know the recipient's name. Try to find a name first.
- **"Hi team,"** or **"Hi all,"**: for groups.

### Closings

- **"Kind regards,"** or **"Regards,"**: the standard professional closing.
- **"Thanks,"** or **"Many thanks,"**: when you have made a request.
- **"Cheers,"**: informal.
- **"Yours faithfully"** (when you opened with "Dear Sir or Madam") and **"Yours sincerely"** (when you used a name): mainly in formal letters, rarely in everyday email.

### Useful email phrases

- "I'm writing to ask about..."
- "Please find attached the quote for..."
- "Could you please confirm by Friday?"
- "Please let me know if you have any questions."
- "Thanks in advance for your help."
- "Following up on my email of 3 March..."

### Keep it clear

Short sentences, clear subject lines and one main topic per email make messages easier to write and to read, for everyone. Put the main point or request first. The article on [stopping emailing and starting communicating](/reados/collections/communication-and-professional-presence/stop-emailing-start-communicating/) offers more advice.

## Australian expressions you may hear

Australian English includes informal expressions and abbreviations that can confuse newcomers:

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Arvo | Afternoon |
| Smoko | A short break |
| Reckon | Think ("I reckon it'll be done Friday") |
| Heaps | A lot |
| Keen | Interested or eager ("Are you keen?") |
| Fair enough | That's reasonable |
| Give it a go | Try it |
| Flat out | Very busy |
| Sweet as | Fine, good |
| Hooroo, see ya | Goodbye |

If you hear an expression you do not understand, it is perfectly acceptable to ask: "Sorry, what does that mean?" Most people will be happy to explain.

## Small talk

Small talk, the brief friendly conversation before meetings, in kitchens and at events, builds relationships in Australian workplaces. It can feel difficult in a second language because topics shift quickly. Safe, common topics include:

- **The weekend**: "Did you do anything good on the weekend?" "Any plans for the weekend?"
- **The weather**: "Hot today, isn't it?" "Looks like rain this arvo."
- **Work in general**: "How's the project going?" "Busy week?"
- **Sport**, which many Australians follow closely: "Did you watch the game?"
- **Food and travel**: "Have you been anywhere nice lately?" "Any good places for lunch around here?"

Topics usually avoided with people you do not know well include salary, religion, politics and personal questions about age, marriage or children. Listening and asking a follow-up question, such as "Oh, really? What was that like?", keeps a conversation going without needing to say much yourself.

## Building confidence and skill

- **Practise daily**: speak, read and write in English every day, even briefly.
- **Learn phrases, not just words**: complete phrases are faster to use in real conversations.
- **Read aloud**: reading articles aloud improves fluency and pronunciation.
- **Keep a phrase notebook**: write down useful phrases you hear or read, and practise them.
- **Write a short paragraph each day** on any topic, to build written fluency.
- **Watch and listen**: podcasts, news and shows in English help with listening, especially to different accents.
- **Use support**: in Australia, the Adult Migrant English Program offers free English classes to eligible migrants and humanitarian entrants, and TAFE and community colleges offer workplace English courses.
- **Accept mistakes**: native speakers make mistakes too. Clarity matters more than perfect grammar.

## For colleagues and managers: communicating across languages

Good communication is a shared responsibility. Native English speakers can make a big difference:

- **Speak clearly and at a moderate pace**, not loudly.
- **Avoid slang, idioms and abbreviations**, or explain them.
- **Use plain English** and short sentences.
- **Check understanding** by asking open questions, rather than "Do you understand?", which most people answer "yes" regardless.
- **Put important information in writing.**
- **Use visuals**: drawings, photos, diagrams and demonstrations work across languages, especially in technical and safety settings.
- **Be patient** and give people time to respond.
- **Value the person's expertise**, which is not measured by their accent.

### Safety communication

On worksites and in factories, misunderstandings can cause injuries. Under work health and safety laws, employers must provide information, training and instruction in a form workers can understand. That may mean translated materials, visual instructions, demonstrations, interpreters and checking understanding practically rather than assuming it.

## A worked example

A CNC programmer who moved to Melbourne from Vietnam is highly skilled but rarely speaks in production meetings. Her manager notices and talks to her privately. She explains that meetings move quickly and she worries about making mistakes in English.

They agree on a few changes. The manager circulates the agenda a day before, so she can prepare her points. She keeps a notebook of meeting phrases and practises them. The manager asks her directly for her view on technical items, giving her space to answer. She enrols in a workplace English evening course.

Within three months, she regularly contributes in meetings. Her suggestion to change the machining sequence for a high-volume part reduces cycle time by 15 per cent.

## Frequently asked questions

**Will my accent hold me back?** Clear pronunciation matters more than accent. Australia's workplaces include people with every kind of accent, and most colleagues care far more about what you say than how it sounds. Speaking at a steady pace and pausing between points makes any accent easier to follow.

**Should I write emails in very formal English to be safe?** Usually not. Overly formal language can sound distant in Australian workplaces. A friendly, clear tone, such as "Hi Tom, could you please send the updated drawing by Thursday? Thanks, Mei", is normal and professional.

**What if I misunderstand an instruction?** Ask early and confirm in writing. Saying "Just to confirm, you'd like..." shows care, not weakness, and prevents costly mistakes.

## Summary

Confidence in business English grows with practice and a reliable set of phrases. Learn phrases for greetings, introductions, clarifying, apologising, requests, thanks and meetings. Use Australian email conventions, and expect informal first-name culture and local expressions. Ask for clarification and paraphrase to confirm understanding. Practise daily through speaking, reading aloud and writing, and use available English programs. Colleagues and managers share responsibility for clear communication: speak plainly, avoid idioms, check understanding, use visuals and value people's expertise regardless of their accent.
