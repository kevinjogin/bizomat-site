A supplier's proposal arrives with six claims and a price. The buyer's first question is not whether the claims are true. It is who has to show that they are. If the buyer must prove each claim false before declining, every proposal is accepted by default. If the supplier must prove each claim true beyond any doubt, nothing is ever bought. Somewhere between lies a fair allocation, and settling it first makes every later step easier.

This chapter sets the default position for the retrofit decision, allocates the burden for each claim, and examines the most misused argument in purchasing: that a lack of complaints proves a product works.

## The proposal

This is an illustration. The plant engineer at a moulding factory receives a supplier's proposal for a retrofit of the two cooling-water pumps, P1 and P2. It costs A$21,300 excluding GST and makes six claims.

```figure
caption: This is an illustration. The register lists each claim in the supplier's own words. Most of the evidence sits with the supplier; the evidence about the site sits with the buyer.
- C1 | "Saves up to 40% of pump energy" | The drives at this site's duty | Physics is public; the duty is the buyer's
- C2 | "Delivers 70 °C water at a COP of 7.5" | The optional heat pump | The supplier's test reports
- C3 | "Seals last three times as long as standard" | The long-life seals | The supplier's test and field data; the buyer's own seal records
- C4 | "Controller MTBF 150,000 hours, field proven" | The monitoring controller | The supplier's field failure records
- C5 | "Maintenance-free" | The whole package | The supplier's manuals and service schedules
- C6 | "Payback in 14 months" | The whole package | Built from C1 and C3 and the buyer's costs
```

## The default position

Every decision has a default: what happens if nobody proves anything. For the retrofit, the default is the plant as it stands. The pumps run, throttled, with standard seals, and the factory knows from four years of records how they behave and what they cost. The default is not free, but it is known. The proposal asks the factory to move from a known position to an uncertain one, so the reasons for moving must be shown.

This idea has a long history in a different field. In 1972 Antony Flew argued, in a paper called "The Presumption of Atheism", that in a dispute about whether something exists, the burden of proof lies with the party asserting that it does, much as a court presumes innocence until guilt is shown. Christine Overall, writing in *The Cambridge Companion to Atheism*, summarises Flew's view as two tasks for the person making a positive claim: show that the claim is meaningful, so that it is clear what would make it true, and show that there are good reasons to think it applies. She also suggests that "burden of evidence" is a fairer phrase than "burden of proof", since proof is rarely available in such questions.

Both points carry straight over to the proposal. The supplier, who makes the claims, must first make each one definite enough that it is clear what would make it true. "Up to 40%" fails that test, as Chapter 4 shows. Then the supplier must provide evidence that the claim applies to this site. And the evidence asked for is evidence, not proof: enough to act on, scaled to the stakes, as Chapter 5 sets out.

## The presumption is not free

The presumption has critics, and their objection matters for purchasing. William Lane Craig, in the same Companion, argues that a presumption in favour of one side cannot simply be declared; it needs its own justification. He points out that a person who wants to conclude that something does not exist, rather than merely that it has not been shown to exist, takes on a burden of their own. The Companion's editor, Michael Martin, makes a related distinction in his introduction: not believing a claim is different from believing it false, and to establish that a claim is false requires both refuting the reasons for it and giving reasons against it.

For the plant engineer this means two things. First, her default is a decision too. Keeping the throttled pumps has a cost, about 11.4 kW each for 6,000 hours a year, and declining the proposal is a choice to keep paying it. The status quo carries no burden of proof, but it does carry a price, and an honest comparison includes it. Second, she must keep separate two conclusions: "the supplier has not shown this" and "this is false". The first is enough to decline a claim for now. The second needs evidence of its own. Chapter 2 builds on that difference.

## When an absence of evidence counts

Craig's chapter also contains the most useful single point in this debate for anyone who buys equipment. He cites the forensic maxim that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, then qualifies it: an absence of evidence does count against a claim, but only when, if the claim were true, we would expect to have more evidence than we do.

Purchasing is full of arguments from absence. "No customer has complained." "We have had no failures reported." "Nobody has ever asked for that." Each is evidence only if complaints, failures or requests would reliably reach the person saying it.

```figure

title: Does this absence of evidence count?
question: If the claim were false, would the evidence of it have reached the person who says there is none?
caption: An absence counts in proportion to how reliably the missing evidence would have been seen. Ask how failures are reported, by whom and to whom, before treating silence as support.
- Yes, reliably | It counts | A complete, independent record with no failures is real evidence for the claim.
- Only sometimes | It counts weakly | Treat silence as a small amount of support, and ask how incomplete the reporting is.
- * No, or unknown | It does not count | Silence says nothing; ask for evidence that would have been recorded either way.
```

This is an illustration. The supplier's proposal says of the controller that "no field failures have been reported in the past year". The plant engineer asks how a field failure would reach the supplier. The answer: through its distributors, when a customer claims under warranty. Controllers that fail after warranty, or that customers replace themselves, or that are claimed through an installer, would not be counted. The absence of reports therefore says little. What would count is a failure record from a group of installed controllers whose every failure is known, such as those under a service contract. Chapter 2 examines the data the supplier eventually provides.

## Allocating the burden claim by claim

The fairest rule is that each party carries the burden for the facts in its own domain: the facts it asserts, or the facts only it can find out cheaply. That splits the burden in the retrofit case more evenly than it first appears.

```figure
type: steps
title: Allocating the burden for each claim
caption: The same rule applied to every claim. Two burdens fall on the buyer, because the facts are about its own site.
- Name the default | The plant as it stands, with its known performance and cost.
- Find who asserts the claim | The supplier for C1 to C6; the buyer for any claim about its site, such as the pumps' duty.
- Find who holds the evidence | Field records, test reports and manuals with the supplier; flow, head, hours and failure records with the buyer.
- Assign each burden | To the party that asserts the fact, or that can establish it most cheaply.
- Ask for evidence, not proof | Set the standard by the consequence of being wrong (Chapter 5).
- Record what is still unproven | The decision can go ahead on some claims while others are tested (Chapter 8).
```

Two burdens fall on the buyer. The energy saving from a variable speed drive depends on how far the flow is turned down and how much of the pump's head goes to lifting water rather than overcoming friction, and only the factory knows those. If the plant engineer wants to test C1, she must first measure the pumps' actual flow, head and hours. And if she wants to test C3, the claim that seals last three times as long, she must know how long the present seals last. Her four years of records are the baseline against which the claim will be judged. A buyer who has not measured its own plant cannot hold a supplier to a claim about it.

### Parsons and the shifting burden

Keith Parsons, discussing attempts to move the burden in the philosophical debate, notes how a change in the standards for justified belief can shift the burden from one side to the other, so that the critic must now prove a negative. The same move appears in sales. A supplier who says "show me why it wouldn't work at your site" has quietly shifted the burden from the claimant to the buyer. The plant engineer's reply is polite and firm: the claim is the supplier's, so the evidence is too, except for the site facts, which she will supply.

## Writing the burden into the reply

The plant engineer turns the allocation into a reply to the supplier. It is a short letter, and its structure is the same for any proposal.

For each claim, it restates the claim in the supplier's words and says what the factory would need in order to rely on it. For C1, a calculation of the saving at the pumps' measured duty, which the factory will supply, with the drive's own losses stated. For C2, the heat pump's test report, with the source and sink temperatures at which the coefficient of performance was measured. For C3, the seal's test or field data in the same duty, with the failure records it rests on. For C4, the field failure data behind the MTBF figure: the number of units, the operating hours counted and how, and the failures recorded. For C5, the maintenance schedule from the products' own manuals. For C6, the calculation of the payback, with each input and its source.

The letter also states what the factory will provide: four weeks of logged power, flow and hours on each pump, its seal failure records for four years, and its electricity tariff. And it says how each claim will be checked after installation, if the factory buys: metered energy against the baseline, and seal life against the records.

The reply does not accuse the supplier of anything. It treats each claim as a statement the supplier presumably wants to be relied on, and asks for what reliance requires. A supplier that answers in full is easier to buy from. One that answers with more adjectives has told the buyer something useful too.

## Burdens inside the factory

The same allocation applies between the factory's own departments, and there it is often harder to enforce. In the retrofit discussion, the production manager says that the cooling-water flow cannot be reduced, because the moulds would run hot. If that is true, the drives will save little, because the flow they would turn down is the flow production needs.

The production manager asserts it, so the burden is his, and the evidence is the factory's to collect. The plant engineer proposes a short trial: reduce the flow to P1's circuit in steps over a week, with mould temperatures and part quality logged, and stop at the first sign of a problem. That is cheaper than arguing, and it settles a claim that would otherwise decide the purchase by default. The production manager agrees, partly because the trial protects him too: if the moulds do overheat, the evidence is on record, and nobody can later blame him for blocking a saving.

Internal claims often escape the burden because the person making them has authority, or because nobody wants to question a colleague. But a claim from inside the factory can stop a sound purchase as surely as a supplier's claim can start an unsound one. The rule is the same for both: whoever asserts a fact carries the burden of evidence for it.

## The failure case: the controller nobody measured

This is an illustration. Three years earlier the factory bought an energy-saving controller for its air compressors, on a brochure claim of "savings of 20 to 35%". Nobody measured the compressors' energy use before it was installed. A year later the electricity bill was slightly lower, but production had also fallen, the tariff had changed and one compressor had been overhauled. Nobody could say whether the controller had saved anything. The supplier pointed to the lower bill. The finance manager pointed to the lower production. Both were guessing.

### How it is detected and corrected

The failure is detected by asking, before any purchase that rests on a performance claim: what measurement will show whether the claim came true, and have we taken the baseline? If the answer is no, the claim cannot be checked after installation, and the burden of proof has effectively been abandoned. The correction for the retrofit is to log each pump's power, flow and hours for four representative weeks before anything is changed, with production volume recorded alongside, so that the same measurement afterwards can be compared like with like.

## What the burden does not require

Allocating the burden to the supplier does not mean demanding certainty. No supplier can prove that a seal will last three times as long at this site before it has run here. If the factory demands that, it will never buy anything new, and it will keep paying for the throttled pumps forever. The burden is to provide the evidence a reasonable buyer would need to act, at the stakes involved, and to make the claim definite enough to be checked afterwards. Chapters 5 and 8 develop that balance.

Nor does it mean distrust. A supplier that makes definite claims, provides its evidence and agrees how the claims will be checked is easier to buy from than one that offers adjectives. The allocation protects both sides: the buyer from claims it cannot verify, and the supplier from disputes about what was promised.

## Exercise: allocate the burden

For each of the following claims, say who carries the burden of evidence, what evidence would count, and whether an absence of evidence would count.

1. A contractor says its insulation will cut a tank's heat loss by half.
2. The factory's production manager says the cooling-water flow cannot be reduced, because the moulds would overheat.
3. A seal supplier says "we have never had a seal returned from a plastics plant".
4. A drive supplier says its drives are "proven in thousands of installations".

**Worked answer.**

1. The contractor asserts it, so the contractor carries the burden for the insulation's performance; the factory carries the burden for the tank's present heat loss, which it can measure. Evidence that counts: a heat-loss calculation from the insulation's conductivity and thickness at the tank's temperature, and a before-and-after measurement. Absence of evidence is irrelevant here; the claim can be calculated.
2. The production manager asserts it, about the factory's own plant, so the burden is internal. Evidence that counts: mould temperatures logged at reduced flow in a controlled trial. The claim matters because it limits C1: if flow cannot be reduced, a variable speed drive saves little.
3. The supplier asserts it, but it is an argument from absence. It counts only if returns from plastics plants would reliably reach the supplier and be recorded by industry. Ask how many seals have been sold into plastics plants, for how long, and how returns are recorded. Without that, it says little.
4. The supplier asserts it. "Thousands of installations" is evidence that the product exists and sells, not that it meets any particular claim at this site. Ask for the claim it is meant to support, and for evidence about that claim specifically.
