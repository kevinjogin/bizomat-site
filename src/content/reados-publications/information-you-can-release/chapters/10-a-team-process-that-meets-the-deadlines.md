Every chapter of this book has had a date in it: 14 days to acknowledge a request, 30 days to decide it, 30 days to assess a data breach, 10 working days to publish a release. Teams rarely miss these limits through ignorance of the law. They miss them because a request sits in the wrong log, an extension stays in a phone call, or the one person who knows the date is away. The law sets the limits; only a process meets them.

This is an illustration. By the end of October 2027, NRIO's Information Access and Privacy Team had come through five crowded months: the RCFP request and its two releases, the spreadsheet breach, the community group secretary's request, two internal reviews and an IC review application. Each problem left a control behind: the intake rule (Chapter 1), the hold notice (Chapter 2), the written-only rule for extensions (Chapter 3), the release check against the consultation register (Chapter 5), the five questions on reasons (Chapter 6) and the publication check (Chapter 9). After one more failure in October, Ruth Adeyemi joins them into one process, sizes the team against it and plans for the weeks when everything falls due at once.

## Intake and triage on the day it arrives

Intake is where most deadlines are won or lost: the clock starts on receipt, not when someone reads the email. This is an illustration. NRIO's one mailbox for FOI and privacy requests is screened every working day (Chapter 1), and every item is registered that day under the law it invokes, not its subject. An FOI request meets the four elements in s15(2) of the [Freedom of Information Act 1982](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text). A request for a person's own information that does not invoke the Act is an APP 12 request, with its own 30 days. A suspected data breach starts the Part IIIC assessment clock, and containing it comes first.

Triage then asks five questions:

- **Which law and which clock?** The register calculates the dates.
- **Is it valid?** If not, the agency must take reasonable steps to help (s15(3)).
- **Is it ours?** A transfer needs the other agency's agreement; time runs from first receipt (s16(1), (5)(b)).
- **How big?** Pages, document holders, third parties, likely exemptions.
- **Who works on it?** An owner, a backup and an authorised decision-maker (s23).

This is an illustration. NRIO sorts items into three classes. Routine items, such as most own-information requests, get a sample check. Standard items get a second-officer check before decision. Complex items get the full check, a written plan in the first week and a hold notice on day one. The RCFP request was complex from the start: three kinds of document, the Minister's office and likely third parties. Priya Shah became case officer and Liam O'Connor decision-maker that morning, but the hold notice went out only after the deletion in Chapter 2.

## A case register with alerts for more than one person

One register holds every FOI request, APP 12 and APP 13 request, privacy complaint, breach assessment, review and disclosure log entry. Each entry has:

- the event date and every statutory date that follows from it, calculated by the register, not typed;
- the written basis for any change of date: the s15(6) determination, the s15AA agreement and notice, or the Commissioner's s15AB decision (Chapter 3);
- an owner, a backup and a decision-maker, named separately;
- the consultation register, the search record and the hours spent.

This is an illustration. NRIO's register sends alerts five and two working days before each statutory date to the owner, the backup and the team mailbox. Every Monday a rotating pair runs a 15-minute meeting on everything due in the next 10 working days. NRIO's rule is that the backup for an internal review had no part in the original decision.

## Templates and checks

Templates carry the parts of a document the law fixes, so that officers spend their time on the facts. NRIO keeps one for each step with a legal form: the acknowledgement (s15(5)(a)), extension notices (s15(6)(b), s15AA), consultation notices (s27, s27A), the charges notice (s29(1)), decision notices (s26, s27(6), s27A(5)), the internal review decision (s54C), the APP 12 refusal notice (APP 12.9), the breach assessment record and the log entry (s11C).

A template cannot make findings. Section 26(1)(a) requires the notice to "state the findings on any material questions of fact", and only the officer who has read the documents can write them. A template sentence that states a conclusion invites the thin reasons of Chapter 6. Each template has an owner, a date and a review whenever the law changes. A template that still sends review to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal has been wrong since the Administrative Review Tribunal replaced it on 14 October 2024.

This is an illustration. At NRIO a second officer, never the one who prepared the work, checks every decision that refuses anything, every release with third-party information and every log entry. Ruth samples one routine own-information release in five. The checker asks questions; the decision stays with the authorised decision-maker.

## Staffing the work in hours

A team is the right size when its capacity covers the year's work with room for the weeks that go wrong. Count the work in units, give each unit an hours figure, and convert to full-time equivalents (FTE).

This is an illustration. Ruth starts from the team's expected workload for 2026–27 (Chapter 1). The hours per case are NRIO's planning assumptions, to be replaced by measured hours from the register. So is counting "a handful" of breach assessments as five.

| Work | Cases | Hours each | Hours |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| FOI requests for the applicant's own personal information | 60 | 12 | 720 |
| Other FOI requests | 80 | 30 | 2,400 |
| Privacy enquiries and complaints | 25 | 10 | 250 |
| Data breach assessments | 5 | 40 | 200 |
| Casework | 170 | 21 on average | 3,570 |

Not all work comes in cases. NRIO adds an allowance of 940 hours: reviews 120, the IPS and disclosure log 100, privacy advice and impact assessments 100, training document holders 100, and reporting and team management 520. That makes 3,570 + 940 = 4,510 hours.

Productive hours use the planning figures from Chapter 3: a 37.5-hour week and 30 per cent of time lost to leave, public holidays, meetings and training. A year is 52 × 37.5 = 1,950 paid hours, and 70 per cent of that is 1,365 productive hours per person.

```figure
type: formula
title: From the team's workload to FTE
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's planning assumptions. FTE is rounded to two decimal places only at the end.
formula: Workload hours = cases × minutes per case ÷ 60, plus non-case hours
formula: Productive hours per person = 52 × 37.5 × 70%
formula: FTE = workload hours ÷ productive hours per person
- Cases | 170 a year, at an average of 1,260 minutes (21 hours)
- Non-case work | 940 hours a year
- Productive share | 70%, after leave, holidays, meetings and training
step: 170 × 1,260 ÷ 60 = 3,570 hours; 3,570 + 940 = 4,510 hours
step: 52 × 37.5 = 1,950; 1,950 × 70% = 1,365 hours per person
step: 4,510 ÷ 1,365 = 3.3040, so 3.30 FTE and 4 people
```

The [Staffing Need](/tools/staffing-need/) tool does the casework part. Enter 170 units, 1,260 minutes per unit, 1,950 paid hours per person and 70 per cent efficiency. It returns 3,570 hours of workload, 1,365 productive hours per person, 2.62 FTE (3,570 ÷ 1,365 = 2.6154) and 3 whole people. It warns when rounding up leaves less than 0.15 FTE spare, and here 3 − 2.62 leaves 0.38. Add the non-case hours outside it, and do not remove leave twice: the 70 per cent already does.

Four people give 4 × 1,365 = 5,460 hours against 4,510, leaving 950 hours spare: the team is 82.6 per cent loaded (4,510 ÷ 5,460), close to Chapter 3's eight-week check of 82.1 per cent. The average hides two things. Peaks: June to August 2027 brought the RCFP request, the breach and the secretary's request together. And skills: only Ruth and Liam can decide FOI requests, so spare hours help little if both are busy.

## Escalate early, with options

This is an illustration. NRIO's escalation triggers are a statutory date within five working days with the work not on track, a document holder more than three working days late, a possible eligible data breach, and a case officer and decision-maker who disagree on an exemption. The path runs from the case officer to Liam, Ruth, the General Counsel and, where only the agency head can act, the agency head.

Escalate with options:

- ask the applicant to agree in writing to up to 30 more days, and notify the Commissioner (s15AA);
- apply to the Commissioner for more time for a complex or voluminous request (s15AB);
- begin a request consultation process where a practical refusal reason may exist (s24, s24AA);
- move work within the team, or ask for help from outside it.

Never let a period run out quietly. A deemed refusal is the agency head's own decision (s15AC(3)). If the Minister's office is told that a release is coming, that is information, not approval: the decision belongs to the authorised officer (s23), and the timetable to the Act.

## Training the people who hold the documents

Most of the hours in a large request are spent outside the team. Sam Reid's officers searched their own mailboxes for the RCFP request (Chapter 3), and one of them deleted a folder of drafts within it before it was restored (Chapter 2). Document holders need to know five things:

- a request reaches every format: emails, chats, texts and file notes;
- nothing within an open request, review or complaint is destroyed or altered;
- how to search and record the places, terms, dates and time taken;
- to send everything within scope, unmarked, with any concerns noted separately;
- NRIO's turnaround of three working days.

This is an illustration. Ruth's team gives each program area a 30-minute session each year, a one-page guide and the hold notice template. The search record shows why: two of Priya's 14 search hours went on emails that should have been on the master file.

Privacy training is a legal duty under the [APP Code 2017](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2017L01396/latest/text). An agency must include privacy education or training in any staff induction program (s16(1)), and must take reasonable steps to provide appropriate training annually to all staff with access to personal information (s16(2)).

## Learning from reviews

Every review and complaint is advice the team has already paid for. Record what was decided, why, and what will change: a template, a check, a training point or a records habit. Read the pattern each quarter from the team's own register.

This is an illustration. The RCFP internal review affirmed Liam's decision (Chapter 6), but the reviewer had to rebuild the findings his notice left out. Affirmed is not the same as right first time, so the five questions became part of the check. The sporting club's early release (Chapter 5) changed the release check. The IC review is still running; its outcome will be recorded the same way.

## When it goes wrong: one person holding every deadline

This is an illustration. Until October 2027, the register named one owner for each case and sent alerts only to that owner, and the weekly check was always Liam's. Liam ran some cases himself and coordinated internal reviews. On Monday 11 October 2027 he fell ill without warning and was away for two weeks, returning on Monday 25 October.

Two of his items fell due while he was away. An internal review of another request, received on Tuesday 21 September, needed a fresh decision by Thursday 21 October, 30 days later (s54C(3)). The reviewer, from another branch, was waiting for the file Liam had planned to send. The alerts of Thursday 14 and Tuesday 19 October went to Liam's inbox, and the weekly checks of 11 and 18 October did not happen. Separately, an own-information request Liam was running fell due on Wednesday 20 October. Priya learned of it only when the applicant rang on Tuesday 19 October, and Ruth spent a day and a half on an unfamiliar file to decide it in time.

When 21 October passed, the agency head was taken to have personally affirmed the original decision (s54D(2)). Liam found it at his first check on 25 October. Ruth applied in writing to the Information Commissioner for further time (s54D(3)); the time was allowed, and the reviewer decided within it, so the deemed affirmation was taken never to have applied (s54D(6)). The cost was an application to the regulator, weeks of delay for the applicant and two rushed pieces of work. All of it came from one person being the only link between the register and the work.

The control is the design described above: an owner and a backup for every case, alerts to both and to the team mailbox, a weekly check by a rotating pair, and an absence rule under which the backup takes over the same morning. Internal reviewers now receive the due date in writing and their own alerts.

## Exercise: the FTE need and a deadline calendar

This is an illustration.

**Part 1.** Ruth replaces the planning assumptions with hours measured in the register over the previous year. Own-information requests took 12 hours on average, other FOI requests 35, privacy enquiries and complaints 10, and breach assessments 45. Volumes and the 940 non-case hours are unchanged. Work out the FTE need and the spare hours with four people. NRIO plans to keep the team at or below 85 per cent loaded, the line Chapter 3 used. How much work must come out, or how much capacity must be added, to get there?

**Part 2.** Using the rule NRIO adopted after October, build the deadline calendar the team should have had on Monday 9 August 2027, when the journalist's internal review application arrived, to mid-September. Give each date, its alerts, an owner and a backup, and say which week is most exposed.

### Worked answer

**Part 1.**

- Casework: 60 × 12 = 720; 80 × 35 = 2,800; 25 × 10 = 250; 5 × 45 = 225. Total 720 + 2,800 + 250 + 225 = 3,995 hours, an average of 3,995 ÷ 170 = 23.5 hours, or 1,410 minutes per case.
- In Staffing Need: 170 units, 1,410 minutes, 1,950 hours, 70 per cent. The result is 2.93 FTE (3,995 ÷ 1,365 = 2.9267) and 3 people, and the tool warns: rounding up leaves 0.07 FTE, under 0.15.
- With non-case work: 3,995 + 940 = 4,935 hours; 4,935 ÷ 1,365 = 3.6154, so 3.62 FTE and still 4 people.
- Spare: 5,460 − 4,935 = 525 hours, down from 950. Loading: 4,935 ÷ 5,460 = 90.4 per cent, above NRIO's 85 per cent line.
- To reach 85 per cent: 0.85 × 5,460 = 4,641 hours, so 4,935 − 4,641 = 294 hours must come out. Alternatively, capacity must rise to 4,935 ÷ 0.85 = 5,805.9 hours, adding 345.9 hours, or 0.25 FTE (345.9 ÷ 1,365 = 0.2534).

The team still needs four people, but its buffer has almost halved. Over eight weeks, 525 spare hours a year is about 81 hours (525 × 8 ÷ 52 = 80.8), so one breach like June's, about 80 hours (Chapter 3), would use all of it. Hiring is not the first answer. If templates and better filing save 4 hours on each of the 80 other requests, that removes 80 × 4 = 320 hours, more than the 294 needed, and loading falls to 4,615 ÷ 5,460 = 84.5 per cent. Ruth should also ask the agency head to authorise a third decision-maker: two are a capacity limit the FTE figure does not show. A short-term 0.25 FTE for peaks is the fallback if the savings do not appear.

**Part 2.** Alerts fall five and two working days before each date. For example, from Wednesday 1 September, five working days back is Wednesday 25 August and two is Monday 30 August. No public holiday falls in the period.

| Item | Date | Alerts | Owner | Backup |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First log entry | Wed 11 Aug | Wed 4 Aug, Mon 9 Aug | Ethan | Priya |
| Held information release: not before the third parties' period ends on Fri 27 Aug; NRIO's date (Chapter 5) | Wed 1 Sep | Wed 25 Aug, Mon 30 Aug | Priya | Liam |
| Secretary's APP 12 request: 30 days after Mon 2 Aug (Chapter 7) | Wed 1 Sep | Wed 25 Aug, Mon 30 Aug | Ethan; Ruth decides | Priya |
| Internal review decision: 30 days after Mon 9 Aug | Wed 8 Sep | Wed 1 Sep, Mon 6 Sep | Ruth, with the EL2 reviewer | Ethan |
| Second log entry, if release is on 1 Sep | Wed 15 Sep | Wed 8 Sep, Mon 13 Sep | Ethan | Priya |

The first entry was published on Wednesday 4 August (Chapter 9), so it closes. Ethan, not Liam or Priya, backs up the internal review, because both worked on the original decision.

The most exposed week is Monday 30 August to Friday 3 September. It holds two alerts on Monday, two items due on Wednesday 1 September, and the internal review's five-day alert on the same day. Ruth decides one of the three live items and coordinates another, and Ethan owns one and backs up another. Two moves spread the load. Set an internal target of Friday 27 August for the secretary's response, so Wednesday holds only the release. And confirm every backup in writing before that week, not during it.

This book began with the laws that give people access to government information, protect their privacy and keep the record. It has followed one small team through a request, a breach, a personal request and a release: finding the records, counting the days, weighing exemptions and the public interest, consulting the people affected, writing reasons that survive review, handling personal information, assessing a breach and publishing. Each lesson rests on the others: a sound exemption is no use if the records cannot be found, and a good decision helps nobody after its deadline. The laws supply the tests and the time limits. Meeting them, request after request, is the work of a team that writes things down, checks each other's work and shares its deadlines.
