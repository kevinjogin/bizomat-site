A Budget decision turns advice into promises: so many bridges, by these dates, for this much money. Someone now has to keep them. Advice that reads well and then fails in delivery has not landed, however good the analysis behind it.

This is an illustration. On Tue 9 May 2028 the government agreed that NRIO would assess 800 council bridges over 2028–29 and 2029–30, for A$10.0 million over four years (Chapter 6). The program starts on Mon 3 Jul 2028, and the first assessments begin on Mon 2 Oct 2028. NRIO has never run a service like this, and the options paper named that as Option C's main risk (Chapter 3). Grace Mitchell's team had just under eight weeks to turn the proposal's delivery section into a plan people could work from. This chapter follows that plan, from governance and milestones to measures, briefing and risk.

## Design for delivery from the first brief

Implementation does not start when the policy work ends. Most programs that fail in delivery were set up to fail: a date fixed by an announcement, a capability assumed, a cost that left out the people who run it.

Section 17(f) of the [Australian Public Service Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) (the Directions) says that upholding the APS Value of Impartial requires "providing advice that takes account of the context in which policy needs to be implemented". It is a requirement, applied having regard to each person's duties and responsibilities. For a policy officer, it means every option you advise on carries honest answers to five questions:

- Who will do the work, and do they exist yet?
- What must be built, bought or hired, and how long does each take?
- Who else must act: councils, firms, other agencies?
- What will it cost to run, including after the funding ends?
- How will anyone know whether it is working?

Ask the people who would do the work, not the people who would like it done. If another area will deliver, let it check the dates before the proposal is lodged.

This is an illustration. Grace's team asked these questions from the options paper on. Tom asked three engineering firms what an assessment would cost and how many their engineers could do in a year, and the answers shaped both the A$9,500 rate and the phasing (Chapter 5). NRIO's procurement area said a panel would take about three months, which is why the costing allowed only nine months of assessments in 2028–29. Before the Budget, the team drafted the bridge data standard: the kind of work Chapter 6 allows before a decision, cheap and useful whatever ministers decided.

## The plan, and who decides what

An implementation plan says what will be done, by whom, by when and with what money, and who decides when things change. Keep it to a few pages, so the people delivering it will read it.

Governance answers one question: who can decide what. Write it down before the first problem arrives, and set tolerances: how far a result may move from the plan before a decision goes up a level.

This is an illustration. NRIO's CEO, its accountable authority, approved the plan on Fri 16 Jun 2028. Its governance has four parts:

- **The program board** meets monthly, chaired by the Director, Strategy and Policy, as senior responsible officer, with Grace, the program manager, Zoe Fraser and a procurement adviser. It may approve changes that keep each year's assessments and costs within 10 per cent of the plan and leave the review date alone. Anything larger goes to the CEO.
- **The program team** runs the panel, the nominations, the data checks and the register. With the CFO area's agreement, recruitment began after the Budget for start dates from Mon 3 Jul, when the money would be there.
- **Grace's team** remains the policy owner. It writes the measures, drafts the Minister's briefs on the program and designs the review in Chapter 10.
- **A council reference group** of councils and their associations meets each quarter to advise on the nominations guidance and the data standard. It decides nothing, and its terms of reference say so.

## Milestones that work back from the first assessment

Set milestones from the work, not the announcement. Start with the date that matters most and work backwards, asking what must already be true at each step. Give each step an owner, and mark the critical path: the steps whose delay delays everything after them.

This is an illustration. The fixed point was Mon 2 Oct 2028. For assessments to start that day, three things had to be in place: a panel under contract, confirmed nominations and a data standard to assess to. The register could come later, as long as checked reports had somewhere safe to wait.

**The panel.** It is a procurement, not a grant (Chapter 3), so the Commonwealth Procurement Rules and NRIO's procurement instructions govern how it is bought, and the procurement area applies them. The policy team involves that area before dates are promised, and promises firms and councils nothing outside the process. The approach to market sought firms for 800 assessments, with a way to extend if a further tranche were agreed, and promised none (Chapter 6).

**The nominations.** Amara Osei drafted the nominations guidance after the team met on Wed 10 May 2028. With 800 places, it ranks bridges in a published order: first, bridges with a precautionary limit or closure on a freight or access route; then bridges with no load assessment in the last 10 years. Each nomination records the bridge's current limit, any closure, its signed detour and the date of its last assessment. Those fields give the evaluation a starting point for every nominated bridge (Chapter 10).

## Performance measures for the corporate plan

Section 35(1) of the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) requires the accountable authority to prepare a corporate plan at least once each reporting period, and section 16E of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) sets its content. The plan must cover at least four reporting periods (s 16E(1)). For each, it must say how performance will be measured and assessed, through specified measures that meet section 16EA and "specified targets for each of those performance measures for which it is reasonably practicable to set a target" (s 16E(2), item 5). It must be published on the entity's website by the last day of the second month of the reporting period (s 16E(3)): 31 August, for a year that starts on 1 July.

Section 16EA sets six tests. Measures meet it if, in the context of the entity's purposes or key activities, they relate directly to them, use sources and methods that are "reliable and verifiable", give an unbiased basis for assessment, and allow assessment over time. Where reasonably practicable, they mix qualitative and quantitative measures. They include measures of outputs, efficiency and effectiveness "if those things are appropriate measures of the entity's performance".

Put in the corporate plan only the few measures that show whether the activity works, and keep the rest for the program board.

This is an illustration. NRIO's 2028–29 corporate plan, due by Thu 31 Aug 2028, adds bridge load assessment as a key activity. Grace's team drafted four measures, and NRIO's performance area checked them against section 16EA. The panel's prices were not yet known, so the cost target came from the costing.

Two choices need their reasons on file. First, there is no target for limits lifted. A target would push engineers and councils towards one answer, and the objective is limits that are right, not limits that are gone (Chapter 1). Reporting every outcome together gives the unbiased basis section 16EA asks for, and section 16E asks for targets only where one is reasonably practicable. Paying firms the same for every assessment, whatever it finds, protects the same independence.

Second, the mean detour avoided is missing: it is costly to measure every year, so it belongs to the evaluation (Chapter 10). The limits measure continues in 2030–31 and 2031–32, after the assessments end, because councils keep acting on results.

## Keeping the Minister informed

Section 19(1) of the PGPA Act lists five things the accountable authority "must" do. It must keep the responsible Minister informed of the entity's activities, and give the Minister or the Finance Minister the reports and information they require. It must notify the responsible Minister as soon as practicable after making a significant decision. It must give "reasonable notice" of a significant issue that may affect the entity, and notify as soon as practicable after becoming aware of one that has affected it. Section 19(3) allows the rules to prescribe matters for deciding what is significant, so ask your governance area what applies.

The duty is the accountable authority's, not yours, but it can be met only with what officials report. So the plan should say what counts as significant for this program, who decides and how fast it moves. Decide in advance: a team deciding in the middle of a problem tends to answer "not yet".

This is an illustration. The plan sets a rhythm and a trigger. During procurement, the Minister's office receives a one-page update each month; after that, a progress brief each quarter, cleared by the CEO. Outside that rhythm, the board treats four things as significant and puts each to the CEO within two working days, with a draft brief:

- a delay that would move the first assessments past Mon 2 Oct 2028, or miss either year's target by more than 10 per cent;
- an average cost per assessment more than 10 per cent above A$9,500, that is, above A$10,450;
- an assessment that finds an open bridge unsafe, once the council that owns it has been told;
- a dispute with a council or a panel firm that is likely to become public.

The Minister's office should never learn of a problem from a journalist.

## Rating the risks that could stop delivery

Rate a plan's risks against your agency's framework and its written descriptors, so that two people rating the same risk get the same answer.

The [Risk Rating (5 × 5 matrix)](/tools/risk-score/) tool does the arithmetic. It multiplies likelihood (1 to 5) by consequence (1 to 5) and bands the score: Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14 and High 15 to 25. It suggests a response for each band: monitor a Low risk, mitigate a Medium one and give it an owner, and treat a High one before relying on the plan, and escalate it. The bands are the tool's defaults; your agency's framework decides.

This is an illustration. The program's register uses NRIO's 5 × 5 matrix. Likelihood runs from 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain); 3 is possible, and 4 is likely, meaning expected at some point during the program. The plan words the consequences for the program:

- 2, minor: a delay of weeks that the plan absorbs;
- 3, moderate: a milestone slips by up to three months, or costs rise by up to 5 per cent;
- 4, major: a year's target missed by more than 10 per cent, costs up by more than 10 per cent, or a person seriously hurt;
- 5, severe: a death, or a program that cannot deliver its purpose.

Take the risk that the register is not ready when the first reports arrive. Its build starts in July, and reports start arriving in November. Likelihood 3, possible. Consequence 3, moderate: for up to three months, councils cannot see results and checks pile up. The score is 3 × 3 = 9, Medium. The treatment is to build the parts that load and check reports first, hold accepted reports in a checked interim store, and send each council its own results directly. Re-rated, likelihood stays at 3, but consequence falls to 2, a delay of weeks to the shared view: 3 × 2 = 6, Low. The treatment worked on the consequence, and took the register off the critical path.

The score sorts risks for attention; it does not measure them. It also hides its parts: 2 × 5 and 5 × 2 both make 10, but one is a rare disaster and the other a frequent nuisance, so record both factors.

## When it goes wrong: a help desk announced before it existed

This is an illustration. On Tue 15 Apr 2025, before the bridge work began, a media release announced that NRIO would open a drainage design help desk for small councils from 1 July, with A$2.4 million over three years, or A$0.8 million a year. The date came from the announcement, not a delivery plan. NRIO had no engineers on staff, no contract to supply them and no system to log questions.

A contract for engineering advice took the procurement area four months. The help desk opened on Mon 3 Nov 2025, 125 days after Tue 1 Jul. Meanwhile 70 councils had sent questions to a general inbox, where some waited more than three months. Of the first year's A$0.8 million, A$0.35 million went unspent, which is 0.35 ÷ 0.80 = 43.75 per cent, and NRIO had to ask to move it into later years. The Minister's office spent months answering councils' letters.

Three controls would have prevented it:

1. A delivery check before any date is announced: who, with what, by when, answered by the delivery and procurement areas.
2. Dates taken from the critical path, with the earliest credible date stated plainly in the advice, as section 17(f) of the Directions asks.
3. If the announcement must come first, announce what is certain, the funding and the intent, and give the start date once the capability exists.

## Exercise: rate four implementation risks

This is an illustration. It is Tue 13 Jun 2028, and the program board must agree the plan's risk ratings before the CEO signs on Friday. Use NRIO's descriptors above and the Risk Rating tool's arithmetic.

1. **The panel is not in place by Fri 29 Sep 2028.** NRIO has never procured an engineering panel, and three months leaves no slack. The first assessments would slip by up to three months.
2. **Panel firms cannot field enough bridge engineers** for 300 assessments in 2028–29 and 500 in 2029–30. The survey and the councils' roundtable both found engineers scarce (Chapter 2). A shortfall would miss a year's target by more than 10 per cent.
3. **Councils nominate late, or off the priorities.** Small councils have few staff, but respondents alone reported 410 limited bridges and 1,540 unassessed in 10 years, so candidates are plentiful. A shortfall would delay confirming some of the first 300 by weeks.
4. **An assessment finds an open bridge unsafe, and it is not acted on quickly.** The council owns the bridge and decides; NRIO cannot close it. If a heavy vehicle crossed a failing bridge, people could die.

Rate and band each risk, and rank them. Propose a treatment for the highest and re-rate it, and say what the Minister should hear now.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

| Risk | Likelihood | Consequence | Score | Band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Panel not in place | 3, possible | 3, moderate | 3 × 3 = 9 | Medium |
| 2 Not enough engineers | 4, likely | 4, major | 4 × 4 = 16 | High |
| 3 Nominations late or off-priority | 3, possible | 2, minor | 3 × 2 = 6 | Low |
| 4 Unsafe bridge not acted on | 2, unlikely | 5, severe | 2 × 5 = 10 | Medium |

The reasons matter more than the numbers. Risk 2 rests on the program's own evidence: engineers are scarce, and the program adds demand. For risk 4, councils have every reason to act on an unsafe finding, so slow action is unlikely, but its consequence is a death.

By score the ranking is 2 (16), 4 (10), 1 (9) and 3 (6). Do not treat risk 4 as minor because it scores well below risk 2. Its consequence is 5, and the tool warns that severe consequences need controls even at low likelihood. The control is in the plan: the panel contract requires the engineer to tell the council and NRIO the same day an unsafe condition is found, NRIO confirms in writing within two working days, and the register records what the council did.

Risk 2 is High, so it is treated before the plan relies on it, and escalated to the CEO with the plan:

- appoint several firms to the panel, so that no single firm's capacity limits the program;
- ask each firm to state, by quarter, the engineers it will commit;
- schedule assessments in regional batches, so engineers spend their days assessing rather than driving;
- set a trigger. Three hundred assessments over the nine months from October to June means 200 by the end of March. If fewer than 160 are done by Fri 30 Mar 2029, which is 80 per cent of 200, the board reports to the CEO, and the Minister's office gets reasonable notice that the year's target is at risk.

Re-rated, likelihood falls to 3 and consequence stays at 4, because a shortfall would still miss a target: 3 × 4 = 12, Medium. The treatment makes the risk less likely and visible earlier, not harmless.

The Minister should hear now, in the first progress brief, which two risks matter most, 2 and 4, and how each is treated. That is part of keeping the Minister informed of NRIO's activities, as section 19(1)(a) requires of the accountable authority. Neither is yet a significant issue; the trigger is what would turn risk 2 into one.
