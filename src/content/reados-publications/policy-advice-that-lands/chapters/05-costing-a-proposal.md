Impact analysis asks whether an option is worth doing. A costing asks a narrower question: what will it cost the government, in which year, and for what? A benefit–cost ratio can be right while the costing behind a Budget proposal is wrong, and ministers decide on the costing.

This is an illustration. On Thu 11 Nov 2027 the Minister agreed that NRIO should develop Option C, the central assessment service, as a new policy proposal for the 2028–29 Budget. Chapter 4 treated C's A$14.5 million as one sum spent at the start, a fair simplification for comparing options. A Budget proposal needs to show what the money buys, what each part costs, when it will be spent and what has been assumed. Grace Mitchell had about four weeks. Tom Becker had the evidence, and Zoe Fraser, the Finance Business Partner in the CFO area, had the method. The costing was final on Fri 10 Dec 2027.

In the case, tables are in A$ million and every costing amount excludes GST, which is how NRIO's CFO area builds costings. Ask yours which convention it uses, and keep to one.

## Build the cost from its drivers

A cost driver is the quantity that makes a cost go up or down: bridges assessed, staff employed, systems built. A costing built from drivers can be checked, explained and scaled. A round number cannot.

Write each cost as a quantity multiplied by a unit rate, and record where both come from. The quantity comes from the policy design. The rate comes from evidence: market prices, recent invoices, existing contracts or the agency's own costs for similar work. Say what the rate includes, such as travel to remote bridges.

This is an illustration. Option C's main driver is the number of priority bridges assessed: 1,200 over three years. Tom set the unit rate at A$9,500, the average of indicative rates from three engineering firms and councils' recent invoices. It sits towards the low end of the A$8,000 to A$15,000 that councils reported in the survey, because a panel buying 1,200 assessments to one data standard should pay less than a council buying one or two. That is a judgement, not a fact, and the costing says so.


The quantity has drivers of its own: how many bridges councils nominate and how fast the panel can work. The survey named the shortage of bridge engineers as one reason councils do not assess, so a costing that ignored capacity would contradict the proposal's own evidence.

## Separate program costs from running costs

Program costs are the money the policy exists to spend: the payments, grants or services that reach the people it is for. Running costs are what the agency spends to deliver it: staff, systems, evaluation and the like. Budget practice calls the first administered and the second departmental. Appropriation Acts use the same terms for their items. Agencies usually estimate, fund and report the two separately, and your CFO area decides which side of the line each item falls on.

The split shows how much of the money reaches the problem, and exposes the costs most often left out: nobody at the policy stage thinks about who will answer the phone in year three.

This is an illustration. In Option C the program cost is the assessments: A$11.40 million, paid to the panel firms for work done for councils. The running cost is A$3.10 million over four years.

And the A$0.25 million for evaluation is there because evaluation was planned at design time, as Chapter 10 explains.

## Phase the cost across the Budget year and forward years

The [Charter of Budget Honesty](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05333/latest/text) defines the budget year as the financial year to which a budget relates (Sch 1 cl 3). The government's fiscal strategy statement specifies objectives and targets "for the budget year and the following 3 financial years" (cl 9(1)(d)), and the budget report's fiscal estimates cover the same four years (cl 12(1)(a)). The Charter does not govern your proposal, but it explains why a costing covers four years. The three after the budget year are the forward years.

Phasing puts each cost in the year it will be incurred. Phase from the delivery plan, not from the total divided by four. Money in the wrong year is an error even when the total is right: the program is short when it needs the money and holds money it cannot use. Do not assume that money unspent in one year moves to the next; ask your CFO area how your agency handles it.

This is an illustration. Grace's delivery plan assumed that the program would start on Mon 3 Jul 2028, that the panel would be procured between July and September, and that the first assessments would begin on Mon 2 Oct 2028. That leaves nine months of assessment in 2028–29, so the costing allows 300 bridges in that year. The panel works at full pace in 2029–30, with 500, and finishes the last 400 in 2030–31. The final year carries only evaluation and upkeep.

| A$ million | 2028–29 | 2029–30 | 2030–31 | 2031–32 | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Program (administered): assessments at A$9,500 each | 2.85 (300 bridges) | 4.75 (500) | 3.80 (400) | 0 | 11.40 |
| Running (departmental): staff, data register, evaluation | 1.25 | 0.75 | 0.70 | 0.40 | 3.10 |
| **Total** | 4.10 | 5.50 | 4.50 | 0.40 | **14.50** |

## Write down the assumptions and the risks

An assumption is something you have decided to treat as true for the costing. A risk is something that might happen and change the cost, the timing or what the money buys. Both belong in the proposal, because ministers decide on them as much as on the totals.

This is an illustration. The main assumptions in Option C's costing were these.

| Assumption | Basis | If it is wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A$9,500 per assessment, held flat for three years | Three firms' indicative rates and councils' invoices | Each A$1,000 on the rate moves the cost by 1,200 × A$1,000 = A$1.20 million |
| 300, 500 and 400 assessments a year | The delivery plan; panel capacity to be tested at procurement | Slower work moves money into later years |
| Councils nominate 1,200 suitable bridges | Survey respondents alone reported 1,540 with no load assessment in 10 years | Fewer assessments and smaller benefits |
| Register build A$0.60 million, then upkeep | NRIO's own estimate | Any overrun falls on NRIO's departmental budget |

Test the assumption that matters most. The rate is the largest driver, and councils reported costs from A$8,000 to A$15,000. At 10 per cent above the assumed rate, A$10,450, the 1,200 assessments would cost 1,200 × A$10,450 = A$12.54 million, A$1.14 million more. If the money is fixed instead, A$11,400,000 ÷ A$10,450 = 1,090 assessments, rounded down: 110 fewer than planned.

So a proposal should say how a cost risk would be absorbed: by more money, or by less work within the same money. For Option C, built from units, it would mean fewer bridges.

Do not hide risk inside the rate. A padded rate looks like evidence and is not. Whether a proposal may carry a contingency, and how one is shown, is a question for Finance's costing guidance and your CFO area.

## Work with the CFO area from the first day

A costing is a joint product. The policy team owns the design and the assumptions. The CFO area owns the method: staff rates, the split between administered and departmental, phasing conventions, fit with the agency's existing estimates, and the government's Budget rules.

This is an illustration. Grace put Zoe on the costing the day after the Minister's agreement. They agreed on one costing file, versioned and dated in NRIO's records system, with Zoe as its only editor. Tom would give Zoe the evidence for each driver in writing. Every design change would go through Grace. And the costing would be final by Fri 10 Dec 2027, so that the Cabinet material could be finished around it.

Give your CFO area the design, drivers, sources, delivery plan and assumptions; ask it for staff rates, classifications, overlapping funding and this year's Budget rules. Then keep the numbers in one place: a figure changed in a Cabinet draft but not in the costing starts two versions of the truth.

## Budget estimates under the PGPA Act

Section 36 of the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) requires the accountable authority of every Commonwealth entity to prepare budget estimates covering the entity's activities for each reporting period, and to give them to the Finance Secretary in line with any directions. The estimates must "fairly present the estimated financial impacts of the entity's activities" (s 36(2)(a)). The Finance Secretary may give written directions (s 36(3)), and they are not legislative instruments (s 36(4)), so they are not on the Federal Register of Legislation. Your CFO area works to them.

A proposal's costing is not the entity's estimates, but once a proposal is agreed its money becomes part of them. An optimistic costing moves into the estimates, then into a branch budget, then into variance reports someone else must explain. The companion book *Public Money You Can Account For* shows that path from the business manager's side.

## Agreeing the costing with Finance

In practice, a Budget proposal's costing is usually checked with the Department of Finance before ministers consider it, so that they decide on numbers the central agencies accept. How, by whom and by when are set by the government's Budget rules and Finance's guidance, which can change between Budgets. Read the current *Budget Process Operational Rules* and Finance's costing guidance through your CFO area.

Expect questions about every driver, and answer from the evidence file. If Finance's view of a number differs from yours, record both views and the reasons and tell your manager; do not blend them into a third number nobody can explain.

This is an illustration. Zoe and the CFO area finished Option C's costing on Fri 10 Dec 2027, ready for those steps. From then on, any change to the design would reopen it.

## What the Parliamentary Budget Office does and does not do

The Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) is not part of the Budget process for your proposal. Its purpose, under section 64B of the [Parliamentary Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00536/latest/text), is to inform the Parliament by providing "independent and non-partisan analysis of the budget cycle, fiscal policy and the financial implications of proposals".

Section 64E sets its functions. The PBO costs policies on request by Senators and Members outside the caretaker period for a general election, and by authorised members of parliamentary parties and independent members during it (s 64E(1)(a), (b)). It also answers parliamentarians' budget questions, makes submissions to committees, reports on election commitments and does its own research (s 64E(1)(c) to (e)).

Two limits matter to an agency officer. The PBO's functions do not include "preparing economic forecasts" or "preparing budget estimates (whether at the whole-of-government, agency or program level)" (s 64E(2)). And it must use the forecasts, parameters and fiscal estimates in the most recent reports released under the Charter (s 64E(3)). So the PBO is not where your agency's estimates or its Budget costings are prepared.

What it may do is cost a parliamentarian's version of a policy like yours. This is an illustration. Outside an election, a Senator could ask it to cost a A$120 million bridge strengthening fund much like Option D. The PBO may make written arrangements with agency heads to obtain information (s 64F(1)). If a request reaches NRIO, it is answered through NRIO's own channels and any such arrangement: accurately, without commentary, and never with Cabinet material. Chapter 6 returns to costings during an election.

## When it goes wrong: the cameras nobody ran

This is an illustration. In 2024, another NRIO team proposed flood-depth cameras at 60 low-level causeways, so drivers could check water levels before setting out. The costing showed 60 cameras at A$50,000 each to buy and install, 60 × A$50,000 = A$3.0 million, and nothing else. The proposal was agreed as costed.

The cameras worked. Running them did not. Each needed a data service and maintenance, the images needed a platform, and someone had to manage faults. That came to about A$0.40 million a year: A$0.25 million of staff, A$0.10 million of data and communications and A$0.05 million of maintenance. Four years of it, 4 × A$0.40 million = A$1.60 million, was 1.60 ÷ 3.00 = 53 per cent of what the proposal had asked for.

There was no money for any of it. The team absorbed what it could from its departmental budget and delayed other work. Failed cameras stayed offline for weeks, councils stopped relying on the network, and the A$3.0 million bought far less than promised.

The missing control was simple: a review that asks, line by line, who will run this, on what systems, with what maintenance, how it will be evaluated and what it will cost after the funding ends. Option C's departmental line, A$3.10 million or about 21 per cent of A$14.50 million, is what asking them looks like.

## Exercise: cost the 800-bridge version

This is an illustration. Suppose Grace is asked how the costing would look for a smaller first tranche: 800 bridges over 2028–29 and 2029–30 (300 and 500), with the remaining 400 left until a review. Keep the rate at A$9,500. Use these departmental estimates:

- 2028–29: A$1.25 million (staff 0.65 and data register build 0.60), unchanged;
- 2029–30: A$0.75 million (staff 0.65 and upkeep 0.10), unchanged;
- 2030–31: A$0.30 million (a smaller close-out team 0.20 and upkeep 0.10);
- 2031–32: A$0.10 million (upkeep 0.10), because the register holds 800 bridges rather than 1,200.

Prepare the costing table. Work out the saving against the 1,200-bridge version, split into program and running costs. Compare the cost per bridge in the two versions and explain the difference. Name one thing that has dropped out that Grace must raise.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. The program cost follows the bridges: 300 × A$9,500 = A$2.85 million and 500 × A$9,500 = A$4.75 million, so 2.85 + 4.75 = A$7.60 million. The running cost is 1.25 + 0.75 + 0.30 + 0.10 = A$2.40 million.

| A$ million | 2028–29 | 2029–30 | 2030–31 | 2031–32 | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Program (administered) | 2.85 (300 bridges) | 4.75 (500) | 0 | 0 | 7.60 |
| Running (departmental) | 1.25 | 0.75 | 0.30 | 0.10 | 2.40 |
| **Total** | 4.10 | 5.50 | 0.30 | 0.10 | **10.00** |

Both ways agree: 7.60 + 2.40 = 10.00, and 4.10 + 5.50 + 0.30 + 0.10 = 10.00.

The saving is 14.50 − 10.00 = A$4.50 million. Of that, 11.40 − 7.60 = A$3.80 million is program cost, exactly the 400 bridges not assessed (400 × A$9,500). The other 3.10 − 2.40 = A$0.70 million is running cost: A$0.40 million less staff in 2030–31 (0.60 down to 0.20) and A$0.30 million less in 2031–32 (0.25 of evaluation and 0.15 of upkeep, down to 0.10 of upkeep). The first two years are identical.

The version assesses 400 ÷ 1,200 = 33.3 per cent fewer bridges but costs only 4.50 ÷ 14.50 = 31.0 per cent less. The cost per bridge rises from A$14,500,000 ÷ 1,200 = A$12,083 (rounded) to A$10,000,000 ÷ 800 = A$12,500. The reason is fixed costs: the register build and the first two years of staff cost the same whatever the number of bridges, and are now spread over 800 instead of 1,200. Say so before anyone mistakes it for waste.

What has dropped out is the A$0.25 million final evaluation. The only money in 2031–32 is upkeep, so the program would have no funded final evaluation, and the review before any further tranche would carry the evidence alone. Grace should put that in writing, so that ministers decide it knowingly. It is the cameras failure in a smaller form: a running cost that disappears from a costing does not disappear from the program.
