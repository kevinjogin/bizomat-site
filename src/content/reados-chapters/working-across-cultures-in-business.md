An Australian business sends a revised drawing to an overseas factory and asks whether the new tolerance is achievable. The reply is prompt and positive: yes, no problem. Six weeks later, the first shipment arrives with a large share of parts out of tolerance. The factory had doubts all along, but saying so directly to a customer felt impolite, and the question had been asked in a way that invited a yes. Both sides followed the process. They simply read it differently.

Small businesses work across cultures more than they often realise: importing components, exporting products, engaging overseas designers or developers, serving customers from many backgrounds and employing people who grew up with different workplace norms. Most of the time, it works well. But when things go wrong, the cause is often not language or technology. It is a difference in how people interpret authority, disagreement, uncertainty, relationships, time and commitment, a difference nobody noticed because everyone assumed their own way of working was normal.

This article explains how cultural differences show up in everyday business delivery, how to use cultural frameworks without stereotyping, what to keep consistent and what to adapt, and the practical habits that prevent the most common misunderstandings. It is general information for owners and managers who work with people from other countries and backgrounds.

## Culture is a delivery issue, not etiquette

Etiquette, such as how to greet people or exchange business cards, matters, but it is not where the real risk sits. The risk sits in how people interpret the working arrangements that hold delivery together:

- **Authority**: whether people feel able to question a manager, a customer or a specification.
- **Disagreement**: whether it is expressed openly, indirectly or not at all.
- **Uncertainty**: how much ambiguity people tolerate before asking for direction, and how early they raise problems.
- **Relationships**: whether trust must be built before business commitments, or follows from them.
- **Time and commitment**: what a deadline means, and whether "yes" means "I agree", "I heard you" or "I will try".

The same formal process can produce different behaviour in different settings. A risk register may exist in every location of a business, yet in one team people raise emerging problems early, while in another they wait until they are certain, because raising an unresolved problem feels like admitting failure. The process is identical; the quality of information is not.

## Use frameworks to ask questions, not to predict people

Researchers have developed several frameworks that describe how national cultures tend to differ. Geert Hofstede's cultural dimensions, for example, include power distance (how far people accept unequal distribution of power), individualism versus collectivism, and uncertainty avoidance (comfort with ambiguity and risk). Other models distinguish **low-context** communication, where meaning is carried mainly by explicit words, from **high-context** communication, where much of the meaning lies in the relationship, the setting and what is left unsaid.

These frameworks are useful prompts. They are dangerous as predictions. They describe tendencies across large populations, and individuals vary enormously within any country. An engineer trained abroad, a second-generation Australian or a manager in a multinational may work quite differently from any national average. Organisational culture, professional culture and personal style matter as much as nationality.

The safer use is to turn frameworks into questions about the specific people you work with:

- How comfortable are they challenging a customer or a senior person?
- How do they normally express disagreement or doubt?
- How early do they raise problems, and through whom?
- Do they expect a relationship before making commitments?
- How do they interpret deadlines, and what does a promised date mean?
- What does participation in a meeting look like to them?

The answers come from observation, conversation and experience, not from a country profile.

## Your own habits are not neutral

Every working style carries cultural assumptions, including yours. Workplace habits common in Australia, such as informality, first names with senior people, understatement, dry humour, irony and a preference for flat hierarchy, are normal to many Australians and can be confusing elsewhere. A casual "no worries, whenever suits" may be read literally. Irony may be taken at face value. An informal manager may seem uncertain about their own authority to someone used to clearer hierarchy.

The most useful starting point is curiosity about your own defaults. Ask, observe, test and adjust, rather than assuming your way is the standard others are failing to meet.

## Standardise the essentials, adapt the rest

Working across cultures does not mean abandoning standards. It means being clear about which things must be the same everywhere, and which can flex.

| Keep consistent | Adapt as needed |
|---|---|
| Safety requirements | Communication style and directness |
| Ethical standards and anti-bribery rules | Meeting design and participation methods |
| Legal and regulatory obligations | How relationships are built |
| Product specifications and acceptance criteria | How feedback and recognition are given |
| Decision rights and escalation thresholds | How consultation happens before decisions |
| Records and evidence required | Channels and timing of communication |

Being explicit about this separation avoids two opposite mistakes: imposing your own habits everywhere, and relaxing essential standards in the name of cultural sensitivity.

## Practical habits that prevent misunderstanding

### Ask questions that cannot be answered with a reflexive yes

"Do you understand?" and "Can you do this?" invite a polite yes. Better questions ask people to explain, describe or estimate:

- "How would you inspect this dimension?"
- "What would make this tolerance difficult to hold?"
- "What is the earliest and latest date this could ship?"
- "What could stop this going to plan?"

Asking people to summarise back what has been agreed, in their own words, quickly shows whether understanding is shared.

### Make bad news easy to deliver

In many settings, telling a customer or a senior person about a problem feels risky. Make it explicitly expected and safe. Ask for risks as a standing agenda item. Thank people for early warnings. Offer private channels as well as group meetings. Separate the person from the problem. Where a supplier's sales contact always says yes, build a working relationship with the people who do the work, such as the production or quality manager.

### Write it down, show it, and agree what done means

Written specifications, annotated drawings, photographs, physical samples and agreed acceptance criteria reduce reliance on interpretation. For manufactured goods, a **golden sample**, an approved reference item kept by both parties, makes quality expectations concrete. Define what "done", "approved" and "shipped" mean, because these words carry different expectations in different settings.

### Use plain, unambiguous language

Avoid idioms, slang, sports metaphors, sarcasm and abbreviations that readers outside your own culture may not know. Write dates unambiguously, such as 3 April 2027 rather than 03/04/27, which reads as a different date in some countries. State units, currencies and time zones explicitly. Keep sentences short in important messages. For safety-critical or contractual communication with people working in a second language, consider professional translation or interpreting.

### Allow for different ways of participating

In multicultural teams, open discussions tend to be dominated by confident speakers working in their first language. Send material before meetings, invite written input, ask each person for their view in turn, allow time for responses, and follow up individually. Recognition can also be received differently: some people value public praise, while others find being singled out uncomfortable. Ask the person rather than assuming.

### Plan around time zones and holidays

Map overlapping working hours and agree response times. Keep a calendar of major holidays in each partner's country, such as Lunar New Year, when many factories close for an extended period and production slows before and after. Build these into schedules from the start, rather than discovering them when an order is late.

### Build relationships deliberately

In many business cultures, trust and personal relationships come before significant commitments. Regular video calls, visits where practical and time for conversation beyond the immediate task are investments, not distractions. Meeting the people who make your products also tells you far more about a supplier than any document.

## Ethics and law travel with you

Hospitality and gift-giving customs vary, but Australian law follows Australian businesses overseas. Foreign bribery is a serious offence under Australian law, including for conduct abroad. Keep any hospitality modest, transparent and recorded, and take advice if you are unsure. Other obligations also apply across borders, including export controls on certain goods and technology, sanctions, product safety and consumer law, and privacy. Cultural flexibility never extends to these. The [exporting for small manufacturers](/reados/collections/manufacturing-operations-and-design-for-manufacture/exporting-for-small-manufacturers/) article covers practical export requirements and support.

## Culture inside your own workplace

Many Australian workplaces bring together people from many backgrounds. The same principles apply internally. Be explicit about expectations that newcomers might not share, such as whether staff are expected to raise concerns with their manager, how to ask for leave or how to disagree in a meeting. Check understanding of safety information by observing people doing the task correctly, not by asking whether they understood. Work health and safety duties include providing information, training and instruction in a form workers can understand, and anti-discrimination laws protect workers regardless of background. The Fair Work Ombudsman publishes information in many languages.

## Do not make everything about culture

Overcorrection is also a risk. Unclear specifications, weak competence, poor incentives and badly defined roles cause problems in any team, and calling them cultural differences hides the real cause. Before attributing a problem to culture, check whether the basics were in place: a clear requirement, a capable person, the right information and a sensible process.

## A calibration cycle

Treat cross-cultural working as something to learn, not something to get right once:

1. **Form hypotheses**: before starting with a new partner, note where differences in authority, communication, uncertainty and time could create risk.
2. **Agree working arrangements**: a short written agreement covering communication channels, response times, escalation, definitions of done, approvals and holidays.
3. **Observe**: watch how information actually flows, how problems surface and how commitments are kept.
4. **Adjust**: change meeting formats, questions, documents or relationships based on what you see.
5. **Review periodically**: what worked, what surprised you and what to change next.

## A worked example

This is an illustration. A 15-person Australian business designs aluminium outdoor furniture and has it made by a contract manufacturer overseas. Over a year, it receives several shipments with inconsistent finish and dimensional problems, and two shipments arrive weeks late. Each time, the factory's sales contact had confirmed that everything was on track.

The owner reflects on the pattern rather than blaming the factory. The business's questions had mostly invited yes or no answers. Specifications were spread across emails. Holidays in the factory's country had not been built into schedules. And all communication went through a sales contact who saw the role as keeping the customer happy.

The business makes several changes. It writes a short working agreement with the factory covering weekly video calls, a standard agenda with risks as the first item, a shared holiday calendar and named contacts for production and quality. Each product gets a consolidated specification with annotated photographs and an approved golden sample held by both sides. Instead of asking "Can you hold this tolerance?", the product manager asks the factory to explain how it will measure the dimension and what it would need to hold it. In one such conversation, the quality manager explains that a jig is worn, and the business pays for half of a replacement. The owner visits the factory, meets the production team and spends time getting to know the factory's managers. Lunar New Year is scheduled as a planned gap in production, with stock built beforehand.

Within six months, shipments are arriving on the agreed dates and finish problems have largely disappeared. The factory's contacts begin raising issues before shipment rather than after. The business applies the same lesson at home: its warehouse safety induction, previously a talk followed by "Any questions?", is redesigned around demonstration and observed practice, with key points in pictures, so that understanding is checked by watching people work.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Treat culture as a delivery issue**, not just etiquette.
- **Use frameworks as questions**, not predictions about individuals.
- **Recognise that your own habits are cultural too.**
- **Standardise safety, ethics, law, specifications and decision rights**; adapt style and process.
- **Ask questions that need more than yes.**
- **Make early bad news welcome.**
- **Write specifications down** and use reference samples.
- **Use plain language**, unambiguous dates and explicit units.
- **Plan around time zones and holidays.**
- **Invest in relationships** with the people who do the work.
- **Check problems for ordinary causes** before calling them cultural.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a shared language means shared meaning.**
- **Stereotyping individuals** from national averages.
- **Treating your own working style as neutral.**
- **Relaxing essential standards** in the name of flexibility.
- **Accepting "yes" as confirmation** without checking understanding.
- **Relying only on a sales contact.**
- **Ignoring other countries' holiday calendars.**
- **Blaming culture** for unclear requirements.

## Questions to ask

- Where could differences in authority, disagreement or uncertainty affect this work?
- What must stay the same everywhere, and what can adapt?
- How do we know our partner has understood, beyond hearing yes?
- How early do problems reach us, and through whom?
- Have we agreed in writing what done means?
- Which holidays and time differences affect our schedule?
- When something went wrong, was it really cultural, or something more basic?

## Bringing it together

Cultural differences rarely cause problems through manners. They cause problems through different readings of authority, disagreement, uncertainty, relationships and commitment, hidden beneath shared processes and a shared language. Use cultural frameworks to generate questions rather than predictions, recognise your own habits as cultural, and be explicit about what must stay consistent and what can adapt. Then build simple habits: questions that need more than yes, safe routes for bad news, written specifications and samples, plain language, planned holidays and real relationships with the people doing the work. Review and adjust as you learn, and remember that not every problem is cultural.
