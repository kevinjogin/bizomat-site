On the evening before the pilgrims leave Southwark, the innkeeper of the Tabard, Harry Bailly, proposes a game. Each pilgrim is to offer a pair of stories on the ride out and another pair on the ride home. Whoever tells the best will be given supper at the others' expense when they return to his inn. He will ride with them at his own cost and act as their guide. Anyone who disputes his judgement will pay everyone's expenses on the road. The pilgrims agree at once, and ask him to be their governor, judge and record keeper.

It is the first contract in English literature that most readers meet, and it has nearly every fault a contract can have. This chapter reads the Host's rules as an owner reads a draft contract, then applies the same reading to the grandstand's head contract and sets the society's contingency.

## Reading the Host's rules as a contract

The rules are set out in the closing lines of the General Prologue. Stripped of their verse, they contain the parts any agreement needs: scope, a prize, a judge, a sanction and a price.

```figure
type: compare
title: The Host's rules, clause by clause
cols: What the rules say | What a careful owner would ask
caption: The Host's terms, paraphrased from the General Prologue. Each has a gap that the Tales themselves expose.
- Scope | two tales out and two back from each pilgrim | about 30 pilgrims, so about 120 tales: was that ever realistic?
- Award criterion | the tale of best "sentence" and most "solaas" | meaning and pleasure: how are two criteria weighed against each other?
- Prize | supper at the others' cost, at the Tabard | at what price, and who decides the menu?
- * Judge | the Host himself | he owns the inn where the prize will be eaten
- Sanction | whoever disputes his judgement pays all expenses on the road | so nobody can safely challenge a wrong decision
```

Three faults stand out. The scope was never achievable: the Tales as they survive contain about two dozen stories, one per teller at most, and the pilgrims never reach Canterbury in the text. The award criterion names two qualities, sentence (moral weight) and solaas (enjoyment), without saying how to trade one off against the other. And the judge has an interest in the outcome. Whoever wins, the supper is eaten at the Host's inn, and the losers pay him for it.

The sanction makes the conflict worse. A rule that penalises anyone who challenges the judge removes the only remedy for a bad decision. Chaucer's pilgrims spend much of the journey quarrelling with each other and with the Host, and the Host spends much of it interrupting tales he dislikes. The rules did not prevent dispute; they only made it costly to raise.

## The grandstand contract under the same reading

This is an illustration. The society's head contract with the builder uses AS 4000 general conditions with special conditions drafted by the design engineer's firm. The firm is also named as superintendent. The clerk of works reads the draft special conditions with the Host's rules in mind, asking the same questions of each clause: is the scope achievable, are the criteria checkable, is the judge independent, and can a wrong decision be challenged?

```figure
type: checklist
title: Findings from the clerk of works' review of the draft special conditions
caption: This is an illustration. Two items were resolved before signing; two were left open for the committee to decide with legal advice.
- x Scope: "complete grandstand ready for use" | rewritten as a schedule of deliverables, including the occupation certificate
- x Criterion: "roof to be watertight" | replaced with a water test of each gutter and roof penetration, witnessed and recorded
- ? Judge: superintendent is the firm that designed the structure | conflict when a claim alleges a design error
- ? Sanction: no time bar on disputes, but no expert determination step | disputes would go straight to litigation
```

The first two were drafting gaps of the Host's kind. "Ready for use" sounds clear until the builder hands over a finished structure without the certificate that allows the public to sit in it. "Watertight" sounds clear until it rains and the parties argue about how much water is a leak. In each case the clerk of works replaced a quality the parties would judge differently with a test they would carry out together.

The third finding is the Host's problem in modern form. A superintendent is required to act honestly and fairly in assessing claims, and design firms act as superintendent on many projects without difficulty. But when the builder claims extra time because a detail on the drawings cannot be built, the superintendent is assessing a claim that its own design was wrong. The clerk of works recommended, and the committee agreed after taking advice, a special condition that any claim alleging a design error over A$10,000 be reviewed by an independent engineer before the superintendent decides it.

## Rules about time and money

Two further rules in the contract matter for every later chapter. Retention of 5% is held from each progress payment as security for completion and defects. Liquidated damages of A$1,500 per working day apply if the builder does not reach practical completion by the contract date, adjusted for any extensions of time granted.

Liquidated damages are a pre-agreed estimate of the owner's loss, not a penalty, and the figure should be one the owner can explain. The clerk of works checked the society's: A$1,500 per working day covered the hire of temporary seating that the society would need if the stand were not ready for the show, spread over the working days of the likely delay, plus the committee's extra administration. The working was filed with the contract, because a figure the owner cannot explain is easier for a builder to challenge.

## Contingency: the pilgrims' reserve

The Host's rules made no provision for anything going wrong on the road. A construction budget must. Contingency is money set aside for costs that are likely to arise within the agreed scope but cannot yet be identified: unknown ground, a latent service, a design detail that turns out to need more steel. It is not money for things the owner later decides to add.

The [Contingency Allowance](/tools/contingency/) tool gives a screening figure. It asks for a base cost and three ratings from 1 to 5, for estimate uncertainty, complexity and risk exposure. It adds the three ratings, divides by 15, and sets the contingency rate at 5% plus that fraction of 20%, so that the rate runs from 9% at the lowest ratings to 25% at the highest. It is a screening method only: it says so itself, and recommends quantified risk analysis for large or poorly defined projects.

This is an illustration. At the concept stage, before ground investigation and with only a sketch design, the society's engineer rated uncertainty 4, complexity 3 and risk 3. At contract signing, with a geotechnical report, a full design and a fixed-price head contract, the clerk of works rated uncertainty 2, complexity 3 and risk 2.

```figure
type: formula
title: Screening contingency for the grandstand, at concept and at contract
formula: rate = 5% + 20% × (U + C + R) ÷ 15
caption: This is an illustration. The rate falls as uncertainty is retired. The base is the society's whole budget of A$1,180,000, not only the head contract, because the seats, fees and services carry risk too.
- U | estimate uncertainty rating, 1 to 5
- C | complexity rating, 1 to 5
- R | risk exposure rating, 1 to 5
step: Concept: rate = 5 + 20 × (4 + 3 + 3) ÷ 15 = 18.33%; allowance = 1,180,000 × 18.33% = A$216,333
step: Contract: rate = 5 + 20 × (2 + 3 + 2) ÷ 15 = 14.33%; allowance = 1,180,000 × 14.33% = A$169,133
step: Approved budget including contingency = 1,180,000 + 169,133 = A$1,349,133 ex GST
```

The society's committee approved A$169,133 of contingency, bringing the total budget to A$1,349,133 excluding GST. Just as important as the figure was the rule for using it, written into the committee's minutes:

1. Contingency pays only for costs within the approved scope that could not be identified when the budget was set.
2. Each release is recommended in writing by the clerk of works, with the cause and the amount, and approved by the president and treasurer.
3. Anything that changes the scope, however small, goes to the full committee as a scope change, with its own funding.
4. The remaining contingency is reported at every committee meeting alongside the risks it is meant to cover.

## Failure: contingency used as a wish list

The rule was tested in week 7. A committee member, seeing the steel frame go up, asked for shade sails over the walkway in front of the stand. A quote came in at A$18,400 excluding GST. The member's view was that the project had A$169,133 of contingency and nothing had gone wrong yet, so the sails could come out of it.

The request failed the first test in the minutes: shade sails were not in the approved scope. Paying for them from contingency would have done two things. It would have spent money set aside for genuine risks, leaving the project short if the ground or the design produced a surprise, which in week 9 it did. And it would have hidden a scope change inside a risk line, so that the committee's reports would no longer show what the project had become.

```figure
type: decision
title: Is this a call on contingency?
question: Was this cost within the approved scope, and could it have been identified when the budget was set?
caption: The shade sails failed the first question. The water main found under the old footing in week 9 passed both.
- Within scope and not identifiable earlier | release contingency, with a written cause and amount | the week 9 water main relocation
- * Outside the approved scope | not contingency: a scope change for the committee, with its own funding | the shade sails
- Within scope and identifiable earlier | an estimating gap: fund from contingency, and record the lesson | a missed item in the services budget
```

Detection was simple because the rule existed in writing before the request arrived. The correction was to put the sails to the full committee as a separate proposal, where they were deferred until after the show, when the society would know how much contingency was left.

## Tracking the reserve through the job

A contingency that is set once and never reported tends to be spent once and never explained. The clerk of works keeps a contingency register: each release, its cause, the date, the approval, and the balance left against the risks that remain.

This is an illustration. By the end of week 14, three releases had been approved.

The register shows two things the committee needs. The project is using its reserve for the purpose it was set aside for, and at a rate the remaining risks can bear: 18% of the allowance has gone with about half the work done and the ground work, the riskiest part, finished. If the register had shown 60% gone at the same point, the committee would have needed to decide early whether to find more money or trim scope, not discover it at the final account.

## A supper at a certain price

One part of the Host's arrangement was sound. The pilgrims did not leave the cost of the prize open: they agreed that the supper would be at a set price before they left. That is the essence of a lump-sum contract. The builder agrees to deliver a defined scope for a fixed sum, and takes the risk of its own costs running over.

The protection works only if the scope is defined well enough to price. Where part of the work cannot yet be defined, a contract can carry a provisional sum: an allowance named in the contract, with the final cost adjusted to what is actually spent. The grandstand contract carried one provisional sum, A$22,000 for the kiosk's fit-out, because the society had not yet chosen its kitchen equipment. The clerk of works tracks provisional sums alongside contingency, because both are places where the final cost can move, and the committee needs to see them together.

## When the plan shrinks on the road

The Host's plan for about 120 tales shrank, in practice, to about two dozen, and nobody recorded a decision to change it. Projects shrink the same way. A feature is quietly dropped to save time, a finish is downgraded to save money, and at handover nobody can say when the change was made or who agreed it. The clerk of works keeps a scope register beside the contingency register: every addition, deletion or substitution, with the instruction that authorised it and its effect on cost and time. A scope that changes on the record is managed; a scope that changes off it is simply lost.

## Who judges, and how to challenge

The Host's last fault was a sanction that punished anyone who questioned his decisions. Construction contracts handle the same problem with a dispute process: notice, a time for the parties to confer, often expert determination, and then arbitration or the courts. The steps exist so that a wrong decision can be challenged without blowing up the project.

The clerk of works' role in this is narrow but important: keeping the record that a dispute will turn on. A superintendent's decision is reviewed on the evidence. If the site diary, photographs and inspection records show what happened and when, a dispute is shorter and cheaper, whichever way it goes.

## Exercise: rewrite three clauses

The draft special conditions contained three more clauses. For each, say which of the Host's faults it shares and rewrite it so that it can be checked.

1. "Seating shall be installed to the satisfaction of the Principal."
2. "The Contractor shall maintain good access for the Society's members during construction."
3. "The Superintendent's decision on any matter shall be final."

**Worked answer.** (1) Like the Host's "best sentence and most solaas", the criterion is a judgement with no standard, and the judge is the owner. Rewrite: "Seats shall be installed to the supplier's installation instructions and drawing GS-S-04 revision C, with every fixing torqued as specified and recorded on the installation checklist, inspected by the Superintendent." (2) The scope is undefined: what is "good access", where and when? Rewrite: "The Contractor shall keep the pedestrian path from the north gate to the pavilion, shown hatched on drawing GS-A-01, open, lit and separated from the works by a barrier at least 1.8 m high, at all times except during crane lifts notified 48 hours in advance." (3) This is the Host's sanction: it removes any challenge to a wrong decision. Rewrite: "A party dissatisfied with a decision of the Superintendent may give notice of dispute within 10 business days, after which the dispute resolution clause applies." In each case the rewrite names a test, a place, a time or a route, so that whether the clause was met is a fact, not an opinion.
