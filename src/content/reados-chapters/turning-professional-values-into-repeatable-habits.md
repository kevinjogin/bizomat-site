Professional values often sound clear until a busy day forces a choice. You want to be reliable, but accept a date before checking capacity. You value learning, but move straight from one completed job to the next without reviewing what happened. The gap is between a description of yourself and the actions your working arrangements support.

In *Atomic Habits*, James Clear argues that repeated behaviour and a person's sense of identity can reinforce each other. His practical starting point is to consider the kind of person you want to become, then create evidence through small actions. Applied to business, this provides a way to translate values into ordinary work without treating identity as a fixed personality label or a guarantee of success.

## Separate outcomes, processes and identity

An **outcome** is a result, such as completing a project or reducing missed commitments. A **process** is the sequence of actions used to produce work. **Professional identity**, in this context, is the way you understand the kind of practitioner, colleague or founder you are trying to be.

These operate at different levels. Delivering a report is an outcome. Checking its assumptions and obtaining review are process steps. Seeing yourself as someone who makes evidence-based decisions is an identity that those actions may support.

Clear's framework asks you to connect the levels rather than focus only on the final result. Completing one accurate report is useful; developing a repeatable checking practice can affect many reports. Over time, performing that practice gives you concrete reasons to regard careful work as part of how you operate.

The identity is not proof of competence. Someone who describes themselves as analytical can still make a calculation error. Keep the quality standard and the evidence visible so that a useful self-description does not become an excuse to avoid checking.

## Define a value through an observable action

A **value** is a principle you consider important in choosing how to act. To use it in habit design, specify what someone would observe when you apply it. This turns an abstract preference into a practical decision about recurring work.

Reliability might mean reviewing dependencies before confirming a date. Curiosity might mean asking what evidence could challenge a preferred idea. Respect might mean checking another person's understanding before treating a handover as complete.

Choose an action small enough to repeat at a relevant moment. The aim is not to compress the entire value into one gesture. It is to establish one useful expression that can become dependable and lead to better decisions.

| Value or aspiration | Observable action | Evidence to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reliability | Check capacity before making a commitment | Dates are based on an actual work plan |
| Curiosity | Record a question that could challenge the proposal | Investigations include contrary evidence |
| Care | Review a consequential detail before handover | Errors are identified before reliance |
| Respect | Invite clarification at a work transition | Both people understand the next step |

Select behaviour relevant to your actual role. A practice that helps a designer may not be the most useful starting point for someone coordinating delivery. The value can remain shared while its expression differs.

## Examine the stories that shape your choices

A **self-description** is a general statement you make about how you work. Some are useful summaries of experience; others conceal assumptions that are ready for review. A founder who says they are the person who fixes everything may find it difficult to let colleagues develop responsibility.

Look for descriptions that repeatedly justify an unhelpful action. Being a perfectionist might be used to explain why a useful draft never reaches review. Being spontaneous might excuse unclear commitments. The problem is not the label itself but the decision it appears to settle without examination.

Translate the description into a specific, testable claim. Instead of assuming that you cannot delegate, identify the work that has been difficult to hand over and what was missing. Perhaps the task lacked clear instructions, an agreed completion condition or access to information.

This approach preserves agency without pretending that circumstances do not matter. Workload, tools, authority and experience can all constrain behaviour. Changing the story alone will not remove those constraints, but questioning it can help you identify what needs to change.

## Choose an identity that leaves room to learn

Prefer descriptions centred on useful principles rather than infallibility. Someone who checks and improves their work has room to discover errors. Someone who must always be the expert may experience the same discovery as a threat to the role they are trying to maintain.

Keep the identity broad enough to survive a change in method. A founder who cares about solving a customer problem can change the product. A founder whose identity is tied entirely to one particular solution may find that change harder to consider.

Avoid descriptions that imply unlimited availability or exceptional sacrifice. Being dependable can mean making realistic commitments and communicating early. It does not need to mean accepting every request regardless of capacity.

Write the intended identity in ordinary language, then immediately attach a behaviour to it. Without that second step, the exercise can remain a motivational statement. The behaviour is what makes the idea usable on a day when the language alone has little influence.

## Build evidence through manageable repetition

A **habit** is a learned response that becomes familiar through repetition in recurring circumstances. When using habits to support professional values, choose a situation that already occurs and an action that belongs naturally within it.

For example, after drafting a recommendation, record the most important assumption before sharing it. The repeated situation provides a place for the behaviour, and the resulting note gives evidence that the value of careful reasoning is being practised.

Keep the first version manageable. If the action requires a lengthy reflection after every minor task, it may not fit the work. Match the effort to the significance of the decision and expand the practice only when the evidence supports doing so.

Record whether the action occurred, but also inspect its quality. Writing any sentence in an assumptions field is not the same as identifying a material uncertainty. The practice needs a useful standard, otherwise the record can support a flattering identity without improving the work.

## Worked example: becoming more dependable with commitments

This is an illustration. A founder wants to become more dependable when coordinating prototype work with a small group of collaborators. Recent commitments have been made quickly in conversation, and several have later needed revision because a dependency was overlooked.

The founder defines the intended behaviour: before confirming a delivery date, check the required inputs and available work time, then record the agreement. This connects the value of reliability with an action at the moment the promise is made.

Over a review period, the founder makes ten new commitments. Eight are recorded after the check; two are made informally and entered later. The record therefore shows the new practice was used at the intended point for eight of ten commitments, or 80%.

That figure is an observation about use of the practice, not a measure of the founder's character. The review examines why the other two commitments bypassed it. Both arose during brief conversations away from the usual planning document.

| Part of the review | Illustrative finding | Practical response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intended value | Dependability | Retain the focus on realistic promises |
| Chosen action | Check and record before confirming | Keep the action at the decision point |
| Use during the period | Eight of ten commitments | Examine the two exceptions |
| Exception pattern | Informal conversations away from the record | Use a provisional response until the check is complete |
| Outcome review | Some dates still changed | Examine changing conditions as well as initial estimates |

The founder adopts a clearer response for informal requests: acknowledge the request and state when a confirmed date will be provided. This preserves a useful distinction between willingness to help and an approved commitment.

The next review also looks at outcomes. If a checked commitment changes because new information arrives, that does not automatically show the practice failed. The founder examines whether the original promise was reasonable and whether the change was communicated promptly. The intended identity is supported by honest handling of reality, not by pretending that dates never move.

## Let mistakes inform the practice

A missed action should prompt an examination of the circumstances. Was the cue absent, the tool inaccessible, the workload unrealistic or the action poorly defined? The answer can identify a practical adjustment rather than a judgement about whether you are the right kind of person.

Clear's account allows repeated actions to build evidence gradually. A single exception need not erase the wider pattern, but neither should a positive self-description conceal repeated problems. Keep both the trend and the specific consequence in view.

If a mistake affects someone else, address that effect directly. Habit development is not a reason to treat missed commitments as private learning only. Correct the information, communicate the revised position and make any appropriate practical repair.

Then decide what changes in the system. A reminder may be enough when the action was simply overlooked; a capacity problem requires more than another reminder. The response should fit the cause you can actually identify.

## Keep personal values separate from public claims

Wanting to become a careful practitioner does not justify advertising experience you have not gained. An intended identity describes a direction for behaviour. A public claim describes something another person may rely on when making a decision.

Use precise language about current capability, evidence and stage of development. You can demonstrate care through a clear account of limitations. A new founder does not need to sound established to show that they take the work seriously.

The distinction also applies internally. Announcing a team value does not establish that the supporting practice exists. Examine what happens in actual decisions, particularly when speed, convenience or a preferred result conflicts with the stated principle.

For the relationship between claims and conduct, [Building professional credibility through everyday actions](/reados/collections/communication-and-professional-presence/building-professional-credibility-through-everyday-actions/) provides practical examples. Identity-based habit design adds a way to make the relevant conduct more consistent over time.

## Apply shared values without demanding sameness

A team can agree on a working principle while using different methods to uphold it. One person may prepare a written summary before a discussion; another may clarify the issue through conversation and document the result afterwards. Assess whether the required information and outcome are achieved.

Do not turn a value into a narrow personality test. Curiosity does not require speaking frequently in meetings, and reliability does not require a particular style of dress or constant visibility. Define the relevant behaviour so that different people can contribute appropriately.

Invite people to identify conflicts between values and operating arrangements. A team cannot consistently prioritise careful checking if the schedule leaves no time for it. The leader's response to that observation is part of the actual standard being established.

Keep the practice within the work relationship. A shared commitment to accurate handovers does not require employees to adopt a prescribed personal identity. Agree on the behaviour needed for the work and leave room for individual self-understanding.

## Review the identity when the role changes

A useful identity at one stage can become restrictive later. A founder may initially need to perform every task directly, then need to build a system others can operate. Continuing to equate usefulness with personal intervention can prevent that transition.

Ask which underlying principle deserves to remain. Perhaps the important value is responsibility for a good result, while the method changes from doing every check personally to establishing clear review arrangements. This preserves continuity without freezing the old behaviour.

Look for situations in which defending the identity takes priority over examining evidence. Rejecting feedback because you are known for quality is a warning that the description has become less useful. The claim should remain open to what the work shows.

Schedule a review after a material change in responsibilities, business stage or working conditions. Consider which practices still help, which need adaptation and which should be retired. An identity that supports learning should permit those decisions.

## Apply the method in a small Australian business

Start with a value that affects a recurring problem. In a small team, one dependable habit around commitments, records or handovers may have a visible effect across several kinds of work. Choose the action where the problem arises rather than building a separate values programme.

For a founder working with external collaborators, make the practice visible through the agreement. Record what is needed, who will act and when the next update will occur. This creates shared evidence of reliability without relying on familiarity or assumptions.

Where the business is still exploring its direction, choose an identity compatible with uncertainty. Being a careful investigator can support testing several ideas and stopping weak ones. An identity that requires every idea to succeed can make evidence harder to use.

Keep the workload proportionate. A short note at an important decision can be enough to begin. Add structure when it solves a real problem, and review whether the habit continues to justify its effort.

## Common mistakes with identity-based habits

One mistake is treating an identity statement as an achievement. Describing yourself as organised does not establish that the necessary records exist. Attach the statement to an observable action and inspect the result.

Another is turning a missed action into a moral verdict. This can obscure a practical obstacle and make the record less honest. Examine the cause and consequence of the event rather than making a sweeping judgement about the person.

A third is choosing an identity that makes improvement difficult. Always right, endlessly available and indispensable are fragile working standards. Prefer principles that allow review, boundaries and shared responsibility.

Finally, avoid assuming that identity explains every habit. Available resources, task design and changing circumstances matter. Use Clear's framework as one useful lens while continuing to examine the conditions around the behaviour.

## Questions to ask

- What professional value do I want ordinary actions to demonstrate?
- Which recurring situation provides a useful place to practise it?
- What observable behaviour would count as evidence?
- Does the working environment make that behaviour practical?
- How will I respond to an exception without hiding its consequences?
- Can the identity adapt when my responsibilities or evidence change?

## Bringing it together

Professional values become more useful when they guide actions that can be repeated and reviewed. Clear's identity-based approach connects the kind of practitioner you want to be with evidence from the work you actually perform.

Choose a meaningful behaviour, support it with a workable system and let the results inform your self-description. Keep the principle steady where it remains valuable, while allowing the method and the role to change.
