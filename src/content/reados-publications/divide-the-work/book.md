## About this book

Every growing engineering business reaches the same question. Three skilled people have been doing everything: quoting, cutting, folding, welding, finishing, delivering. Demand rises. Should they keep doing everything, only more of it, or should the work be divided so that each person does part of it?

Adam Smith opened *The Wealth of Nations* in 1776 with that question. His pin factory, where ten workers dividing about eighteen operations made far more pins than ten workers each making whole pins, is one of the best-known examples in economics. Less often remembered is how carefully he qualified it. Dividing work gains dexterity, saves the time lost moving between tasks, and leads people to invent machines for the task in front of them. But the division of work is limited by the extent of the market, it needs a stock of materials and tools built up before it can begin, and very narrow work has human costs that a sensible business plans against.

This book applies each of those ideas to a small engineering business, with numbers. It is not a history of economics. Smith provides the checklist; the calculations, the case and the decisions are modern.

## Who it is for and how to use it

The book is for owners, managers and engineers in small manufacturing and engineering businesses, particularly those growing from a handful of generalists towards a team with defined roles. It assumes basic arithmetic and familiarity with workshop work.

Read the chapters in order. Chapter 1 sets out the case and the overall gain. Chapters 2 to 4 take Smith's three gains one at a time. Chapter 5 asks whether the market is large enough to justify dividing the work. Chapters 6 and 7 deal with balancing the divided work and the stock it needs. Chapter 8 covers the waiting that specialised roles can create, and Chapter 9 the costs of narrow work and the option of buying specialist work from another business. Each chapter ends with an exercise and a worked answer.

The calculations use several BIZOMAT tools: staffing need, learning curve, set-up reduction value, jig and fixture return, capacity check, bottleneck and line balance, WIP and lead time, queue wait, and hire or outsource. Each chapter states what the tool calculates and where its model stops.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A regional stainless steel fabrication business makes hygienic benches for food processing plants. Three fabricators each build whole benches: cut and fold the top and shelf, weld the leg frame, polish, assemble and inspect. The owner quotes, draws and buys. Demand has been steady at about six benches a week.

A distributor offers a contract for a new bench design at fifteen a week, delivered to two capital cities several hundred kilometres away. The owner must decide whether to hire more generalists or reorganise the work into stations. The book follows that decision from the first estimate to a working station team, including the parts that go wrong.

All businesses, people, prices, times and results in the case are invented for teaching. They are not BIZOMAT work. Money values are in Australian dollars and exclude GST unless stated.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Decisions about staffing, pay, employment and contracting are governed by Australian employment, tax and work health and safety law, and by the relevant award or agreement; the book points to the kinds of question to ask, not to legal answers. Check contractor arrangements with the Australian Taxation Office's guidance and Fair Work Ombudsman information, and take professional advice where needed.

Smith's works are in the public domain and are paraphrased. The supplied Companion informed the reading of Smith; the structure, case, calculations and exercises are original.
