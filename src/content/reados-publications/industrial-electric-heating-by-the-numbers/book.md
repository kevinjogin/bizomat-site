## About this book

Electric heat is everywhere in industry: the oven that cures powder coating, the furnace that tempers springs, the induction coil that hardens a shaft, the heater that keeps an oil tank fluid, the band heaters on an injection moulding barrel. Every one of them turns electrical energy into heat in a resistance, and every one of them can be understood, sized and checked with a handful of relationships that were already well understood in 1895.

This book is for manufacturing and maintenance engineers, plant managers, technicians and students who specify, size, run or troubleshoot electric heating equipment. It assumes basic electricity, heat and algebra in SI units. It does not teach electrical installation work, which in Australia must be done by licensed electricians to the Wiring Rules, and it does not replace equipment makers' data. It explains, with numbers, where the heat comes from, where it goes, and what it costs.

## An 1895 handbook as a starting point

*Electric Heating*, by Edwin J. Houston and A. E. Kennelly, was published in New York in 1895 as part of their Elementary Electro-Technical Series, written to give the general public reliable information about electricity. Kennelly went on to become one of the leading electrical engineers of his generation. Their small book covered the principles of electrical heating, the heating of bare and covered wires, fuses, electric heaters and cooking, electric welding and electric furnaces, at a time when most of these were new.

Their numbers can be checked, and most of them hold. Their resistivity for copper, their temperature coefficient for pure metals, their electric kettle and their estimates of the energy to heat a steel bar are all close to modern values. Each chapter of this book takes one of their themes, checks it and applies it to a modern case.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A fabrication and coating business is installing an electric batch oven to cure powder coating on steel frames and brackets, and a small electric furnace for tempering springs and tool steel parts. Through the book, its engineer works out the oven's heaters, supply, warm-up and running costs, checks its cables and protection, designs a heating element, sizes the tempering furnace, looks at induction and infrared alternatives, sets up the control, and compares electric heat with gas.

The business, the oven, the furnace, their dimensions, settings and costs are invented for teaching. They are not KEVOS® work, and they are not designs for any equipment. Real equipment is designed by its makers to the applicable standards and installed by licensed electricians.

## How to use the book

Chapters 1 to 4 cover the electrical side: Joule heating and energy, resistance and temperature, how conductors heat and cool, and cables and fuses. Chapters 5 to 8 cover the equipment: heating elements, ovens, furnaces, and induction and infrared heating. Chapter 9 deals with control and Chapter 10 with safety, cost and the choice between electric and gas heat. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Electrical installation and repair work must be done by licensed electricians in accordance with AS/NZS 3000 and the regulator's requirements, and electrical risks must be managed in line with Safe Work Australia's model code of practice on managing electrical risks in the workplace as adopted in each jurisdiction. Ovens and furnaces present burn, fire and, with some coatings and processes, fume and explosion hazards that their makers' instructions and the work's risk assessment must address. The 1895 practices are described as history, not practice.
