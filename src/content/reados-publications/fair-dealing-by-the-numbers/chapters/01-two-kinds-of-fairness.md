"Is that fair?" is one of the most common questions in business and one of the least precise. A customer asks it about a price. A welder asks it about overtime. A supplier asks it about who got the order. Each question sounds similar, but they call for different kinds of answer, and mixing them up is a reliable way to treat someone unfairly while believing you are being reasonable.

Aquinas, following Aristotle, divides particular justice into two species (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.61 a.1). One governs dealings between two parties, such as a sale. The other governs the sharing of something common among several people. He gives each its own measure, and the difference between those measures is the foundation for everything else in this book. This chapter explains the two, sets up the case the book follows, and shows how to tell which kind of fairness a question is really asking about.

## Fairness between two parties

The first kind, which Aquinas calls commutative justice, governs exchanges between two parties: buying and selling, lending, hiring, and also the involuntary "exchanges" that happen when one person harms another's property or reputation (II-II q.61 a.3). Its rule is equality of thing to thing. What each party gives should equal what it receives.

Aquinas calls the measure of this equality the arithmetical mean (II-II q.61 a.2). His example is simple. If two people each hold 5, and one takes 1 from the other, one now holds 6 and the other 4. Justice restores them both to 5, the mean between 6 and 4, by moving exactly 1 back. Not 2, not a round apology, and not an amount adjusted for how wealthy each party is. The size of the wrong fixes the size of the correction.

In a workshop, this is the justice of the quote and the invoice. The customer pays a price; the workshop supplies a tank. If the tank is what was described and the price is a fair estimate of its worth, the exchange is equal. If the workshop overcharges by A$682.50, it has gained A$682.50 and the customer has lost A$682.50, and equality is restored by returning exactly that amount. Chapter 7 works through precisely this case.

## Fairness in sharing among many

The second kind, distributive justice, governs the sharing of something common among several people: honours, offices, resources, or burdens (II-II q.61 a.1). Its rule is not equal amounts but equal proportion. Each person's share should stand in the same ratio to whatever entitles them to it.

Aquinas calls this the geometrical mean, and again his example is numerical. Six is to 4 as 3 is to 2, because both ratios are 1.5. The differences are not equal, since 6 exceeds 4 by 2 while 3 exceeds 2 by only 1, but the proportions are (II-II q.61 a.2). Distributive fairness is about getting the ratios right.

In a workshop, distributive justice appears whenever something scarce has to be shared: welding hours among customers, overtime among staff, a bonus pool, or the business that goes to competing suppliers. The question is never simply "is everyone getting the same?" but "is everyone getting a share proportionate to what entitles them to it?"

## The running case

This is an illustration. A four-person fabrication workshop in regional Australia, with an owner who also estimates, two welder-fabricators and an apprentice, charges its labour at a loaded rate of A$105 an hour. Over the next eight working days its two welders have 120 welding hours available between them: 2 people × 7.5 hours × 8 days.

Three customers want those hours.

1. **A food manufacturer** wants a quote for a 3,000-litre stainless steel holding tank for a salted product. The tank's welding content is about 40 hours of a 62-hour job. The customer's purchase order would follow acceptance of the quote.
2. **A quarry** has a signed order for repairs to a conveyor guard, about 50 welding hours, due in five working days.
3. **An equipment dealer**, a long-standing friend of the owner, has asked informally about 12 trailer frames, about 60 welding hours, with no order yet.

Together they want 150 hours, 30 more than exist. The tank job will also test the workshop's costing, its description of what it is selling, its handling of a defect and its correction of an invoice. Each later chapter takes one of those tests.

## Equal amounts are not equal treatment

Suppose the owner, wanting to be even-handed, splits the 120 hours equally: 40 to each customer. It sounds fair. Look at what it does in proportion to what each customer needs.

The tank receives 40 of its 40 hours, 100%. The quarry receives 40 of 50, 80%. The dealer receives 40 of 60, about 67%. An equal split of amounts has produced unequal treatment, because the customers' needs are unequal. The arithmetic of exchange has been used on a problem of distribution.

A proportional split gives each customer the same fraction of what it needs. Since 120 hours is 80% of the 150 wanted, each receives 80%: 32 hours for the tank, 40 for the quarry and 48 for the dealer. The ratios are equal, which is Aquinas's geometrical mean.

Proportion to need is not the end of the matter. Aquinas says distributive justice gives in proportion to each person's standing in relation to what is shared, and in business that standing is usually a matter of entitlement: a signed order, a promised date, a safety obligation. The quarry has a signed order due in five days; the dealer has an informal enquiry. Treating them in proportion to hours wanted ignores that difference. Chapter 6 returns to this question, because choosing the right basis for proportion is the hard part of distributive fairness.

## Money as the common measure

A workshop exchanges unlike things: steel and skilled hours for money. Aquinas notes that exchanges are not always between equal things, and that money was devised as a measure so that unlike things could be exchanged in proportion (II-II q.61 a.4). A tank and a sum of dollars are not alike, but a price lets them be compared.

That observation has a practical consequence. The price is the place where commutative justice is either kept or broken. If the price fairly represents the worth of what is supplied, the exchange is equal. If it does not, because of a mistake, a misdescription or an exploitation, the exchange is unequal even if both parties signed. Chapters 2 to 5 examine each of those ways a price can go wrong.

## Equal exchange is not a profitless exchange

A reader meeting the idea of equality in exchange for the first time sometimes concludes that a fair price must equal cost, so that any margin is a kind of overcharge. Aquinas does not say that, and the workshop should not believe it.

He discusses directly whether a trader may sell for more than he paid (II-II q.77 a.4). His answer distinguishes motives and circumstances. Gain pursued for its own sake, without limit, he regards with suspicion. But gain is not wrong in itself, and it becomes legitimate when it serves a necessary or worthy end, such as supporting a household or supplying the community with what it needs. He also recognises that a price may properly rise because of improvement to the goods, a change in their value, or the cost and risk of bringing them to the buyer.

In a workshop, almost all of the margin falls under those headings. The owner's own labour in estimating and managing the job, the risk that the estimate is wrong, the overhead that keeps the doors open between jobs, and the income that supports the people who work there are real costs of supplying the tank, even if they do not appear on a materials list. A price that covers them is not an unequal exchange. It is an honest estimate of what it costs a going concern to make and deliver the thing.

What equality does rule out is a price that bears no honest relation to worth: one inflated because the buyer is desperate, or because the buyer is rich, or because the buyer cannot see what they are getting. Chapter 2 builds the tank's cost and price, and examines exactly where a premium stops being compensation for the seller's cost and becomes a charge on the buyer's need.

## A failure: the wrong kind of fairness

Two arguments made in the workshop during the first week show how easily the two kinds of fairness are confused.

The first came from the owner about the tank. "The food manufacturer is a big company. They can afford a bit more than a small customer would pay." That argument applies a distributive idea, the parties' standing, to an exchange. In an exchange the measure is the worth of the thing, not the wealth of the buyer. Charging a large customer more for the same tank because it can pay is not proportionate treatment; it is an unequal exchange.

The second came from the owner about the frames. "The dealer always pays within a week. He should get his hours first." That argument applies an exchange idea, the dealer's good payment record, to a distribution. Prompt payment is a reason to value the dealer as a customer, and it might justify better payment terms in his own exchanges. It is not, by itself, a reason to give him capacity already needed for a signed order with a firm date.

Both errors are detected by the same question, asked before any argument about fairness is accepted: is this an exchange between two parties, or a sharing among several? If it is an exchange, the test is equality between what is given and received. If it is a sharing, the test is equal proportion to a stated entitlement. An argument that uses the other kind's measure is answering the wrong question.

The correction in both cases was to restate the question. For the tank: what is the tank worth, and does the price represent it? For the frames: what entitles each customer to a share of the 120 hours, and in what proportion? Both are harder questions than the ones the owner started with, and both are answered later in the book.

## Where each kind shows up

Most decisions in a small business fall clearly into one kind or the other. A few combine them, and those need care.

| Decision | Kind of fairness | Test |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing a quote | Exchange | Does the price fairly estimate the worth of what is supplied? |
| Describing the goods | Exchange | Does the customer receive what was described? |
| Correcting an overcharge | Exchange | Is exactly the excess returned, promptly? |
| Allocating welding hours | Sharing | Is each share proportionate to a stated entitlement? |
| Sharing overtime among staff | Sharing | Is the basis known in advance and applied consistently? |
| Choosing a supplier from quotes | Both | Exchange in the contract; sharing in giving each bidder equal treatment |

The last row is the hardest. Choosing a supplier is an exchange with the winner, but the competition that precedes it is a distribution of opportunity among the bidders. Chapter 6 shows how changing the selection criteria after seeing the quotes breaks distributive fairness even when the eventual contract is a fair exchange.

## Exercise: share a bonus pool

The owner sets aside A$3,000 as a year-end bonus for the two welders and the apprentice. Their billable hours for the year were 600, 450 and 450. Calculate an equal split and a split proportional to billable hours, and say what would have to be true for the proportional split to be the fair one.

**Worked answer.** An equal split gives each person A$1,000. A split proportional to billable hours gives 3,000 × 600 ÷ 1,500 = A$1,200 to the first welder and 3,000 × 450 ÷ 1,500 = A$900 to each of the others, which preserves equal dollars per billable hour: A$2.00 each. This is a distribution, so the question is what entitles each person to a share. Proportion to billable hours is fair only if billable hours are the right measure of contribution, and if everyone knew in advance that the bonus would be shared that way. If the apprentice's hours were lower because the apprentice spent time in training, as the business required, then billable hours would understate their contribution and penalise them for following the plan. A fair distribution starts by stating its basis before the shares are known, so that the basis is chosen for its merit rather than for the result it produces.
