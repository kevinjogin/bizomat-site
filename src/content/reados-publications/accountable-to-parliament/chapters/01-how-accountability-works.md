Accountability, in the Commonwealth sense, means having to explain and justify what was done with public power and public money to someone who can ask questions, test the answers and act on them. For an Australian Government agency that someone is not one body but several: the Minister, Parliament and its committees, the Auditor-General, the Commonwealth Ombudsman, and the courts and tribunals. Each asks different questions, on its own timetable, with its own powers.

An official who knows how those channels fit together can see a question coming and answer it accurately the first time.

This is an illustration. On Monday 24 May 2027, nine days before her agency's Budget estimates hearing, Clare Whitfield pinned a single page to the wall of her team's room. It showed every hearing, deadline and report she could see coming in the next twelve months. This chapter explains what is on that page and who answers for each item.

## Clare's unit and what it owns

This is an illustration. Clare works at the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO), a fictional non-corporate Commonwealth entity. Its Chief Executive Officer (CEO) is both its accountable authority under the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013 (the PGPA Act) and its agency head under the Public Service Act 1999. The Parliamentary and Accountability Unit sits in the Office of the CEO, and Clare, an EL1, is its Parliamentary and Accountability Manager.

The unit does not own the facts. The program and corporate areas own what NRIO does. Sam Reid, for example, manages the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP), a grants program, and owns its numbers. The unit owns the process. It commissions briefs and answers, sets the standard they must meet, checks them against their sources and against what NRIO has already said, tracks every deadline and keeps the record. The CEO clears what goes out in NRIO's name. Anything that speaks for the Minister goes through the Minister's office.

The split matters. If the unit writes the facts, nobody who knows the program has checked them. If it stops checking, the agency can tell two bodies two different things.

## Responsible government: who answers for what

Australia has responsible government. Ministers sit in Parliament and answer to it for their portfolios, including the agencies in them. Officials serve the government of the day through their Minister. They do not answer to Parliament in their own right as a Minister does, but they appear before its committees to explain how programs are administered and what the facts are.

The Public Service Act puts this into one of the six APS Values. Section 10(4) says: "The APS is open and accountable to the Australian community under the law and within the framework of Ministerial responsibility." Both halves count. Officials keep records and give evidence that someone outside can test, and they explain the administration and the facts, while choices of policy, and the reasons for them, are for ministers to defend.

The PGPA Act adds a line inside the agency. The accountable authority answers for how the entity is governed, how it uses public resources and how it performs.

This is an illustration. At NRIO, one question about the RCFP can have three right owners. The Minister answers for the decisions to fund particular projects. The CEO answers for how NRIO ran the round. Sam answers to the CEO for the program's records and numbers. A Senator who asks why the Minister chose a project is asking the Minister. A Senator who asks how many applications were assessed is asking for a fact NRIO owns.

## Keeping the Minister informed

Section 19(1) of the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) gives the accountable authority five duties. It must "keep the responsible Minister informed of the activities of the entity", and give the responsible Minister or the Finance Minister the reports, documents and information they require. It must notify the responsible Minister as soon as practicable after making a significant decision, give reasonable notice of any significant issue that may affect the entity, and notify the Minister as soon as practicable after becoming aware of a significant issue that has affected it. The section says "must": these are requirements.

The Act does not define "significant". The duty sits with the accountable authority, who can meet it only if officials bring decisions and issues forward early. The practical answer is a habit often called "no surprises": anything likely to draw questions from Parliament, the media or the public reaches the Minister's office from the agency before it reaches the Minister from anyone else. That habit goes further than section 19 requires, and it should. A Minister who answers to Parliament for an agency needs time to understand an issue before being asked about it.

This is an illustration. At NRIO, Clare's unit runs the alert process for the CEO. An alert is one page: what happened or was decided, what NRIO is doing, what is already public, and when the next update will come. It goes to the Minister's office the same day, and a copy goes on the unit's register so that later briefs and answers can be checked against it.

Informing the Minister is not asking permission: if a decision is the accountable authority's to make, the alert reports it and does not hand it over. Dealings with the Minister's office also call for discretion. Section 13(6) of the APS Code of Conduct says an employee must "maintain appropriate confidentiality about dealings that the employee has with any Minister or Minister's member of staff".

## The duty to tell the truth

The APS Code of Conduct, in section 13 of the [Public Service Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text), applies to every APS employee, and section 14(1) says agency heads "are bound by the Code of Conduct in the same way as APS employees". The duty that runs through this whole book is in section 13(9). An APS employee "must not provide false or misleading information in response to a request for information that is made for official purposes", in connection with their APS employment.

A Senator's question at a hearing, a question on notice, a committee's call for submissions, an auditor's request for documents and a notice from the Ombudsman are all requests for information. Treat each one as the kind of request the Code describes. In practice that means three habits. Check a number against its source before you give it. Say what you do not know. Do not let a true sentence leave a false impression, for example by quoting a figure that answers a slightly different question from the one asked.

Two other Values point the same way. Impartial, in section 10(5), asks for advice that is "frank, honest, timely and based on the best available evidence". Stewardship, in section 10(6), asks the APS to build "its capability and institutional knowledge": in this work, records good enough for the next person to answer for what this person did.

Mistakes still happen. Whether a particular wrong statement breaches the Code is a question for the agency's own processes. What is not in question is what to do once you know: tell the people who need to know, and correct the record promptly. Chapter 10 covers how.

## The bodies that hold an agency to account

Four kinds of body examine a Commonwealth agency's work from outside.

**Parliament and its committees.** At Estimates hearings, Senate committees examine the government's proposed spending and the administration of agencies. Committees of either House, or of both, also run inquiries. The Joint Committee of Public Accounts and Audit (JCPAA) has duties under its own Act, including "to examine all reports of the Auditor-General" tabled in each House (Public Accounts and Audit Committee Act 1951, s 8(1)(c)). Committees' procedures come from each House's powers, standing orders and resolutions, which sit outside legislation. This book describes them only in general terms.

**The Auditor-General.** Section 8(1) of the [Auditor-General Act 1997](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05248/latest/text) says: "The Auditor-General is an independent officer of the Parliament." Supported by the ANAO, the Auditor-General "may at any time conduct a performance audit" of a Commonwealth entity (s 17(1)), can direct a person to provide information and documents (s 32), and has each performance audit report tabled in each House (s 17(4)(a)). The JCPAA advises the Auditor-General of the Parliament's audit priorities, but cannot direct the Auditor-General's activities (Public Accounts and Audit Committee Act, s 8(1A)).

**The Commonwealth Ombudsman.** Under section 5(1) of the Ombudsman Act 1976, the Ombudsman investigates action that relates to "a matter of administration", on a complaint or on the Ombudsman's own motion. The Ombudsman is not authorised to investigate "action taken by a Minister" (s 5(2)(a)). An officer "must use the officer's best endeavours to assist the Ombudsman" (s 32(2)).

**Courts and tribunals.** They decide particular cases. A court can decide whether a decision was lawful; a tribunal can review some decisions on their merits, where an Act gives that right.

Behind all four sits the agency's own assurance. Section 45(1) of the PGPA Act says the accountable authority "must ensure that the entity has an audit committee". The committee, and internal audit, report to the accountable authority, not to Parliament, and they can find first what an outside body would find later. Chapter 7 covers them.

## The accountability calendar

Some accountability work runs on a cycle set by law. The accountable authority must prepare a corporate plan at least once each reporting period (PGPA Act s 35(1)(a)), and the PGPA Rule requires it to be published "on the entity's website by the last day of the second month of the reporting period" (s 16E(3)): 31 August, for a year that starts on 1 July. The annual report, with the annual performance statements in it, must be given to the responsible Minister by "the 15th day of the fourth month after the end of the reporting period" (PGPA Act s 46(2)(a)): 15 October, for a year that ends on 30 June.

Tabling is a separate step. Where an Act sets no period for tabling, as section 46 sets none, section 34C(3) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 allows 15 sitting days of each House after the day the Minister receives the report. Giving the report to the Minister and tabling it have different deadlines. Do not confuse them.

Other work arrives on dates set by someone else. The Senate schedules Estimates hearings, and each committee sets its date for answers to questions on notice. An inquiry committee sets its own date for submissions. The Auditor-General decides when to audit; once a proposed report arrives, the agency has 28 days to comment (Auditor-General Act s 19(7)(b)). A complaint to the Ombudsman can come at any time, and a notice under section 9 sets its own date.

The questions at Estimates, the inquiry, the complaint, the audit and the annual performance statements all come back to that round, which is why the unit keeps one record of what NRIO has said.

## When it goes wrong: the Minister heard it from a newspaper

This is an illustration. In March 2027, before the year this book follows, NRIO's executive decided to close a small regional office when its lease ended and move its staff to a larger office about an hour's drive away. The property and people areas planned the change with care, and the staff were told in person on Monday 15 March 2027.

The next morning a regional newspaper rang the Minister's office for comment. The adviser who took the call had never heard of the closure. The office said it would look into it, and the story that ran on Wednesday 17 March said the Minister had not been told. That afternoon the office asked the CEO for a full brief by 9 am the next day.

The decision may have been sound. The cost came from the order of events. The Minister had to answer for a decision first heard of from a reporter, and the CEO spent a day repairing trust instead of explaining the decision.

Nobody acted in bad faith. The executive paper covered the lease, the costs, the staff and the timetable, but had no line asking who outside NRIO needed to know first. Nobody thought of the Minister's office because the decision was within the CEO's authority. That was the gap: authority to decide is not permission to surprise.

The control is the alert, built into the decision process instead of left to memory. NRIO's executive papers now answer one question before a decision is made: does the Minister's office need to hear about this from NRIO, and when? If yes, the unit sends the alert before anyone outside the executive is told. The Act does not say whether closing an office is a "significant decision" under section 19(1)(c), and the practice does not need to settle that, because it tells the Minister's office either way.

The 28-day count is easy to get wrong, so show it. The proposed report arrives on Monday 10 April 2028, and the 28 days run after that day. April has 20 days left after the 10th (11 to 30 April), so 28 − 20 = 8 days fall in May. The last day is Monday 8 May 2028.

The map shows three things. The dates of the three committee events are set by the Senate and its committees, not by legislation, while two deadlines are fixed by law: 15 October and 28 days. One duty appears in every row: whatever the body, NRIO must not give it false or misleading information. And the Minister is never far away: clearing answers, tabling the annual report, and making the decisions the Ombudsman cannot investigate.
