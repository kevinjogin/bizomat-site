Smith did not want people to stop admiring well-made things. He thought the love of order and contrivance was one of the forces that kept industry moving, and that many of the improvements in machines came from it: some from the people who used them, some from the people who made them, and some from people whose trade was simply to observe and combine ideas. A business without that love would invent little.

The job shop's year has shown both sides of it. The love of system produced proposals that would have served the shop badly: a robot for the smaller loss, live dashboards for daily decisions, a precision upgrade that missed its target, and forty procedures imposed in a day. It also produced the quick-change workholding, the bar feeder, the air gauge, the scheduling package and fifteen standard sheets written by the people who use them. This chapter reviews the year by its ends, shows how easily a review slips back into admiring the systems, and sets out how the shop keeps the ingenuity while checking the result.

## The year's changes

This is an illustration. Over twelve months the shop made these changes, each tested against its end in earlier chapters:

- **Quick-change workholding**, presetting and standard programs on the lathe, about A$22,000 (Chapter 2).
- **A bar feeder** on the lathe for bar work, about A$35,000 (Chapter 3).
- **An air gauge** with masters for the tight bearing seats, about A$9,500, plus a soaking rule (Chapter 4).
- **Three months of process work** on scheduling, then a **scheduling package**, about A$8,000 to set up and A$1,100 a month (Chapter 5).
- **Fifteen standard work sheets**, co-written with the operators and split into rules and guidance (Chapters 6 and 7).
- **A first-off rule** for every batch (Chapter 7).

And it declined three proposals: the robot cell, the custom software with live dashboards, and the CMM upgrade, keeping only the CMM's programming software.

## The failure case: a review of the systems

The owner asks the office manager to prepare a review for the end of the year. Her first draft reports:

- scheduling package available **99.8%** of working hours;
- **2,400** schedule updates recorded;
- **15** standard sheets written and approved;
- bar feeder running on **31** bar jobs;
- all first-off approvals signed.

Every figure is true, and every one describes a contrivance working as designed. None says whether the shop now delivers on time, costs less per part or makes fewer bad parts. The review has slipped back into the love of system: it admires the order of the systems rather than checking the ends they were meant to serve.

### How it is detected

The test is the one from Chapter 1, applied to the review itself. For each figure, ask: is this an end the business committed to, or a measure of the means? Availability, update counts and documents written are measures of the means. On-time delivery, cost per part and reject rates are ends.

### How it is corrected

The review is rewritten around the ends stated in Chapter 1's exercise, with the system measures moved to an appendix where they belong.

The rewritten review reports first on delivery, the shop's main end:

Then it compares the lathe's work on a like-for-like basis. The [before and after comparison tool](/tools/before-after/) in BIZOMAT Tools compares cycle time, unit cost and defect rate before and after a change, and reports how many measures improved, held or worsened, with a warning to confirm the result over a representative period before standardising. For the lathe's recurring jobs, with the average time per part including each job's share of changeover, the owner enters **6.8 minutes** before and **5.9 minutes** after, unit costs of **A$14.20** and **A$12.60**, and defect rates of **3.1%** and **2.2%**.

The tool reports **improved** on all three measures: cycle time **13.2%** better, unit cost **11.3%** better, and the defect rate **0.9 points** better.

## What caused the improvement

A review is tempted to credit each change with its share of the improvement, and to credit the most admired change most. The honest position is more modest. Several changes reached the lathe in the same year: quick change, the bar feeder, the scheduling process, the package and the standard sheets. The improvement in delivery and cost per part is the result of the combination, and the shop cannot cleanly divide it among them.

Some attribution is still reasonable. The changeover hours fell from about 18 to about 7 a week, which is close to what the quick-change estimate in Chapter 2 predicted, so most of the lathe's released hours can fairly be credited to it. On-time delivery had already risen from 78% to about 85% during the process work, before the package was bought, so the process change deserves much of the credit there. The package's own contribution is harder to separate. The review says so plainly, rather than inventing a precise split.

That honesty has a practical use. Next year, when someone proposes another scheduling feature or another robot, the shop's record shows what actually moved the ends, with its uncertainty, rather than a tidy story that favours whichever contrivance was most loved.

### Checking that the comparison is fair

The before and after comparison tool warns that a result should be confirmed over a representative period, and the warning matters here. Average time per part depends on the mix of work as well as on the process. If the year had brought more long batches and fewer short ones, the average time per part would have fallen even with no improvement at all, because changeover time would be spread across more parts.

The owner checks this in the simplest way available. Instead of comparing all the lathe's work, he compares only the fifteen recurring jobs that ran in both years, at similar batch sizes. On those jobs the time per part still falls by about the same proportion, so the improvement is not an artefact of the mix. He records the check in the review, with the list of jobs, so that anyone reading it later can repeat it.

A second check is the defect rate. A fall from 3.1% to 2.2% sounds definite, but on a few thousand parts a year it amounts to a few dozen fewer rejects. The owner reports it as an improvement worth confirming next year rather than as a proven result, and notes that the first-off rule and the air gauge both arrived in the second half of the year, so a full year under both will say more.

## What did not improve

An honest review also lists what did not move, or moved the wrong way. The shop's has three entries.

- **Office time during the changeover to the package.** For the first two months, the office manager kept the whiteboard and the package running side by side and spent more time on scheduling, not less. The saving began only in the third month. The review records it so the next rollout plans for it.
- **The measuring room queue.** Giving lathe first-offs priority at the measuring machine, as Chapter 7's rule requires, lengthened the wait for other work. The queue has grown from about half a day to about a day for non-urgent parts. The review flags it as the next problem to examine, not as a reason to relax the rule.
- **The CMM programming software.** It was kept because programmers liked it and it was cheap. No end measure was named for it, so the review cannot say whether it helped. That is the one purchase of the year that skipped the shop's own test, and the review says so.

The third entry is the most useful. It shows that the love of system does not disappear after one lesson. A small purchase, liked by the people who use it, slipped through without an end. The cost was modest; the habit it represents is not.

## Reviews as a habit

The owner makes the review a fixed part of the year, not an occasion. Every approved change carries a review date and the end measure to check then, written on its proposal. Declined proposals are kept too, with the reason and the condition that would change it: the robot cell's record notes that a second-shift contract with secure volume would make it worth testing again, as Chapter 3's exercise showed. A good decision not to buy something is still a decision about the shop's ends, and it deserves the same record as a purchase.

## The business case, after the fact

The owner finishes by recasting the year's main package, quick change, scheduling set-up and training, as a business case, to compare with the robot cell he did not buy. The [business case with NPV tool](/tools/business-case/) screens an investment from its initial cost, annual benefit, annual operating cost, horizon, a delivery risk rating and the business's discount rate. It gives the annual net benefit, simple and discounted payback, the net present value, and the net present value with benefits 20% lower, as a test of robustness.

For the package, he enters an investment of **A$36,000** (A$22,000 for quick change, A$8,000 for the software set-up, A$6,000 for training and process time), an annual benefit of **A$54,540** from Chapter 2's lathe hours, an annual operating cost of **A$13,200** for the software subscription, five years and a 10% discount rate. For the robot, he enters the shop's own case from Chapter 3: A$185,000, a benefit of A$27,418, an operating cost of A$9,000, eight years and the same rate.

The package the shop bought returns its cost in under a year and stays strongly positive even if its benefit is 20% lower. The robot the shop declined would have lost money at the shop's discount rate. The difference is not that the robot is a worse machine. It is that the package was chosen for the shop's end and the robot was first proposed for its own beauty.

## Keeping the ingenuity

None of this should make the owner suspicious of every new machine. Smith's point was that the love of contrivance is a motive worth having. The shop's best improvements this year were ingenious: the quick-change system needed clever standard part families, the air gauge needed careful thought about masters and temperature, and the standard sheets needed the operators' craft. What changed was the order of the questions, not the appetite for improvement.

The owner turns that into a one-page proposal format that any improvement, from anyone in the shop, now uses:

```figure
type: checklist
title: The shop's one-page improvement proposal
caption: This is an illustration. Delight in the idea is welcome, and is recorded last. The first five items keep the end in front of the means.
- x The end: what result changes, and its current level
- x The price of that end: what a unit of improvement is worth
- x Realistic use: the shop's actual jobs, batches and people
- x A simpler alternative, and what it would deliver
- x A review date, and the end measure to check then
- x Why the proposer likes it, in a sentence
```

The last item is deliberate. People propose things they find beautiful, and pretending otherwise only hides the motive. Writing it down, after the end and the price, gives it its proper place: a reason someone cared enough to propose the change, not a reason to approve it.

The format also widens who proposes. Smith noted that improvements come from users, makers and observers alike. This year the soaking rule came out of the lathe operator's work on the rejected bores, the office manager suggested the change impact rating, and the owner, a lover of machines, proposed the bar feeder once he had seen the lathe's log. Each came through the same page.

## The love of system, in its place

```figure
type: layers
title: What the year taught the shop about the love of system
caption: This is an illustration. Each lesson came from a proposal that was admired first and tested second. The motive stays; the order of questions changes.
- Name the end first | the robot addressed 4 hours of loading; changeovers took 18
- Use realistic work | the reference site was not the shop
- Need, not capability | the CMM upgrade kept better time on parts that were already on time
- Fit the process before the software | process work moved delivery before any purchase
- Work with people | 15 used sheets beat 40 imposed ones
- *Rules where rules belong | a first-off rule, and guidance for the rest
- Review the end, not the system | availability and update counts are not results
```

Smith's watch collector is still with us, in every workshop and office where someone admires a well-made thing. The answer is not to stop admiring it. It is to ask, before buying, what the watch is for, and whether the person who wears it will be any more punctual.

## Exercise: the bar feeder's case

Use the business case with NPV tool's method for the bar feeder from Chapter 3: an investment of **A$35,000**, an annual benefit of **A$22,195**, an annual operating cost of **A$1,500**, eight years and a 10% discount rate. What are the annual net benefit, simple payback, net present value and downside net present value? How does the result compare with the investment appraisal in Chapter 3, and why might they differ?

**Worked answer.** The annual net benefit is A$22,195 − A$1,500 = **A$20,695**. The simple payback is A$35,000 ÷ A$20,695 = **1.69 years**, and the tool gives a discounted payback of 1.95 years. The net present value at 10% over eight years is about **A$75,406**, and with benefits 20% lower it is about **A$51,725**, so the case is robust.

The investment appraisal in Chapter 3 gave a net present value of about **A$77,739** for the same machine. The difference, about A$2,300, is the present value of the A$5,000 salvage value at the end of eight years, which the investment appraisal includes and the business case screen does not. Both tools agree on the decision; the screen is slightly more conservative. The habit worth keeping is to know what each tool includes, so that differences between them are understood rather than puzzling.
