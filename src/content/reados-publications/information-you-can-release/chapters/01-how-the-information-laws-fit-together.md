Information work in an Australian Government agency runs on several laws at once. The Freedom of Information Act 1982 gives people a right to documents. The Privacy Act 1988 governs personal information. The Archives Act 1983 decides how long records are kept and who may destroy them. Each has its own tests, time limits and reviewers, and most of the trouble happens where they meet: a request sent down the wrong path, a deadline nobody owns, a record deleted under one law while another still needed it.

This chapter draws the map the rest of the book uses: why the access laws exist, what each does, who oversees them, who may decide what, and how one event can engage several laws. The case starts on Monday 7 June 2027, when a request arrives in the mailbox of a small team in a fictional agency.

## The team and the request

This is an illustration. The Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO) is a fictional non-corporate Commonwealth entity. Its Information Access and Privacy Team, in the Legal and Governance Branch, handles freedom of information (FOI) requests, privacy matters, data breach assessments and published information. NRIO's website gives one mailbox for FOI and privacy requests, screened every working day.

## Why the access laws exist

Section 3(1) of the [Freedom of Information Act 1982](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text) is plain: "The objects of this Act are to give the Australian community access to information held by the Government of the Commonwealth". Agencies must publish information, and people have a right of access to documents. Parliament intends this to increase public participation and scrutiny of government, and to treat government information as a national resource. Powers under the Act are to be used "to facilitate and promote public access to information, promptly and at the lowest reasonable cost" (s3(4)).

Section 11(1) turns the objects into a right: "every person has a legally enforceable right to obtain access" to a document of an agency or an official document of a Minister, other than an exempt document. Under s11(2), access is not affected by any reasons the person gives, or by what the agency believes them to be. There is no application fee. The journalist need not explain the request, and a reporter's purpose changes nothing.

Section 3A adds that the Act does not limit any other power to publish or give access to information. An FOI request is not the only way in.

The Information Commissioner issues guidelines under s93A. They are not legislative instruments, but an agency deciding a request must have regard to them (s15(5A), s93A(2)). This book does not quote them; read the current version alongside it.

## Three laws with different jobs

The FOI Act asks who can see a document. Part II requires agencies to publish information under the Information Publication Scheme. Part III sets out requests, time limits and decisions, and Part IV the exemptions. Part V lets people ask for their personal records to be amended or annotated. Parts VI to VIIA provide for internal review, review by the Information Commissioner (IC review) and review by the Tribunal.

The [Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text) asks how personal information must be handled. Personal information is "information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable" (s6(1)). Section 15 says an APP entity, which includes an agency, must not breach an Australian Privacy Principle (APP). The 13 APPs in Schedule 1 cover collection, notice, use and disclosure, security, access (APP 12) and correction (APP 13). Part IIIC adds the notifiable data breaches scheme.

The Archives Act asks how long a record must exist and who may end it. It forbids destroying, transferring or altering a Commonwealth record except as the Act allows, and later opens records to the public through the National Archives. Chapter 2 covers it.

The laws lean on each other. APP 12.2 lets an agency refuse access to personal information to the extent the FOI Act requires or authorises a refusal. APP 11.2, the duty to destroy or de-identify personal information no longer needed, does not apply to information in a Commonwealth record; the Archives Act governs that. And the FOI Act protects other people's privacy through its own conditional exemption (s47F).

## The Information Commissioner and the Tribunal

The [Australian Information Commissioner Act 2010](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2010A00052/latest/text) establishes the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) in s5. Section 6 names three information officers: the Information Commissioner, the Freedom of Information Commissioner and the Privacy Commissioner. The Information Commissioner holds every function; the other two hold the FOI and privacy functions respectively, and each may perform the other's (s10 to s12). The FOI functions include the s93A guidelines, IC review, complaints and monitoring compliance (s8). The privacy functions include privacy complaints and data breach notifications (s9).

Review of an FOI decision has up to three levels. The applicant can ask for internal review by a different officer (s54, s54C), or go straight to the Information Commissioner (s54L). The Administrative Review Tribunal (ART), which replaced the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on 14 October 2024, reviews the Commissioner's decision on an IC review (s57A(1)(a)). Its guidance and appeals panel does not hear these matters (s57A(3)).

Privacy complaints go to the Commissioner in writing (Privacy Act s36), generally after a complaint to the agency: otherwise the Commissioner must not investigate unless that was not appropriate (s40(1A)).

## Who may decide what

An FOI decision is the agency's, but a person has to make it. Under s23(1), the responsible Minister or the principal officer may decide a request, and so may an officer acting under arrangements one of them has approved. In practice the agency head signs an authorisation naming positions or people. A decision by anyone else is not a decision under the Act.

This is an illustration. NRIO's agency head, its principal officer, has authorised Ruth and Liam. Priya runs the RCFP case: searches, documents, consultations and draft papers. The decision, and the notice carrying his name and designation (s26(1)(b)), will be Liam's. An internal review must go to someone else (s54C(2)); NRIO uses an authorised officer from outside the team.

Two less obvious rules sit behind the authorisation. A decision made personally by the principal officer or the Minister cannot be internally reviewed (s54(1)). And if no decision is notified in time, the principal officer is "taken to have made a decision personally refusing to give access" (s15AC(3)(a)). The agency head owns a refusal nobody made.

Privacy roles come from the Privacy (Australian Government Agencies — Governance) APP Code 2017. An agency must at all times have a designated Privacy Officer (s10(1)) and a Privacy Champion, who must be a senior official (s11(1) and (3)). The agency must ensure the Privacy Officer functions are carried out, including handling privacy enquiries, complaints and requests under the Privacy Act for access to and correction of personal information (s10(5)(a)). The Champion promotes a privacy culture and reviews or approves the privacy management plan (s11(4)). At NRIO, Ruth is the Privacy Officer and the General Counsel the Champion. Assessing a suspected data breach is the agency's duty (Privacy Act s26WH); Ruth leads it.

Whether a record may be destroyed is for the Information Management Unit, applying the Archives Act and authorities from the National Archives. It is never for the person who would like the record gone.

## One event, several laws

This is an illustration. Ruth sketches the RCFP request against the map.

- **FOI Act.** The request is valid: in writing, stated to be an application under the Act, describing the documents and giving an email address (s15(2)). NRIO must take all reasonable steps to acknowledge it within 14 days, by Monday 21 June 2027, and to notify a decision within 30 days after the day of receipt, by Wednesday 7 July 2027 (s15(5)). A written extension of 30 days for consultation (s15(6)) would move that to Friday 6 August 2027 (Chapter 3).
- **Privacy Act.** The documents hold personal information about people connected with applications. The FOI Act decides whether it is released (s47F) and whether they are consulted (s27A); the APPs govern how the team handles it meanwhile.
- **Business information.** Applicants' business affairs may raise s47G and consultation under s27 (Chapter 5).
- **Archives Act.** Every document in scope is a Commonwealth record, and NRIO's rule is that nothing within an open request is destroyed or altered (Chapter 2).

Sixteen days later, on Wednesday 23 June, an RCFP officer sending agreements emails a spreadsheet of the 31 grantees' contact names, phone numbers and bank account details to a private individual with a similar email address. That is a security question under APP 11 and a possible eligible data breach under Part IIIC. It is also an Archives Act question: deleting NRIO's own copies to "fix" it would destroy the evidence.

## Routing a request on the day it arrives

Requests do not arrive labelled. "Everything you hold about me" may be an FOI request, an APP 12 request or an enquiry, and the label decides the clock and the review path.

The FOI Act sets the test. A request must be in writing, state that it is an application for the purposes of the Act, give enough information to identify the documents and say how notices can be sent (s15(2)). If those elements are present, it is an FOI request, whatever its subject. If a person wants to make a request but has not met them, the agency must take reasonable steps to help (s15(3)).

A request for your own personal information that does not invoke the FOI Act is an APP 12 request. An agency must respond within 30 days after it is made and must not charge (APP 12.4(a)(i) and 12.7). An FOI request for the applicant's own personal information is free too (Freedom of Information (Charges) Regulations 2019, reg 7(1)). The paths still differ. FOI brings an acknowledgement, a decision notice with reasons, possible extensions and three levels of review. APP 12 requires written reasons for any refusal and how to complain (APP 12.9).

## When it goes wrong: an FOI request logged as a privacy enquiry

This is an illustration. On Tuesday 9 February 2027, a resident who had complained several times about works near his home emailed the team's mailbox. He wrote that he was applying under the Freedom of Information Act for all documents NRIO held about him, and asked for replies by email. A temporary officer covering the mailbox read "about me" and entered it in the privacy enquiries log. The log had no due-date column, and the email waited behind others.

The request met every element of s15(2), so the clock started on 9 February. The acknowledgement was due by Tuesday 23 February and the decision by Thursday 11 March 2027. Neither happened. On Monday 15 March, day 34, the resident asked where his FOI decision was.

By then the law had acted. Under s15AC(3), NRIO's agency head was taken to have personally refused access on 11 March, with notice taken to have been given that day. Because a deemed refusal counts as the principal officer's personal decision, internal review was not available (s54(1)); the resident's next step was IC review. No charge could be imposed, but none ever could have been: the documents held his own personal information (reg 7).

Ruth apologised in writing that day and applied to the Information Commissioner for further time (s15AC(4)). Liam decided within the time allowed and met any conditions on it, so the deemed refusal was taken never to have applied (s15AC(7)). The cost remained: two missed statutory limits, an application to the regulator, a rushed decision and five weeks without an answer.

The team knew the 30-day rule. The request was routed by its subject instead of the law it invoked, into a log that tracked no dates. NRIO's intake rule, in the figure above, now applies to every item on the day it arrives. Every FOI and APP 12 request goes into one case register with its due dates, and someone other than the case officer checks the register weekly. With about 60 own-information FOI requests a year, this is not a rare case.

## Exercise: route six events

This is an illustration. Over the following months, six events reach Ruth's team. For each, name the law that governs the first step, who at NRIO acts or decides, the first time limit, and where review or oversight lies.

| No. | Event |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Mon 7 Jun 2027: the journalist's request |
| 2 | Wed 23 Jun 2027: the grantees' spreadsheet goes to the wrong external address |
| 3 | While the request is open, an RCFP staff member proposes deleting old draft emails "to tidy up" |
| 4 | After Liam's decision, the journalist applies for internal review |
| 5 | After the journalist is given access, Ethan prepares the disclosure log entry and Information Publication Scheme updates |
| 6 | Mon 2 Aug 2027: the secretary of a community group asks for all the information NRIO holds about her personally in its client records |

### Worked answer

1. **FOI Act, Part III.** Liam decides under his s23 authorisation; Priya is the case officer. Acknowledgement by Monday 21 June 2027 (7 June plus 14 days). Decision by Wednesday 7 July 2027 (7 June plus 30 days), or Friday 6 August 2027 (7 July plus 30 days) after a written extension under s15(6). Review: internal or IC review, then the ART.
2. **Privacy Act, APP 11 and Part IIIC.** A misdirected file holding bank details may be an eligible data breach. If NRIO suspects one, it must assess it and take all reasonable steps to finish within 30 days after becoming aware: by Friday 23 July 2027, counting from 23 June (s26WH(2)). Ruth leads. If it is an eligible data breach, NRIO must give the Commissioner a statement and notify the individuals, or publish the statement if notifying them is not practicable (s26WK, s26WL; Chapter 8). The email and response records are kept. Oversight: the Information Commissioner.
3. **Archives Act, s24.** The drafts are Commonwealth records. Destroying them is lawful only within an exception in s24(2), and the Information Management Unit, not the staff member, decides whether one applies. Some may fall within the open request, so Priya needs to know. The answer is no. Oversight: the National Archives; a breach of s24 is an offence.
4. **FOI Act, Part VI.** The journalist applies in writing within 30 days after notification, or a further period NRIO allows (s54B(1)). Someone other than Liam, at NRIO an authorised officer from outside the team, makes a fresh decision within 30 days after receiving it (s54C), or Liam's decision is taken to be affirmed (s54D). IC review follows within 60 days after notice of a decision (s54S(1)), then the ART (s57A).
5. **FOI Act, s11C and Part II.** NRIO must publish the released information on a website within 10 working days after the day access is given (s11C(3) and (6)), except personal or business information where publication would be unreasonable, and other exceptions (s11C(1); Chapter 9). The scheme updates follow the plan NRIO must prepare under s8(1). Ethan prepares both. Oversight: the Information Commissioner (AIC Act s8).
6. **APP 12, or the FOI Act.** If her written request states that it is an FOI application, the FOI limits and review rights apply. If not, it is an APP 12 request: NRIO must respond by Wednesday 1 September 2027 (2 August plus 30 days) and must not charge. Either way Ruth's team handles it: Ruth is the Privacy Officer (APP Code s10(5)(a)), and she and Liam are authorised FOI decision-makers (s23). If the path is unclear, the team asks her that day. Review: under FOI, internal or IC review; under APP 12, a complaint to NRIO, then the Commissioner (Chapter 7).

Every event has one law that governs its first step, and most engage a second. Seeing both on the first day is the team's job.
