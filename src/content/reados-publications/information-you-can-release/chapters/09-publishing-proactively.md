Most of this book has dealt with information someone asked for. The FOI Act also expects agencies to publish without being asked. Part II requires every agency to run an Information Publication Scheme (IPS), and section 11C requires it to publish, on a website, most of what it releases in response to requests. The Archives Act adds a slower path: records that survive long enough are opened to everyone. Publishing well also saves work, because a document already on the website needs no request, no search and no decision.

This is an illustration. Liam O'Connor's decision of Wednesday 28 July 2027 gave the journalist most of the documents about Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) that day. The information held for third parties' review followed on Wednesday 1 September (Chapters 5 and 6). Ethan Brooks, NRIO's Privacy and Information Officer, keeps the disclosure log and the IPS pages. This chapter follows his work after the decision: what had to be published and by when, what had to stay off the website, and what else changed.

## Four ways information reaches the public

An agency's information reaches the public by four routes. They have different triggers, timing and exceptions, and an officer should know which one a task belongs to before starting it.

```figure

- Law | FOI Act Part II (s8 to s10) | FOI Act s11C | The agency's own power to publish; FOI Act s3A | Archives Act s3(7) and s31
- Trigger | A standing duty | Access given to a document under s11A | A decision to publish or give access outside the FOI Act | Time: the record reaches its open access period
- What | The plan, the s8(2) categories and anything else the agency chooses | The information in the documents released | Whatever the power allows | Commonwealth records that still exist

- Main limits | Exempt matter and information another law restricts (s8C) | Personal and business information if publication would be unreasonable, and others (s11C(1)) | The Privacy Act, secrecy laws and the limits of the power | Exempt records under the Archives Act

```

## The Information Publication Scheme

Part II of the [Freedom of Information Act 1982](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text) sets out the scheme. Section 8(1) begins: "An agency must prepare a plan". The plan shows what the agency proposes to publish, how and to whom, and how it will otherwise comply with Part II. Section 8(2) then lists what the agency must publish, starting with the plan itself. Beyond the list, the agency may publish other information it holds (s8(4)).

```figure
type: checklist
title: What every agency must publish under section 8(2)
caption: Section 8(2) of the FOI Act. An agency is not required to publish exempt matter, or information another law restricts (s8C).
- The IPS plan | s8(2)(a), prepared under s8(1)
- The structure of the organisation | s8(2)(b), for example an organisation chart
- Functions and powers | s8(2)(c), as far as practicable, including decision-making powers that affect the public
- Statutory appointments | s8(2)(d), other than APS employees
- Annual report information | s8(2)(e), from reports laid before the Parliament
- How to comment on policy proposals | s8(2)(f), and to whom
- Information routinely released under FOI | s8(2)(g), except personal or business information that would be unreasonable to publish
- Information routinely given to the Parliament | s8(2)(h), in response to its requests and orders
- FOI contact details | s8(2)(i), for an officer who can be contacted about access
- Operational information | s8(2)(j) and s8A: the rules, guidelines, practices and precedents used in decisions that affect the public
```

Operational information needs the most care. Section 8A defines it as information the agency holds to help it make decisions or recommendations affecting members of the public, and gives the example of its rules, guidelines, practices and precedents. It excludes information the public can already get some other way. Section 10 gives the duty teeth: if operational information is not published and a person could lawfully have avoided a prejudice by knowing it, they must not be subjected to that prejudice. An unpublished rule cannot be held against someone who could not see it.

Four other duties shape the scheme. Published information must be "accurate, up-to-date and complete" (s8B). It must be published to members of the public generally "on a website", by making it available for download, by linking to another site, or by saying how to obtain it (s8D(2), (3)). A charge is allowed only where the person does not download the information, and only to reimburse specific reproduction or other incidental costs (s8D(4)). And the agency must review the operation of its scheme, together with the Information Commissioner, from time to time and in any case within 5 years after its last review (s9). In everything it does under Part II, the agency must have regard to the objects of the Act and the Commissioner's guidelines (s9A, s93A(2)).

This is an illustration. Ethan keeps an IPS register: each s8(2) category, the page that meets it, the NRIO area that owns the content and the date it was last checked. NRIO's own rule, not the Act's, is a check of every page each year and of the affected pages after any change in structure, law or practice.

## The disclosure log

Section 11C applies when an agency gives a person access to a document under section 11A. The agency must publish the information in the document to members of the public generally on a website, in one of the same three ways (s11C(3)). It must do so "within 10 working days after the day the person is given access" (s11C(6)). A working day is any day other than a Saturday, a Sunday or a public holiday in the place where the publishing is done (s11C(7)). The rules on charges match the scheme's (s11C(4), (5)).

Three practical points follow from the wording.

- **The clock runs from access, not from the decision.** When information is held back until a third party's review rights run out (Chapter 5), its release starts a second 10 working days.
- **Only access under section 11A counts.** Access given under APP 12, information given outside the Act, and documents refused do not trigger section 11C.
- **Every later release counts too.** If an internal review or IC review leads to more access, that access needs its own entry.

The log describes what was released, not who asked. The decision notice is a letter to the applicant, not a document released under section 11A, and the applicant's name and contact details are personal information. Describe the request in neutral words and leave the applicant unnamed.


## What stays off the log

The exceptions in section 11C(1) are not the exemptions in Part IV. A document released to an applicant can still hold information that would be unreasonable to publish to everyone. The section excludes:

- "personal information about any person, if it would be unreasonable to publish the information" (s11C(1)(a));
- information about a person's business, commercial, financial or professional affairs, if publishing it would be unreasonable (s11C(1)(b));
- other information of a kind the Information Commissioner determines by legislative instrument, if publishing it would be unreasonable (s11C(1)(c), (2));
- any information that it is "not reasonably practicable to publish" because of the extent of the editing needed to remove the first three kinds (s11C(1)(d)).

The clearest case is a request for the applicant's own personal information. This is an illustration. About 60 of NRIO's roughly 140 requests a year are of this kind. The documents released are mostly about the applicant, so publishing them would usually be unreasonable, and few of them reach the log.

The question is a fresh one each time. A finding that release to the applicant was not unreasonable under section 47F usually carries over to the log, especially for officials doing official work, but not always. Ask whether publication to everyone, permanently and in searchable form, would be unreasonable, and write down the answer.

Section 90 protects the Commonwealth and its officers from actions for defamation, breach of confidence and infringement of copyright for publishing in good faith in the belief that Part II or section 11C requires or permits it (s90(1)(a)). Section 92(1)(a) gives similar protection from criminal liability. Neither section mentions privacy. Personal information that section 11C does not require you to publish is still governed by the Privacy Act and APP 6 (Chapter 7), and publishing it by mistake may be a data breach (Chapter 8).

## Releasing information without a request

The FOI Act is not the only way to give information out. Section 3A applies where a Minister or officer has a power to publish or give access apart from the Act, even to an exempt document. Parliament does not intend the Act "to limit that power, or to prevent or discourage the exercise of that power" (s3A(2)), whether or not anyone has made a request.

Before releasing outside the Act, check four things:

1. **The power.** Who at your agency may release this kind of information, under its instructions and delegations?
2. **Other laws.** The Privacy Act, any secrecy provision and any duty of confidence still apply.
3. **Other people's information.** No consultation process runs outside the Act, so release only what concerns the agency or the person asking.
4. **The record.** What was released, to whom, when, why and on whose authority.

Release in good faith under another power is protected from the same civil actions (s90(1)(c)) and from criminal liability (s92(1)(c)). It gives the person no review rights, so a person who wants a reviewable decision can still make an FOI request.

This is an illustration. After the decision, Ruth Adeyemi pointed unsuccessful applicants who asked her team for their own scores to the feedback Sam Reid's team already offers outside the FOI Act: a written summary of each applicant's own scores and reasons. Each summary carries one applicant's information only, and Sam's team records each one. Each applicant answered this way is one fewer FOI request for the team to process, and none of these letters goes on the disclosure log, because none is access under section 11A.

## The open access period

The [Archives Act 1983](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02796/latest/text) opens Commonwealth records to the public after a set time. For a record that came into existence after 2000, the open access period starts on "1 January in the year that is 21 years after the creation year" (s3(7), table item 13). Cabinet notebooks (s22A) and records containing Census information (s22B) have longer periods. The old "30-year rule" does not describe records made after 2000.

Once a record is in the open access period, is in the care of the Archives or the custody of a Commonwealth institution, and is not an exempt record, "the Archives must cause the record to be made available for public access" (s31(1)). An agency that still holds such a record must make arrangements with the Archives so that it can (s31(2)). From then on the FOI Act steps back: section 12(1)(a) denies access under Part III to a document in the open access period unless it contains personal information.

This is an illustration. The RCFP documents were made in 2027, so they enter the open access period on 1 January 2048 (2027 + 21). The legal advice refused under section 42 today will then be tested against the Archives Act's own exempt-record rules, not against Liam's decision. Only records kept that long will be there to open. Which survive is decided by disposal under an authority from the National Archives (Chapter 2), never by anyone's preference.

## Ethan's updates after the RCFP decision

This is an illustration. Ethan opened a log entry in the case register on Wednesday 28 July 2027. The statutory date was Wednesday 11 August, 10 working days later. NRIO's own target is five working days, Wednesday 4 August, so that a problem found in checking still leaves time to fix it. No public holiday fell in these weeks where NRIO publishes.

He built the entry with the held information still removed. Priya Shah, the case officer, checked it against the schedule of deletions and the consultation register (Chapter 5) before it went up. The entry described the request in a sentence, gave the date of access and offered each document as a searchable, edited PDF. When the held information was released on Wednesday 1 September, the second 10 working days ended on Wednesday 15 September; Ethan published the second entry on Wednesday 8 September.

He also worked through the IPS register:

- **Routine releases (s8(2)(g)).** One request does not make a release routine. Ethan noted the category for review if the same kinds of document are requested again.
- **Operational information (s8(2)(j)).** With Sam, he confirmed that the rules and practices NRIO uses to assess grant applications are published.
- **Accuracy (s8B).** The page on review rights still named the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The Administrative Review Tribunal replaced it on 14 October 2024, so Ethan corrected the page and logged the fix.

## When it goes wrong: the hidden columns

This is an illustration. The first entry nearly went wrong in an ordinary way. The edited spreadsheet the journalist received was a PDF. Wanting readers to sort and search it, Ethan exported a spreadsheet file from the working copy for the log instead. In that copy, the three columns deleted under section 47F, the contact person's name, phone number and email address for each of the 186 applications (Chapter 6), had been hidden, not removed. On screen the export looked exactly like the release and matched the schedule of deletions. On Tuesday 3 August, the day before it was due to go up, Priya noticed that its column letters jumped from D to H. Anyone who unhid the columns would have had the names, mostly mobile numbers and often personal email addresses of 186 people, most of them volunteers.

Published, the cost would have been large and permanent. Liam had withheld that information, so section 11C never applied to it, and section 90 offers no protection for a privacy failure. NRIO would have had to assess within 30 days whether this was an eligible data breach (s26WH; Chapter 8), its second RCFP assessment within two months. Removing the file would not recall copies already downloaded. And 186 people who had trusted NRIO with their details might have learned of it from the newspaper. As it was, Ethan replaced the file with the released PDF, and the entry went up on target on Wednesday 4 August.

Priya caught it by chance: her check compared what showed on screen. The control is a publication check by a second officer, and NRIO wrote it into its procedure:

- publish only from the files actually released, never from a new export of a working copy;
- use formats in which deleted text is gone, not covered or hidden;
- open the log copy and search it for a sample of the deleted words, and look for hidden rows, columns, sheets, comments and file properties;
- compare it against the schedule of deletions and the consultation register, and record who checked and when.

## Exercise: what goes on the log

This is an illustration. For each item, decide whether it goes on the RCFP log entry, in which entry, and why.

| Item | Information |
| --- | --- |
| A | The brief of Fri 7 May 2027 with its ranked list, released on 28 July with the 155 unsuccessful applicants' names and one grantee's finance comments deleted, and the held information removed |
| B | The assessment spreadsheet. The journalist's copy of 28 July had the contact columns and the 155 names deleted, but, through the error in Chapter 5, the sporting club's row went out with its scores and comments still in it before its review period ended |
| C | The file note of the 19 May call and the emails with the Minister's office, naming two advisers. One adviser's name was held until 1 September |
| D | NRIO's legal advice on the program's authority, refused under section 42 |
| E | Liam's decision notice, addressed to the journalist at the newspaper's email address |
| F | The held information released on 1 September: the scores and comments of Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. and three other grantees, one of them the sporting club, and the objecting adviser's name |

Then give the latest lawful date for each entry.

### Worked answer

**A: first entry.** Publish the brief as released. The deletions made under section 47G stay deleted, and the held information stays out until it is released.

**B: first entry, as decided, not as sent.** Publish the spreadsheet with the contact columns and the 155 names deleted. Leave out the sporting club's row, with the other held information, even though the journalist received it in error. Ruth judged that, while the club's review period ran, publishing it would be unreasonable (s11C(1)(b)) and would spread the harm section 27(7) exists to prevent. She records that judgement on the file. The row goes into the second entry once the club's review rights have run out.

**C: first entry, one name later.** Publish the note and the emails, with the objecting adviser's name removed. The names and positions concern official work, and Liam found their disclosure not unreasonable. Ask the s11C(1)(a) question again, record that the answer is the same, and publish.

**D: nothing.** Section 11C covers only information in documents to which access was given. The log may say that one document was refused, without describing its content.

**E: not on the log.** The notice is not a document released under section 11A. Describe the request in neutral words and do not name the journalist.

**F: second entry.** All of it was released on 1 September, after the third parties' review period ended, including the sporting club's row from item B.

**Dates.** Counting working days from the day after access, with no public holiday in the period:

| Entry | Access given | 10 working days | Latest date |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First | Wed 28 Jul 2027 | Thu 29 Jul to Wed 11 Aug | Wed 11 Aug 2027 |
| Second | Wed 1 Sep 2027 | Thu 2 Sep to Wed 15 Sep | Wed 15 Sep 2027 |

Each period counts two full weeks: 29 July, 30 July, 2 to 6 August and 9 to 11 August make 2 + 5 + 3 = 10 working days. If the IC review in Chapter 6 leads to more access, a third entry is due 10 working days after that access.

Chapter 10 turns from what the team publishes to how it runs: the register, templates, checks and people that keep every one of these dates.
