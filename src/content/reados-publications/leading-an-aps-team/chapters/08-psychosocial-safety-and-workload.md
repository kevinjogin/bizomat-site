A threatening phone call can feel like the worst part of an ordinary day. In law it is a work health and safety matter. The Work Health and Safety Act 2011 says "health means physical and psychological health" (s 4), and since 1 April 2023 the Commonwealth WHS Regulations have set out how psychosocial risks must be managed. Abuse from callers and a workload that never eases are risks to health, not features of the job.

This is an illustration. On Monday 16 November 2026, a caller threatened Sophie Grant, and the incident was reported that day. This chapter follows what Hannah did next: who holds which duty, how the risk was rated and controlled, why workload belongs in the same conversation, and how the team was consulted.

## Who holds which duty

The [Work Health and Safety Act 2011](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2011A00137/latest/text) gives different duties to different people. Chapter 1 introduced them. Here is what each means for a team.

The primary duty sits with the person conducting a business or undertaking (PCBU). Section 19(1) says a PCBU "must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of" the workers it engages and those whose work it influences or directs. The Act binds the Commonwealth (s 10). In an agency, the duty reaches the desk through WHS policies, the WHS area and managers.

"Reasonably practicable" is defined in section 18. It weighs the likelihood of the hazard or risk, the degree of harm, what the duty holder knows or ought reasonably to know about it and how to control it, whether suitable controls are available, and only then the cost, including whether it is grossly disproportionate to the risk. Knowledge matters. Once a hazard is reported, the agency knows about it.

Officers must exercise due diligence to ensure the PCBU complies (s 27(1)). That includes reasonable steps to ensure it has "appropriate resources and processes" to eliminate or minimise risks, and processes for receiving information about incidents, hazards and risks and "responding in a timely way" (s 27(5)(c)–(d)). A Commonwealth officer is someone who makes, or participates in making, decisions that affect the whole or a substantial part of a Commonwealth business or undertaking (s 247(1)). A section manager is unlikely to fit that description, but ask your WHS area rather than assume.

Every worker, Hannah included, must take reasonable care for their own health and safety and that of others, comply so far as they reasonably can with reasonable instructions, and co-operate with notified WHS policies and procedures (s 28).

Hannah sits in the middle. She is a worker. She directs the team's work on the PCBU's behalf. And her reports are the information officers need in order to respond. If she does not pass on what she knows, the people who can fund a fix never hear of it.

## Psychosocial hazards in the Regulations

The [Work Health and Safety Regulations 2011](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2011L02664/latest/text) have contained a Division on psychosocial risks since 1 April 2023. A psychosocial hazard arises from or relates to the design or management of work, a work environment, plant at a workplace, or "workplace interactions or behaviours", and "may cause psychological harm" (r 55A). A psychosocial risk is "a risk to the health or safety of a worker or other person arising from a psychosocial hazard" (r 55B).

Regulation 55C says a PCBU must "manage psychosocial risks in accordance with Part 3.1" and "implement the control measures required by regulation 55D". Part 3.1 is the general method. The duty holder must identify reasonably foreseeable hazards (r 34). They must eliminate risks so far as is reasonably practicable or, if that is not reasonably practicable, minimise them so far as is reasonably practicable (r 35). They must follow the hierarchy of control measures (r 36), keep controls effective (r 37), and review them, including when a control is not working or a new hazard or risk is identified (r 38).

In choosing controls, the PCBU "must have regard to all relevant matters" (r 55D(2)). The list includes the duration, frequency and severity of exposure; "how the psychosocial hazards may interact or combine"; the design of work, including job demands; the systems of work; workplace interactions or behaviours; and the information, training, instruction and supervision workers receive.

An abusive caller is a workplace interaction. A workload that never eases comes from the design and management of work. One duty covers both.

## What the Code of Practice adds

The [Work Health and Safety (Managing Psychosocial Hazards at Work) Code of Practice 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L01380/latest/text) was approved by the Minister for Employment and Workplace Relations on 27 October 2024, under section 274 of the Act, and registered on 1 November 2024. It is guidance, but it carries weight. In a prosecution, an approved code "is admissible in the proceeding as evidence of whether or not a duty or obligation under this Act has been complied with" (s 275(2)).

Read it with its own key. The Code's note on how to use it says that "must", "requires" and "mandatory" "indicate a legal requirement exists", while "should" is used "to indicate a recommended course of action". When the Code says you must consult workers about psychosocial risks, it restates the law. When it says you should record the process, it recommends good practice.

Section 1.1 lists common hazards, among them "Job demands", "Low job control", "Poor support", "Lack of role clarity", "Poor organisational justice" and "Violence and aggression". Section 3.1 adds that "Some hazards by themselves may cause serious harm, such as experiencing workplace violence." Section 4.2 asks you to assess each risk by the duration, frequency and severity of exposure.

## The call on 16 November

This is an illustration. At about 11.40 am on Monday 16 November 2026, Sophie took a call from a man angry that an NRIO facility had closed for repairs. He swore at her, demanded her surname and said he knew where the office was and would wait for her after work. Sophie ended the call and told Marcus.

Marcus took Sophie off the phones and told Hannah. Hannah's first conversation with Sophie was about Sophie: was she all right, did she want to go home, was there someone to call. The practical steps followed in the order NRIO's fictional procedures set.

## Rating the risk

This is an illustration. The call notes from August to October held 14 calls marked abusive, about one a week, and 3 of them included a threat. None had been reported as an incident. The controls on 16 November were thin. An officer could transfer a caller to a team leader if one was free, and with Daniel's position empty, one was free less often. There was no guidance on ending an abusive call, no alert system and no habit of reporting.

NRIO's fictional WHS procedure uses the same 5 × 5 matrix and bands as its risk framework, with descriptors written for harm to people. The levels used in this chapter are:

- likelihood 2, unlikely: could happen, but not expected in a given year;
- likelihood 3, possible: could happen in a given year;
- likelihood 4, likely: expected to happen in a given year;
- consequence 3, moderate: harm that needs professional support or some days off work;
- consequence 4, major: serious psychological injury, or weeks off work.

The risk is: "A Client Service Officer is harmed by an abusive or threatening caller." At that rate of exposure, with so little to cut a call short or support the officer, serious harm was expected within a year: likelihood 4. A threat of harm can cause serious psychological injury: consequence 4. The rating is 4 × 4 = 16, High.

The [Risk Rating (5 × 5 matrix)](/tools/risk-score/) tool does the same arithmetic. It multiplies likelihood (1 to 5) by consequence (1 to 5) and bands the score Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14 and High 15 to 25, with a reminder to use your organisation's own descriptors. That reminder is the important part. The score is only as sound as the judgement behind each factor.

A WHS rating is not a decision to accept a risk. Under Part 3.1 the question is not whether the score is low enough, but whether the risk has been eliminated or minimised so far as is reasonably practicable. A Medium score after controls means the controls must be kept working and reviewed. Record both factors, too. An 8 made of frequent minor harm (4 × 2) and an 8 made of rarer serious harm (2 × 4) need different responses.

## Controls, from most to least protective

Regulation 36 sets the order. If a risk cannot reasonably be eliminated, it must be minimised first by substitution, isolation or engineering controls, then by administrative controls, and last by personal protective equipment, combining controls where one is not enough. The Code says changes to the system of work, such as "rostering, working hours, task rotation and breaks", can achieve the higher-order controls (section 5.3). It also warns that "Administrative control measures and PPE do not control the hazard at the source."

This is an illustration. NRIO cannot stop people calling, or guarantee that none will be abusive. It can change how much abuse reaches an officer, for how long and with what support. Hannah, Marcus and the WHS adviser drafted the controls below, and the Director approved them after the team was consulted.

```figure
type: layers
title: The section's call controls, highest first
caption: This is an illustration. The order follows regulation 36. Higher layers change the work; lower layers rely on people following a procedure.
- Substitute | A caller with a history of threats gets a management plan: contact in writing only, handled by a team leader.
- Change the system of work | Officers rotate between phones and email every two hours, a team leader is kept free for escalations in the Monday morning peak, and anyone who takes an abusive call has a break.
- Engineer | The phone system shows an alert before a flagged caller is answered, and transfers to the team leader with one touch.
- Administrative controls | A written procedure for ending an abusive call after one warning; first names only; de-escalation training; a two-minute report for every abusive call; a debrief the same day.
- Personal protective equipment | None identified for this hazard.
```

Two controls follow the Code's own examples, which include "management plans where a client is known to have a history of aggression or violence" and letting workers refuse or suspend service when people do not meet the expected standard of behaviour (Appendix B). The examples are guidance. The requirement is to do what is reasonably practicable in your own workplace.

With these controls, Hannah and the WHS adviser rated the risk again. Fewer officers meet a known abusive caller, abusive calls end sooner and support comes the same day: likelihood 3 and consequence 3, so 3 × 3 = 9, Medium. Abuse can be reduced, not removed, so the risk stays on the register.

## Workload is a hazard too

Workload is the psychosocial hazard a manager controls most directly. The Code's description of job demands includes "Unreasonable or excessive time pressures or role overload" (section 3.1), and r 55D(2)(c) names "the design of work, including job demands and tasks". Hazards also combine. An officer who has taken calls back to back for three hours has less in reserve for an abusive one.

The Fair Work Act 2009 makes the link from another side. An employer must not request or require a full-time employee to work more than 38 hours a week "unless the additional hours are reasonable" (s 62(1)). The first factor in deciding is "any risk to employee health and safety from working the additional hours" (s 62(3)(a)).

This is an illustration. Chapter 2 sized the section's handling work at 7,120 hours a year, about 5.6 FTE, before team-leader duties, service-level cover and the Monday morning peak. One of the six officer positions is empty until 15 February 2027. Even with every other position filled, five officers give 5 × 1,275 = 6,375 productive hours a year, and 7,120 ÷ 6,375 = 111.7 per cent. Any leave or illness makes it worse. Marcus reports that officers have worked through breaks on most Mondays since late October, and that he and Leila are taking calls most afternoons.

The signs Hannah watches for are in her own team's data: queues that grow week on week, people working through breaks or late, leave nobody can take, rising rework and more unplanned absence. The controls are mostly decisions a manager can make or ask for. Plan from demand and show the gap in hours. Agree with the Director what waits when demand exceeds capacity. Protect breaks. Ask about workload in every one-to-one. Escalate early, with numbers and options. Officers above Hannah must ensure "appropriate resources and processes" (s 27(5)(c)), and they can only do that if managers tell them the size of the gap.

## Consulting the team

Consultation is a legal duty. Section 47 requires the PCBU, so far as is reasonably practicable, to consult workers "who are, or are likely to be, directly affected" by a WHS matter. Under section 48, consultation means sharing relevant information, giving workers a reasonable opportunity to express their views and "contribute to the decision-making process", taking their views into account and telling them the outcome in a timely manner. If workers are represented by a health and safety representative (HSR), "the consultation must involve that representative" (s 48(2)). Section 49 requires consultation, among other times, "when identifying hazards and assessing risks", when deciding how to eliminate or minimise them, and "when proposing changes that may affect the health or safety of workers".

This is an illustration. Hannah spoke to Sophie privately first, because she was the person most affected and could choose what was shared. On Monday 23 November, Hannah took the draft assessment to the team meeting, with the HSR for the section's work group present. She shared the call-note figures without names, the rating and the draft controls, and asked what was missing.

The team changed the plan. Several officers asked to give first names only, which became the rule. Leila pointed out that abusive callers often follow up in writing, so the alert now shows on correspondence too. Ruby asked to be walked through the call-ending procedure before her next Monday peak. The Director approved the final controls on Thursday 26 November, and they started on Monday 30 November.

Hannah wrote down what was raised, what changed and why, and sent it to the team. The Code says you "should record your risk management process and the outcomes, including your consultation with workers" (section 7). She set a review for Monday 1 March 2027. Regulation 38 requires an earlier one if a control is not working, a new hazard or risk is identified, or consultation shows a review is needed.

## When it goes wrong: "part of the job"

This is an illustration. The call notes Hannah read on 19 November showed that Sophie's caller had rung twice in October. Two different officers took the calls, and both notes read much the same: abusive, swore at officer, call ended. Neither officer reported an incident, flagged the record or told a team leader. When Hannah asked why, she heard the answer she heard about the other abusive calls: it's part of the job, and the form takes longer than the call.

Each choice made sense on the day. Together, they hid a caller whose behaviour was escalating from abuse to a threat. Sophie took his third call with no warning. Fourteen abusive calls in three months produced no incident reports, so the WHS area had no data, no officer above Hannah had anything to respond to, and each officer absorbed the effect alone. Hannah had not asked either. She had never told the team that every abusive call should be reported, so the habit she inherited carried on.

This is not a question of attitude. Workplace interactions and behaviours are a named source of psychosocial hazard (r 55A). The PCBU must identify reasonably foreseeable hazards (r 34). Officers' due diligence depends on processes for receiving information about incidents and responding in a timely way (s 27(5)(d)). An incident nobody reports does not exist for any of those purposes.

The controls that would have caught it are now in place: a two-minute report for every abusive call, however it ended; an alert on the caller's record; a weekly review of reports by the team leaders; and a monthly count in Hannah's report to the Director. Hannah also changed what she says. Abuse is not part of the job. Handling it safely is.

## Exercise: rate and control two more risks

This is an illustration. Use NRIO's descriptors and bands above. For each risk, write it as one sentence, rate it with existing controls, choose controls in the order regulation 36 sets, rate it again, and say whom you consult and when you review.

- **Risk A: workload until the vacancy is filled.** Use the figures in the workload section: five officers at most until 15 February, officers working through breaks on most Mondays, and team leaders taking calls most afternoons.
- **Risk B: the new controls concentrate exposure.** Abusive callers, and callers under a management plan, now go to Marcus or Leila. Daniel's position used to take escalated calls too, and it is empty until Tessa Morgan starts on 15 February.

### Worked answer

**Risk A.** "Officers and team leaders are harmed by sustained high job demands until the vacancy is filled."

- The gap: 7,120 − 6,375 = 745 hours a year, and 745 ÷ 52 = 14.3 hours a week, averaged over the year.
- With existing controls: at this pace, harm that needs support or days off is expected within the year (likelihood 4), and it would be moderate (consequence 3). 4 × 3 = 12, Medium.
- Controls, highest first. System of work: Hannah and the Director agree what waits, such as non-urgent projects, and Hannah rosters Monday breaks as firmly as she rosters calls. Resources: she asks the Director for temporary help of 15 hours a week until 15 February, which covers the 14.3-hour gap. Administrative: a weekly workload check with the team leaders, and the queue figures in her monthly report.
- After controls, if the help is approved: likelihood 2 and consequence 3, so 2 × 3 = 6, Low. If it is not, the other controls bring likelihood to 3 at best: 3 × 3 = 9, Medium, and Hannah tells the Director so in writing. A rating is only as real as the controls behind it.
- Consult the team and the HSR on the priorities and the roster (s 49(b)). Review weekly against the queue, and before the temporary help ends, because that change could bring the risk back (r 38(2)(b)).

**Risk B.** "A team leader is harmed by repeated exposure to the most abusive callers."

- This is a risk created by a control. Regulation 55D(2)(a) asks about the frequency and duration of exposure, and the Code asks whether controls are working "without creating new risks" (section 6).
- With the new call controls only: repeated contact with the worst callers could cause serious harm (consequence 4), and it could happen in a given year (likelihood 3). 3 × 4 = 12, Medium.
- Controls, highest first. Substitute: callers under a management plan move to written contact, which takes the most hostile calls off the phone. System of work: Marcus, Leila and Hannah share the escalation role in rotation, nobody holds it for more than two hours at a stretch, and a break follows any abusive call. Tessa joins the rotation after training. Administrative: team leaders get the same debriefs as officers, and training in handling escalated calls, which the Code lists as an example control (Appendix B).
- After controls: likelihood 2 and consequence 3, so 2 × 3 = 6, Low.
- Consult Marcus, Leila and the HSR. Review before Tessa joins the rotation, because that changes a system of work (r 38(2)(b) and (3)(b)), and at the scheduled review on 1 March 2027.

All four ratings fall inside the tool's bands: 12 in Medium (7 to 14), 9 in Medium, and 6 in Low (1 to 6).
