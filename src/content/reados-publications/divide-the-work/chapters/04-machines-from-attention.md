Smith's third reason for the productivity of divided work is the most interesting, because it is not about doing the same thing faster but about doing it differently. A person whose whole attention is on one operation is more likely to notice what makes it slow, and to invent a simpler way. His example was a boy employed to open and close a valve on one of the early steam engines, who found that by tying a string from the valve to another moving part of the engine, he could make the engine do the job for him and go and play.

The story is probably improved in the telling, but the observation behind it is familiar to anyone who has run a workshop. The best fixtures, jigs and gauges are usually invented by the person who does the job every day. This chapter follows the B-series welder's fixture from idea to production: how to cost it, how to check that the saving is real, and how a good fixture can still go wrong.

## The idea arrives

This is an illustration. After about thirty B-series frames, the welder has noticed where the time goes. Each frame is fitted up on a flat welding table: legs clamped to cross-rails with G-clamps and squares, diagonals measured, tacked, measured again, adjusted, tacked, then welded in a sequence that limits distortion. Even with experience, fit-up and squaring take close to an hour, and about one frame in twelve needs straightening after welding because it has pulled out of square.

The welder proposes a fixture: a stiff base plate with hardened locating blocks for the four legs and the rails, quick-acting toggle clamps, and stops that hold the frame square during tacking and welding. He sketches it, and estimates that it would cut fit-up and straightening by about **50 minutes** a frame and remove most of the rework for distortion.

The idea is exactly the kind Smith had in mind. A generalist welding one frame in three would have met the same problems, but less often, and with less reason to think about a permanent solution. A welder who fits up fifteen frames a week faces the problem fifteen times, and has both the motive and the knowledge to solve it.

## Cost the fixture

The owner and welder cost the fixture at **A$4,800** excluding GST: plate, blocks and stops at about A$1,900, toggle clamps and fasteners at about A$1,040, and about 30 hours of the welder's own time at A$62 an hour, A$1,860. The welder will build it in time released by the switching savings in Chapter 3, so the build does not take hours away from frames.

The [jig and fixture return tool](/tools/jig-fixture-roi/) in BIZOMAT Tools estimates the case. From the investment, the time saved per run, the runs per month, the labour rate, the monthly scrap and rework saving and the fixture's monthly maintenance, it calculates the monthly labour saving, the net monthly benefit, the annual benefit, the payback in months and the first-year return. Its checks remind the user to confirm safety, repeatability and continued usage, and to measure the saving on the first production runs.

At fifteen benches a week, there are about 62 frames a month. The owner estimates the rework saving at **A$300 a month**, the cost of straightening and occasional re-welding of distorted frames, and maintenance at **A$40 a month** for clamp wear and block replacement. With the welder's estimate of 50 minutes saved, the tool gives a net monthly benefit of A$3,463 and a payback of **1.4 months**.

## The failure case: the saving that shrank

Encouraged by the estimate, the owner approves the fixture. It is built in three weeks and enters production. The welder is pleased with it: frames come off square, and there has been no straightening since it started.

But when the owner times the first twenty frames on the fixture, as the tool's check recommends, the saving is not 50 minutes. It is about **35 minutes**. The difference is loading and unloading. On the flat table, the welder lifted parts into position as he fitted them; in the fixture, every part must be seated against its locating blocks and the toggles closed, and the finished frame must be released and lifted out over the stops. The welder's estimate counted the fit-up time saved, and missed the handling time added.

Rerun with 35 minutes, the tool gives:

- monthly labour saving **A$2,242**;
- net monthly benefit **A$2,502**;
- annual benefit **A$30,028**;
- payback **1.9 months**;
- first-year return **526%**.

In this case the shortfall did not change the decision; the fixture still pays back in under two months. But the habit of measuring the first runs is what protects the business when the shortfall is larger. Estimates of time saved by a fixture are often optimistic in exactly this way, because the person estimating sees the time removed from the difficult part of the job and not the time added to the easy part.

### How it is detected and corrected

The detection is a timed trial on the first production runs, compared with the estimate. The correction here is simply to record the measured saving and use it in planning: the welding station time falls from about 151 minutes at the 150th frame to about **116 minutes** with the fixture, which is the figure Chapter 6 uses for balance. The welder also makes two small changes after the trial, a lifting point on the frame and a relief in one stop, which recover a few minutes of the handling time.

## The second failure case: locking in an error

Three weeks into production, the inspection at the polishing station finds that the leg frames are consistently about 1.5 mm narrower across the top rail than the drawing. They are square, and they are all the same, which is why the first few were passed without comment. But the folded tops are now a tight fit over the frames, and one top has been scratched forcing it on.

The cause is weld shrinkage. The fixture's stops hold the frame at its drawing dimensions during welding, but as the welds cool they pull the frame slightly narrower once it is released. On the flat table, the welder used to allow for this by tacking slightly wide. The fixture had removed that judgement and replaced it with a fixed dimension.

This is a characteristic risk of fixtures, and of divided work generally. A fixture makes every frame the same, which is its purpose, and so it repeats an error just as faithfully as it repeats a correct dimension. The generalist's whole-bench view would probably have caught the fit problem on the first bench, because the same person would have folded the top and welded the frame. At stations, the person who sees the problem is two steps away from the person who causes it.

### How it is detected and corrected

The detection is a first-article check of the fixture's output against the drawing and against the mating part, not just against itself. The owner adds a check to the welding station: the first frame of each day is measured across the top rail and trial-fitted with a top. The correction is to move the stops out by the measured shrinkage, with a note on the fixture recording the allowance and the reason. The [Fixtures That Repeat chapter on defining a fixture as a production method](/reados/publications/fixtures-that-repeat/define-the-fixture-as-a-production-method/) describes the wider discipline of proving a fixture before relying on it.

## Smith's qualification: not all invention comes from the operator

Smith did not claim that every improvement came from the person doing the job. He noted that many came from the makers of machines, and some from people whose trade was to observe rather than to make, who could combine ideas from different fields. In a small business those roles still exist. The welder knows where the time goes; the owner knows what the fixture can cost and what the next design will look like; a supplier of clamping systems knows what is available off the shelf; a customer may know what tolerance actually matters.

The owner therefore treats fixture ideas as a short process rather than a single person's project:

## What the fixture changes about the division of work

The fixture does more than save 35 minutes a frame. It changes how much skill the welding station needs. With the fixture, fit-up is mostly placing parts against blocks and closing clamps; squaring is built in. A second person can now learn to weld B-series frames much faster than the first welder did, because the hardest judgement, holding the frame square through the weld sequence, is carried by the fixture.

That has two sides. It makes cover and cross-training easier, which Chapter 9 depends on. And it moves some knowledge out of the welder's hands and into the fixture, where it is visible and maintainable, which reduces the business's dependence on one person. But it also makes the station's work narrower, which is precisely the effect of division of labour that Smith worried about. The welder who invented the fixture had to understand the whole problem. The person who operates it does not. Keeping the inventor involved in the fixture's upkeep and in the next design is one way to keep that understanding alive.

## When the volume is too low for a fixture

The fixture's case depends on volume. At fifteen benches a week it pays back in under two months. The same fixture at the old volume of six benches a week, about 25 frames a month, with a proportionally smaller rework saving of about A$120 a month, gives a net monthly benefit of about A$984 and a payback of about 4.9 months: still a good case. Fixtures for the low-volume custom work would be a different matter, because each custom design runs only a few times. The extent of the market limits machines as well as people, a point Chapter 5 takes up.

## Making room for invention

If the best fixture ideas come from the stations, the business should make it easy for them to arrive. The owner's arrangements are modest and practical:

- **Time.** Released switching time at each station, a couple of hours a week, is reserved for improvement work, with the owner's agreement on what it is used for. Without time, ideas stay as complaints.
- **A small budget.** Each station can spend up to a few hundred dollars on a trial without a formal case, provided the trial is timed. Larger items go through the costing and trial steps above.
- **Recognition.** The fixture carries the name of the person who designed it on its record sheet, and the measured saving is reported at the monthly meeting. People notice whether their ideas are taken seriously.
- **A record of what did not work.** Trials that fail are written down with the reason, so the same idea is not tried twice and nobody is blamed for an honest experiment.

None of this is elaborate, and it costs far less than the fixture saves. It also counters one of the risks of narrow work. A person who is invited to improve their station is exercising exactly the judgement and invention that Smith feared a very narrow job would dull.

## Exercise: a polishing jig

The polisher proposes a simple jig that holds the folded bench top at a comfortable angle for polishing the coved corners, estimated to save **12 minutes** a bench and to cost **A$1,600** including materials and build time, excluding GST. There is no rework saving, and maintenance is negligible. At 62 benches a month and A$62 an hour, use the jig and fixture return tool's method to find the monthly benefit and the payback. Then name two things to check on the first runs, one about the saving and one about something other than time.

**Worked answer.** The monthly labour saving is 12 ÷ 60 × 62 × A$62 = **A$768.80**, which with no scrap saving and no maintenance is also the net monthly benefit. The payback is A$1,600 ÷ A$768.80 = **2.1 months**, and the annual benefit is about **A$9,226**.

On the first runs, check the **measured saving**, including the time to place the top in the jig and remove it, as the leg-frame fixture showed. And check something other than time: the polisher's **posture and handling**, since the jig's main benefit may be reducing awkward reaching into the coved corners, which matters for the hazardous manual task risk discussed in Chapter 3, and a jig that saves time but is awkward to load could make that risk worse rather than better.
