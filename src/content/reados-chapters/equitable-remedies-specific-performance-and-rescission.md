When a contract goes wrong, most people think first about money. The other party failed to deliver, so they should pay for the loss. In most commercial disputes, that is exactly what the law offers: compensation designed to put the innocent party in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed. Sometimes, though, a cheque does not solve the problem. A business may need the unique machine it bought, not the price difference. It may need a former partner to stop using its confidential designs today, not damages in two years. It may have been talked into a contract that should never have existed, or signed a document that does not record what everyone actually agreed.

For these situations the law has a second toolbox, developed historically by the courts of **equity**. Its main tools are **specific performance** (an order to do what was promised), **injunctions** (orders to stop doing something, or occasionally to do something), **rescission** (unwinding a contract as if it had never been made) and **rectification** (correcting a written document so it records the real agreement). These remedies are powerful, but they are not automatic. A court has a discretion whether to grant them, and it will consider the conduct of the party asking.

This article explains, in general terms, what each remedy does, when it may be available, the limits on it and what a business can do in advance to keep these options open. It is general information, not legal advice. Equitable remedies depend on detailed facts and are often sought urgently, so take legal advice as soon as one seems relevant.

## Two kinds of remedy

It helps to start with the basic distinction.

**Common law damages** are available as of right once a breach and a loss are proved. The court does not ask whether the claimant deserves them. It asks what the loss was. Damages compensate. They do not punish, and they rarely force anyone to do anything except pay.

**Equitable remedies** developed because damages were sometimes inadequate or because a party's conduct made it unconscionable to insist on strict legal rights. They are **discretionary**: even when the requirements are met, a court may decline to grant them if, for example, the claimant delayed unreasonably, behaved badly or would gain an unfair advantage, or if the order would cause hardship out of proportion to the benefit.

| Remedy | What it does | Typical business situation |
|---|---|---|
| Specific performance | Orders a party to perform its contractual obligation | Sale of land or a unique asset; transfer of shares in a private company |
| Injunction | Orders a party to stop, or occasionally to do, a specific act | Misuse of confidential information; breach of a restraint; interference with exclusive rights |
| Rescission | Unwinds a contract and restores the parties to their prior positions | Contract induced by misrepresentation, duress or undue influence |
| Rectification | Corrects a written document to reflect the actual agreement | A signed contract contains a drafting error both parties overlooked |
| Equitable compensation or account of profits | Monetary relief for breach of equitable obligations | Misuse of confidential information; breach of fiduciary duty |

## Specific performance: holding a party to its promise

Specific performance is an order that a party carry out its contractual obligations. It is the remedy most associated with contracts for the sale of land, where each parcel is treated as unique and money is considered an inadequate substitute. It may also be available for the sale of other unique property, such as a rare item of equipment, a particular business or shares in a private company that cannot be bought elsewhere.

Courts approach specific performance with several limits in mind:

- **Damages must be inadequate.** If money would put the claimant in substantially the same position, for example because identical goods can be bought elsewhere, specific performance is usually refused.
- **The court will not supervise ongoing performance.** Orders requiring continuous cooperation over a long period, such as running a business or carrying out a complex building contract, are generally avoided because the court cannot practically police them.
- **Personal services are not compelled.** A court will not order someone to work for, or employ, a particular person.
- **The claimant must be ready and willing to perform.** A buyer seeking to force a sale must show it can and will pay.
- **Discretionary bars apply.** Delay, hardship to the defendant or third parties, and the claimant's own inequitable conduct can all lead a court to refuse the order.

For most businesses, the practical lesson is that specific performance is realistic mainly for property and unique assets. For construction, supply and services contracts, the usual remedies remain damages and the contractual rights in the agreement itself. If you are buying something that is genuinely irreplaceable to you, such as a specific item of plant built to your design or a key piece of intellectual property, think at the outset about how you would secure it if the other party refused to hand it over. Title, escrow and delivery-on-payment arrangements are often more reliable than relying on a court to order performance later.

## Injunctions: stopping harm before it happens

An **injunction** is an order restraining a person from doing something, or, less commonly, requiring them to do a positive act. In business disputes, injunctions are most often sought to stop:

- the misuse or disclosure of confidential information, such as drawings, customer lists or process know-how;
- a breach of a restraint of trade by a former employee, contractor or vendor of a business;
- interference with exclusive rights, such as a supplier selling to a competitor in breach of an exclusivity clause;
- the infringement of intellectual property rights; or
- a party calling on a bank guarantee or other security in breach of the contract.

Injunctions are often needed quickly, before the harm becomes irreversible. Courts can grant **interlocutory** (interim) injunctions that hold the position until a final hearing. To obtain one, a party generally needs to show a serious question to be tried, and that the **balance of convenience** favours an order, considering how much harm each side would suffer if the order were or were not made and whether damages would adequately compensate.

Two practical points catch businesses by surprise. First, the party seeking an interim injunction is usually required to give an **undertaking as to damages**, a promise to compensate the other side for loss caused by the injunction if it later turns out it should not have been granted. That can be a significant exposure. Second, delay is fatal. A business that knows about a threatened breach and waits months before acting will find it hard to persuade a court that urgent relief is needed.

### Restraints of trade need careful drafting

Restraint clauses illustrate why preparation matters. Courts start from the position that restraints of trade are unenforceable unless they protect a legitimate interest and go no further than reasonably necessary. Restraints that are too long, too wide geographically or protect more than goodwill or confidential information may fail entirely. In New South Wales, legislation allows courts to read down an unreasonable restraint, but in other jurisdictions an overreaching restraint may be struck out. Some contracts use **cascading** clauses that offer alternative periods and areas, but these do not guarantee enforceability. A business that depends on a restraint should have it drafted to protect a specific interest, and should be ready to show why that interest exists. ## Rescission: unwinding a contract

**Rescission** sets a contract aside so that the parties are returned, as far as possible, to the position they were in before it was made. It is available where the contract is voidable, typically because consent was obtained improperly, for example by misrepresentation, duress, undue influence or unconscionable conduct, or in some cases of mistake. The [a signature is not the whole story](/reados/collections/commercial-law-and-contracts/a-signature-is-not-the-whole-story/) article explains these grounds and how they arise.

Rescission is different from termination for breach. Termination ends future obligations under a valid contract and preserves accrued rights, including the right to damages for the breach. Rescission treats the contract as if it should never have bound the parties, and focuses on restoring what each has given.

Several limits apply:

- **Election and affirmation.** A party who learns the facts giving rise to the right to rescind and then acts as if the contract continues, for example by accepting further performance or making payments, may be taken to have affirmed it and lost the right.
- **Restoration.** Equity is flexible here, but the court must be able to achieve substantial restoration, often through adjustments such as accounting for benefits received or payments for use.
- **Third-party rights.** If an innocent third party has acquired rights in the property for value, rescission may not be available against that property.
- **Delay.** As with all equitable relief, unreasonable delay can bar rescission.

Rescission is also not the only response to a misleading pre-contract statement. Under the Australian Consumer Law, misleading or deceptive conduct in trade or commerce can give rise to a range of orders, including damages and orders varying or setting aside a contract, and these statutory remedies can be more flexible than rescission at general law.

## Rectification: when the document is wrong

Sometimes the problem is not the deal but the paperwork. A price schedule was transposed, a clause from a previous contract was left in, or a defined term points to the wrong document. **Rectification** allows a court to correct a written instrument so that it records the agreement the parties actually made.

The requirements are demanding. Generally, the party seeking rectification must show, by convincing proof, that the parties had a common intention on the point at the time the document was signed, that this intention continued up to signing and that the document fails to record it because of a mistake. Rectification is about correcting the record of an agreement. It is not a way to improve a bargain or to substitute what a party now wishes had been agreed.

Rectification can also be available in limited circumstances where only one party was mistaken and the other knew of the mistake and took advantage of it, but this is narrower and fact-sensitive.

The practical value of rectification depends on records. A business that can produce the negotiation emails, marked-up drafts and meeting notes showing what both sides agreed is in a far stronger position than one relying on recollection. This is a strong argument for keeping the contract history, not just the signed version. Courts also interpret contracts in context, so in some cases an apparent drafting error can be resolved by interpretation without needing rectification at all.

## Equitable compensation and account of profits

Where someone breaches an equitable obligation, such as an obligation of confidence or a fiduciary duty, equity may award **equitable compensation** for the loss caused or order an **account of profits**, requiring the wrongdoer to hand over profits made from the breach. For businesses, this most often arises with confidential information: a former contractor uses your drawings to make a competing product, and the remedy may focus on what they gained rather than what you lost. Whether an obligation of confidence exists depends on the nature of the information and the circumstances in which it was shared, which is another reason to mark confidential material clearly and record how it was provided.

## Why discretion matters in practice

Because these remedies are discretionary, the way a business conducts itself during a dispute can decide the outcome. Courts consider factors such as:

- **Delay.** Did the claimant act promptly once it knew the facts?
- **Clean hands.** Has the claimant behaved fairly in the matter it complains about?
- **Hardship.** Would the order cause disproportionate hardship to the defendant or innocent third parties?
- **Adequacy of damages.** Would money do the job?
- **Practicality.** Can the order be complied with and enforced without ongoing court supervision?

A business that may need an equitable remedy should therefore keep its own conduct beyond criticism, act quickly and document what it knew and when.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The businesses, figures and events are hypothetical.

A small manufacturer commissions a toolmaker to build a set of press tools for a new bracket for $85,000, paid in three instalments. The contract states that the tools are the manufacturer's property on full payment. After the final payment, the toolmaker refuses to release the tools, claiming a separate unpaid invoice of $6,000 for unrelated work, and suggests it may use the tools to supply the bracket to another customer.

Damages are a poor remedy here. Replacement tools would take about fourteen weeks, the manufacturer's customer orders would be lost in the meantime, and the tools embody the manufacturer's design. The manufacturer's advisers might consider an urgent injunction to stop the toolmaker using or disposing of the tools, and orders for their delivery up. An interim order would likely require an undertaking as to damages, so the manufacturer must be confident in its position. Its strongest evidence is the contract's ownership clause, proof of full payment, and correspondence showing the $6,000 invoice relates to a different job.

Separately, the manufacturer might consider paying the disputed $6,000 under protest, while reserving its rights, if that releases the tools faster and more cheaply than litigation. That is a commercial judgement, not a legal one. The broader lesson sits upstream. Clear ownership terms, staged release of tooling, an agreed process for disputed invoices and photographs of the tools at each payment milestone would all have made the position easier to enforce.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Identify what money cannot replace.** Unique tooling, a key site, confidential designs, critical data and exclusive arrangements deserve specific contractual protection, because damages may not fix their loss.
- **Draft for the remedy you would need.** Ownership and delivery terms, confidentiality obligations, restraints tied to a defined interest and clear security arrangements all make equitable relief easier to obtain, or unnecessary.
- **Keep the negotiation record.** Marked-up drafts, emails confirming points agreed and meeting notes are what make rectification possible.
- **Act quickly.** If you suspect misuse of confidential information or a breach of a restraint, delay weakens any case for urgent relief.
- **Understand the undertaking.** Before seeking an interim injunction, understand the potential exposure under an undertaking as to damages.
- **Check whether you can rescind before you continue.** If you discover a contract was induced by a misleading statement, get advice before accepting further performance, because continuing may affirm the contract.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming a court will order performance.** For most supply, service and construction contracts, the realistic remedy is money and the contractual rights you negotiated.
- **Waiting too long.** Delay is one of the most common reasons equitable relief is refused.
- **Confusing rescission with termination.** They have different effects on accrued rights and on what must be returned.
- **Relying on memory for rectification.** Without contemporaneous records of the common intention, rectification is very hard to prove.
- **Over-wide restraints.** A restraint that tries to protect everything may protect nothing.
- **Ignoring the cost of urgency.** Urgent applications are expensive and carry undertaking risk. Sometimes a commercial solution is better.

## Questions to ask

- If this contract failed, what would we need: money, the asset itself, or for the other party to stop doing something?
- Does the contract clearly give us ownership, delivery rights or confidentiality protection for what matters most?
- Do our restraints protect a specific, identifiable interest for a reasonable period and area?
- Are we keeping the drafts and correspondence that show what was actually agreed?
- If we discovered we had been misled, do we know what conduct might affirm the contract?
- Could we give an undertaking as to damages if we needed urgent relief, and what might it cost?

## Bringing it together

Damages are the default remedy for a broken contract, but they are not always enough. Equity offers orders that compel performance, stop harmful conduct, unwind a contract obtained improperly or correct a document that misrecords the deal. These remedies are discretionary and come with limits: specific performance is largely confined to unique assets and is not used to supervise ongoing work; injunctions require prompt action and often an undertaking; rescission can be lost by affirmation; and rectification needs convincing proof of a common intention.

The most useful steps are taken long before a dispute. Identify what money cannot replace, draft ownership, confidentiality and restraint terms around it, keep the negotiation record and, if trouble arrives, act quickly and get advice.
