## About this book

A sheet metal shop that makes one-off parts thinks in jobs; one that makes products thinks in runs. A run of five hundred identical trays, brackets or enclosures is a different problem from a single fabrication. The product must be specified so that it meets the customer's need with the fewest operations. Its blank must nest well, because material is now the largest cost. Its routing must be planned and timed, because every minute is multiplied by the batch. Its setups must be spread over a sensible batch size, its labour falls as the operators learn, and its first part must be right before the other four hundred and ninety-nine are made.

This book works through those numbers for one product, from the customer's requirement to the run record. It starts with capacity and specification, develops the product's blank and its corners, nests the blank, builds a routing, works out setups, batch size and learning, costs the run, and ends with first-article inspection and the records that make the next run easier.

It is for production engineers, shop supervisors, estimators and product designers in sheet metal manufacturing. It assumes school algebra, a little geometry and a working knowledge of laser cutting and press brake folding. The Library’s Sheet Metal Parts by the Numbers covers bend allowances, springback and press brake force in more depth; this book uses them where a production run needs them. Setting prices and writing quotations are separate subjects, and this book stops at cost.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional product. This is an illustration. An equipment distributor has asked a sheet metal shop for 500 steel tote trays for parts storage in its warehouse. The trays must fit the distributor's shelving, which takes a 600 × 400 mm footprint, hold at least 36 litres to a fill line 20 mm below the rim, carry 25 kg of parts, nest twenty high in no more than 700 mm when empty, and be powder coated.

The shop's design is a flared tray of 1.2 mm cold-rolled steel, 600 × 400 mm at the top and 205 mm deep, with walls flaring at 11.31° from vertical, a hemmed rim, riveted corners, handle slots in the short walls and pressed denesting lugs. It cuts blanks on a fibre laser from 2,400 × 1,200 mm sheet, folds on a press brake, rivets on a bench jig and sends the trays out for powder coating.

The distributor, the shop, the dimensions, the times, the rates, the prices and the mistakes are invented. None describes KEVOS® work or a real business. Money is in Australian dollars, and each figure states whether GST is included.

## How to use the book

Read Chapters 1 and 2 first: they turn the requirement into the tray's dimensions, which every later chapter uses. Chapters 3 to 6 deal with the product's geometry and material, Chapters 7 to 9 with time and cost, and Chapter 10 with inspection and records. Each chapter calculates with the running case, includes a plausible mistake and how it was found, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The geometry is exact, and every calculation was checked by running it. Operation times, machine rates, material prices and coating costs are illustrations: real values come from the shop's own records and suppliers, and the book shows how to measure them. The learning curve and economic batch quantity are long-established models that describe tendencies and give estimates, not guarantees.

A storage tray carries loads and is handled by people. Its load rating, handling features and any claims made about it are the manufacturer's responsibility, under Australian consumer and work health and safety law. This book teaches planning and calculation. It is not a design or a rating for any product.
