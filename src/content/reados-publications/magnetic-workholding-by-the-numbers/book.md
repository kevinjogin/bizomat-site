## About this book

A magnetic chuck holds a steel plate with no clamps in the way of the cutter, and a lifting magnet picks up a plate with no slings or lugs. Both are quick, and both fail in ways that surprise people: a plate slides on a chuck that was holding it a minute ago, a thin part will not stay put however strong the chuck, a lifting magnet that easily held a test plate drops a rusty one. Each failure has a cause that can be calculated.

This book shows how. It starts from the flux density at the contact face, which sets the pull per unit area, and follows that pull through everything that reduces it: air gaps from scale, paint and chips, material that saturates or will not magnetise, plates too thin to carry the flux, parts too small to bridge the poles, and heat. It then compares the holding force with the forces that try to move the part, from milling and grinding, and with the weight and stiffness of plates on lifting magnets. The last chapters deal with distortion, residual magnetism and how to prove a set-up works.

It is for machinists, toolmakers, tooling and manufacturing engineers, fabrication supervisors and the people who plan lifts in a plate shop. It assumes school physics and algebra. Each chapter works the running case with real numbers, includes one plausible mistake and how it was found, links the BIZOMAT tool that does the chapter's calculation where one exists, and ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional part and the shop that makes it. This is an illustration. A machining and fabrication shop in Newcastle, New South Wales, makes the BP-600 base plate, a 600 × 400 × 25 mm mild steel plate that carries two linear guide rails on a packaging machine.

- **Material:** hot-rolled grade 250 steel plate, bought as 2,400 × 1,200 × 25 mm sheets with mill scale, flame cut to size.
- **Milling:** both faces milled on a vertical machining centre fitted with an electro-permanent magnetic chuck, 800 × 500 mm, with square 50 mm poles on a 60 mm pitch.
- **Grinding:** two 80 mm wide rail seats ground to a flatness of 0.01 mm on a surface grinder with an electromagnetic chuck.
- **Handling:** sheets moved from the rack to the cutting table with two permanent lifting magnets, each with a working load limit of 500 kg, on a lifting beam under a 1 t crane.

Flux densities, forces, derating factors, prices and times in the book are invented for the case or calculated from stated assumptions. They do not describe BIZOMAT work or any real business, product or manufacturer's rating.

## How to use the book

Read Chapter 1 first: it sets out the pull formula and the magnetic circuit that every later chapter uses. Chapters 2 to 4 cover what reduces the pull: the material, the gap and the size of the part. Chapters 5 and 6 compare the pull with milling and grinding forces, Chapter 7 compares types of chuck, and Chapter 8 deals with lifting. Chapter 9 covers heat and Chapter 10 residual magnetism and proving a set-up.

Every calculation in the book was checked by running it. The models are deliberately simple, and each chapter says where they stop being reliable. A maker's tested rating for a particular chuck or lifting magnet always overrides an estimate from this book.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. The pull formula and magnetic circuit models give estimates that explain behaviour and show which changes matter; they do not replace the maker's ratings, instructions and derating tables, or a pull test on the real part. Lifting magnets are lifting equipment and must be selected, inspected, used and maintained as the model WHS laws, the relevant Australian Standards and the maker's instructions require, by competent people. Strong magnetic fields can affect pacemakers and other active implants; people with implants should get medical advice before working near chucks and lifting magnets. Decisions about real lifts, machine set-ups and safety remain with the competent people responsible for them.
