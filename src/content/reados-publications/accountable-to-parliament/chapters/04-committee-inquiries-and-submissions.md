Estimates ranges across everything an agency does. An inquiry looks at one subject in depth: a committee receives terms of reference, invites submissions, holds hearings and reports to the Parliament, usually with recommendations. When an agency's program sits near the centre of an inquiry, the committee will want to know what the agency did, why and with what result: in writing first, then in person.

This is an illustration. In July 2027 the Senate referred an inquiry into regional community grant programs to one of its committees. The inquiry is fictional, and so are its dates. The committee wrote to NRIO inviting a submission by Friday 3 September 2027, and later asked NRIO officials to appear at a public hearing on Thursday 21 October 2027. Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP), with its two grants approved against officials' recommendations, is close to the centre of the inquiry. Lucy Tran coordinates the drafting and clearance, writing with Sam Reid, who owns the program's facts. Clare Whitfield oversees the work.

The committee's terms of reference ask it to inquire into:

- (a) the design of regional community grant programs, including their guidelines;
- (b) how applications are assessed and decisions made, including decisions that differ from officials' recommendations;
- (c) transparency, including the publication of decisions;
- (d) any related matters.

## What an inquiry is

Committees of the Senate, of the House of Representatives and of both Houses together inquire into matters referred to them. Most work under each House's powers, standing orders and resolutions; the orders and resolutions are not legislation, and they differ between the Houses. This book describes them only in general terms. For each inquiry, read the committee's own instructions, on its web page and in its letters.

Most inquiries follow the same arc. The committee publishes its terms of reference and calls for submissions. It holds public hearings, and sometimes takes evidence in private. It then reports to the Parliament, and the government decides how to respond to any recommendations.

An inquiry differs from Estimates in three ways. The terms of reference set its scope, so everything the agency says should map to them. Committees usually invite submissions from anyone, so the agency's is read beside those of applicants, councils, experts and critics. And the report may recommend changes to the program the agency runs. The agency's part is to give the committee accurate facts about its administration. What should change is for the committee to recommend and the government to decide.

## Deciding to make a submission

An invitation to make a submission calls for a decision by the right person, made early and recorded. Five questions help.

- Is the agency's work within the terms of reference?
- Does the agency hold facts the committee needs and would not otherwise get accurately?
- Is the government or the portfolio department making a coordinated submission that should carry the agency's material instead?
- What has the agency already said on the subject, and would a submission set the record out clearly in one place?
- Does the Minister know? Under section 19(1)(a) of the PGPA Act the accountable authority must "keep the responsible Minister informed of the activities of the entity" (Chapter 1).

If the agency decides not to make a submission, it should still reply to the committee and be ready to appear if asked.

This is an illustration. Clare put the decision to the CEO in a one-page brief in July. Round 1 is directly relevant to terms (a) to (c), NRIO holds the facts, and NRIO's portfolio department confirmed that it would not cover NRIO's programs in a submission of its own. The Minister's office was told that NRIO intended to make one and would see the final draft before it went. The CEO agreed and named the Director, Regional Programs as the senior owner.

## Drafting a submission that sticks to facts

A good agency submission is a reliable account of the agency's administration for a reader who knows nothing about it. It is not advocacy.

- **Follow the terms of reference.** Use headings that match them, in the same order, so the committee can find what the agency says on each.
- **Start with a short summary.** Many readers will read nothing else.
- **State facts with their sources and dates.** Take every figure from the settled facts already used in the agency's answers to questions on notice (Chapter 3).
- **Describe government policy; do not argue it.** Say what the policy is, where it was stated, and how the agency administers it.
- **State Ministers' decisions as facts:** what was decided, when and for how much. The reasons are the Minister's to give.
- **Leave out what is not the agency's to publish,** such as personal information about applicants and individual complaints still being dealt with.
- **Write for publication.** Committees usually publish submissions. Assume every sentence will be quoted by someone who disagrees with it.

The policy rule rests on the framework the agency works within. Subsection 10(4) of the Public Service Act says: "The APS is open and accountable to the Australian community under the law and within the framework of Ministerial responsibility." An agency is open about its facts and administration; policy belongs to the government, and the Minister answers for it in the Parliament.

This is an illustration. The applicant ranked 32nd in Round 1 complained to NRIO in July 2027 and took its complaint to the Commonwealth Ombudsman in August (Chapter 8). The Ombudsman Act says an investigation "shall be conducted in private" (section 8(2)). NRIO's submission says nothing about the complaint. An open complaint is a matter between the applicant, NRIO and the Ombudsman, and the applicant's details are not NRIO's to publish.

## Clearance before the submission goes

A submission speaks for the agency in public, so clear it at least as carefully as an answer to a question on notice. Senators on the inquiry can read the agency's Estimates evidence, corrections and answers, and will notice any difference.

This is an illustration. At NRIO the draft goes from Lucy and Sam to the Director, Regional Programs for the facts; to Clare for consistency and the sentence test; to the legal area for privacy and anything touching the complaint; and to the CEO for approval. The Minister's office then has the final draft for five working days before NRIO lodges it. The submission remains NRIO's, and so does responsibility for its accuracy: any change the office suggests is tested for accuracy before it is accepted.

## Appearing as a witness

The committee invites; the agency decides who can best answer, at a level senior enough to speak for it. Officials explain the agency's administration and the government's policy as stated, without arguing for or against that policy (Chapter 2). For further reading, see the government's guidelines for official witnesses, named in Chapter 2.

This is an illustration. The CEO, the Director, Regional Programs and Sam Reid will appear on 21 October. Ravi Menon prepares the hearing pack, as he did for Estimates. Clare attends to support the witnesses and to log any question taken on notice, which then follows the process in Chapter 3.

Preparation follows a few rules.

- **Re-read the agency's own submission and earlier answers.** The committee will quote them.
- **Read the other submissions, especially critical ones.** For each claim about the agency's administration, prepare a factual response, not a rebuttal of its author.
- **Brief each term of reference and the likely questions,** from the same settled facts as the submission.
- **Keep any opening statement short and factual,** if the committee invites one, and clear it like the submission.
- **Agree in advance who answers which kind of question,** and how a witness passes a question to a colleague.

At the hearing, the habits from Estimates hold. Answer the question asked. Take a question on notice rather than guess. Correct a mistake at the hearing if you can, or in writing as soon as possible afterwards (Chapter 10). A committee may also take evidence in private, called in camera evidence; a witness who thinks evidence should be given that way asks the committee, which decides.

## Parliamentary privilege and the protection of witnesses

Parliamentary privilege is the set of legal protections that let each House and its committees work without interference. Chapter 2 introduced it. Four provisions of the [Parliamentary Privileges Act 1987](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03430/latest/text) matter to a witness and to the agency that sends one.

**What counts as proceedings.** Section 16(2) says proceedings in Parliament are everything said and done in, for, or incidental to the business of a House or a committee. It expressly includes giving evidence, submitting a document to a committee, and preparing a document for purposes of or incidental to that business.

**How courts may use them.** Under section 16(3)(a), a court or tribunal may not receive evidence, questions or submissions about proceedings in Parliament for the purpose of "questioning or relying on the truth, motive, intention or good faith" of anything forming part of them. A witness can give evidence plainly, knowing it cannot be used in court in those ways.

**Protection of witnesses.** Section 12 makes it an offence to influence another person's evidence, or to induce them not to give it, "by fraud, intimidation, force or threat", by the offer or promise of an inducement or benefit, or by other improper means. It is also an offence to "inflict any penalty or injury upon, or deprive of any benefit" another person because of evidence given or to be given.

**Evidence taken in private.** Section 13 makes it an offence to publish or disclose in camera evidence "without the authority of a House or a committee", unless the House or committee has published it or authorised its publication.

Three cautions keep privilege in proportion.

First, privilege protects the proceedings; it does not make an agency's documents secret. Whether an internal brief prepared for a hearing is itself covered is a legal question that turns on the facts. Ask the legal area, and do not tell anyone a document is privileged.

Second, privilege is not a licence to be careless. The duty not to give false or misleading information (subsection 13(9) of the Public Service Act) applies to every APS employee who gives evidence or drafts a submission for the agency. Separately, a House can deal with an "offence against a House". Under section 4, conduct counts as one only if it is, or is meant or likely to be, an improper interference with a House or committee exercising its authority or functions, or with a member performing their duties. Whether particular conduct meets that test is for the House to determine, and section 7 limits the penalties a House may impose: imprisonment for up to six months, or a fine of up to $5,000 for a natural person.

Third, the protections bind managers too. Briefing an official witness on the facts is preparation. Pressing anyone to change or withhold evidence is not, and neither is any detriment to a staff member because of evidence they gave. If someone in your team gives evidence in a private capacity, take advice before any decision about them that could look connected. And do not circulate a note of in camera evidence, even internally, without first asking the committee's secretariat what the committee has authorised.

## The Joint Committee of Public Accounts and Audit

Most committees take their functions from the orders of the Houses. The Joint Committee of Public Accounts and Audit (JCPAA), with members from both Houses, has its duties set by an Act: the [Public Accounts and Audit Committee Act 1951](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C1951A00060/latest/text). Three of them matter most to an agency.

- **It examines audit reports.** Its duties include examining all reports of the Auditor-General tabled in each House of the Parliament, including reports on performance audits (section 8(1)(c)).
- **It sets the Parliament's audit priorities.** Its duties include "to determine the audit priorities of the Parliament and to advise the Auditor-General of those priorities" (section 8(1)(m)). The Auditor-General must have regard to those priorities (Auditor-General Act 1997, section 10(a)).
- **It cannot direct the Auditor-General.** Section 8(1A) says nothing in section 8(1) "authorises the Committee to direct the activities of the Auditor-General". The Auditor-General Act adds that the Auditor-General is not subject to direction from anyone on whether an audit is conducted, how it is conducted, or the priority given to any matter (section 8(4)).

Section 18 says a person "shall not give false evidence on oath or affirmation before the Committee", with a penalty of five years' imprisonment: a statutory offence specific to the JCPAA. Before any committee, an official witness remains bound by subsection 13(9) of the Code of Conduct.

This is an illustration. Later in the case, the Auditor-General conducts a performance audit of RCFP Round 1 (Chapter 5). Once tabled, its report is among those the JCPAA's duty is to examine, and NRIO may be asked to appear. What NRIO says then should agree with what it told this inquiry and Estimates; where a fact has changed, NRIO should say what changed and why.

## When it goes wrong: a submission that made policy

This is an illustration. In 2025, NRIO made a submission to a different Senate committee inquiry, into the upkeep of regional community buildings (also fictional). A program area that knew the subject well drafted it, and its facts were sound. Its last section, headed "Recommendations", said: "NRIO recommends that Commonwealth facility grants include a maintenance allowance of up to 10 per cent of the grant." The government had taken no such position. The draft was checked for accuracy, but nobody asked whether that sentence was NRIO's to write. The Minister's office first saw it after the committee published it.

The cost came in three parts. At the hearing, Senators asked NRIO's officials whether they disagreed with their Minister, pushing them towards debating policy in public. The CEO then had to write to the committee to explain that the recommendation was not government policy, which made the agency look careless and at odds with the government it serves. And the committee's report quoted the sentence anyway, in support of a recommendation of its own. Relations with the Minister's office took months to recover.

The control is the sentence test in the decision figure above, applied at clearance to every sentence that says "should", "recommends" or "NRIO considers", with the final draft in the Minister's office's hands before it is lodged. Where an agency has evidence that bears on a policy choice, such as what it costs to maintain the buildings it funds, the submission gives the evidence and leaves the conclusion to the committee and the government.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. One sound outline follows the terms of reference in order, after a summary and a short account of the program.

```figure
type: tree
title: Outline of NRIO's submission
caption: This is an illustration. The headings follow the terms of reference in order, so the committee can find NRIO's evidence on each.
- NRIO's submission | approved and signed by the CEO; lodged Thu 2 Sep 2027
  - 1. Summary | one page; every figure repeated with its source in the body
  - 2. NRIO and the RCFP | purpose and roles: NRIO assessed and advised; the Minister decided
  - 3. Design and guidelines | term (a): how the guidelines were set and published
  - * 4. Assessment and decisions | term (b): 186 received, 9 ineligible, 177 assessed; 31 grants worth A$9.98 million; 2 against officials' recommendations
  - 5. Transparency | term (c): what NRIO published, when and where
  - 6. Administrative steps | term (d): changes within NRIO's own authority; no policy recommendations
  - Attachment | data tables drawn from the settled facts sheet
```

**Summary and roles.** The summary gives the purpose of the RCFP, the Round 1 numbers and who decided what. Section 2 states the roles plainly: NRIO assessed applications and advised, and the Minister decided. That should match how NRIO described the roles at Estimates.

**Assessment and decisions.** Section 4 carries the numbers, all from the settled facts sheet, with the arithmetic shown in the attachment.

- 186 applications were received. 9 were found ineligible and not assessed: 9 ÷ 186 = 4.8 per cent. 177 were assessed (186 − 9).
- On Monday 31 May 2027 the Minister approved 31 grants worth A$9.98 million, excluding GST: 31 of the 177 assessed applications, or 17.5 per cent (31 ÷ 177 = 0.175).
- 2 grants were approved against officials' recommendations: Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. (A$140,000) and Yarrowmere Showground Committee Inc. (A$260,000). Together, A$140,000 + A$260,000 = A$400,000, or 4.0 per cent of the A$9.98 million (400,000 ÷ 9,980,000 = 0.040). The other 29 grants (31 − 2) were among those officials recommended.
- The CEO's evidence at Estimates on 2 June, that all 186 applications had been assessed, was corrected by letter on 4 June. A sentence says so, letting the committee see NRIO's figures and its correction together.

The section states the decisions, their date and their amounts. It does not characterise them or offer reasons for them: those are the Minister's.

**Left out, and why.**

- Any view on whether Ministers should approve grants against officials' recommendations. It fails the sentence test.
- The complaint from the applicant ranked 32nd, now with the Ombudsman. Investigations are conducted in private, and the applicant's details are not NRIO's to publish.
- Personal information about individual applicants beyond what is already public.
- Any figure not on the settled facts sheet. A new figure is checked and added to the sheet first.

**Before it is lodged.** The steps in the timeline above: facts cleared by Wednesday 18 August; read-across, sentence test and legal check by Monday 23 August; the CEO's approval and the final draft to the Minister's office on Wednesday 25 August; lodged on Thursday 2 September, a day early.

Built this way, the submission gives the witnesses on 21 October a document they can stand behind line by line.
