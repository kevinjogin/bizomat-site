The steam engine is often told as a story of a single genius and a single invention. The real history is more useful. It took nearly a century of improvement, by an ironmonger, an instrument maker, a self-taught engineer and a Cornish mine captain's son, to turn a crude mine pump into the prime mover of industrial economies. Each made a different kind of contribution: combining existing parts in a new way, improving workmanship and measurement, rethinking where losses occurred, and taking a risk others would not. Along the way, they invented ways to measure performance, sell savings and make dangerous machines safe.

Those patterns still describe how most technical progress happens in businesses today. New products and processes rarely arrive fully formed. They improve through many steps, they are judged by metrics that change as alternatives appear, and they succeed commercially when someone works out how to sell their value. Understanding how steam power developed helps engineers and managers recognise these patterns in their own work.

This article traces the development of the steam engine from Savery and Newcomen through Smeaton, Watt, Trevithick and Evans, explaining the engineering in plain terms, and then draws out lessons about efficiency, improvement, measurement, intellectual property, business models and safety. It is general information for engineers, managers and anyone interested in how technology and business progress together.

## The problem: water in the mines

By the late seventeenth century, British mines were going deeper, and water flooding the workings had become the limiting factor. Pumping by horses, waterwheels and men was expensive and could not keep pace. Scientists had shown that the atmosphere exerts pressure and that a vacuum could be created by condensing steam, but nobody had turned that into a practical machine.

**Thomas Savery** patented a steam-powered pump in 1698 and promoted it in the early 1700s. It used steam pressure and condensation directly in a vessel to draw water up and push it out. It worked, but it was limited in the height it could lift water, wasted fuel and was risky because of the pressures involved.

## Newcomen: a good machine from familiar parts

**Thomas Newcomen**, an ironmonger, completed a more practical engine in 1712, first installed to pump a mine near Dudley Castle. Newcomen's contribution was not a new scientific principle but a clever combination of existing things:

1. A boiler similar to a brewer's copper produced low-pressure steam.
2. Steam filled a vertical cylinder beneath a piston.
3. A spray of cold water inside the cylinder condensed the steam, creating a partial vacuum.
4. Atmospheric pressure pushed the piston down, doing the work.
5. A weighted beam, rocking on a pivot, raised the piston again and worked the pump rods in the mine.

```figure

caption: Cooling and reheating the cylinder every few seconds wasted well over 99% of the fuel's energy.
- Boiler makes low-pressure steam
- Steam fills the cylinder
- Cold spray condenses it: a partial vacuum
- *Atmosphere pushes the piston down
- Weighted beam lifts it and works the pump
```

Valve gear driven by the engine's own motion soon replaced hand operation, increasing the number of strokes per minute.

By modern standards the Newcomen engine was hopelessly inefficient. The cylinder had to be cooled to condense the steam and reheated by the next charge, every few seconds, wasting well over 99% of the energy in the fuel. Critics joked that it took an iron mine to build one and a coal mine to run it.

Yet it transformed mining. A single engine could replace dozens of men and horses working around the clock and allow mines to work below levels that had been impossible to drain. Engines spread across Britain and to continental Europe within a few decades.

The lesson is about **which metric matters**. Newcomen engines were not compared with better engines; they were compared with horses and with mines that could not be worked at all. Thermal efficiency became the decisive measure only later, when engines competed with each other.

## Smeaton: improvement through measurement and workmanship

**John Smeaton**, who adopted the title of civil engineer to distinguish civilian work from military engineering, did not invent a new engine. Smeaton improved the Newcomen engine through careful experiment, more precise cylinder boring, better proportioning of parts and higher quality workmanship, taking the atmospheric engine close to the best it could achieve.

Smeaton's greater contribution was method. Smeaton measured performance systematically, experimenting with water wheels and engines rather than relying on rules of thumb, and quantified the work that a horse could do. Measurement turned engineering judgement into something that could be compared, improved and sold.

## Watt: finding where the losses really were

**James Watt**, an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, was repairing a model Newcomen engine in the 1760s and recognised that most of the waste came from cooling and reheating the cylinder on every stroke. The solution, patented in 1769, was simple in concept: **condense the steam somewhere else**.

- A **separate condenser**, connected to the cylinder but kept cold, did the condensing, while the cylinder stayed hot.
- A **steam jacket** kept the cylinder walls hot.
- An **air pump** removed water and air from the condenser to maintain the vacuum.
- Steam, rather than the atmosphere, pushed the piston, with the top of the cylinder closed.

These changes cut fuel consumption by around three-quarters compared with the Newcomen engine.

Over the following years, Watt and the Birmingham manufacturer **Matthew Boulton**, partners in business, developed engines that worked on both sides of the piston and converted the reciprocating motion into rotation, so engines could drive mills and factories rather than only pumps. Mechanisms such as Watt's parallel motion linkage and the use of a centrifugal governor to control speed followed. By 1800, around 500 Boulton and Watt engines were at work.

Watt's achievement depended on others as well. Precise cylinders became possible when the ironmaster **John Wilkinson** developed a boring machine accurate enough to make large cylinders truly round, showing how one industry's capability often depends on another's.

## Selling savings: the Boulton and Watt business model

Boulton and Watt faced a commercial problem. Their engines were more expensive than Newcomen engines, and mine owners were cautious. Their answer was to charge for the value delivered. Customers paid for parts and erection, and then an annual premium based on one-third of the fuel saved compared with a Newcomen engine doing the same work, for the life of the patent.

This model aligned price with value, reduced the customer's risk and gave Boulton and Watt a stake in performance. It also needed **measurement**: counters on the engines recorded strokes so that work done, and therefore savings, could be calculated. Watt's definition of **horsepower**, 33,000 foot-pounds of work per minute, gave customers a familiar unit for comparing engines with horses.

The same idea appears today in energy performance contracts and outcome-based service agreements, in which suppliers are paid from the savings or results they deliver. The [selling performance, not products](/reados/collections/growth-business-models-and-new-markets/selling-performance-not-products/) article covers how businesses can sell outcomes rather than equipment.

## Patents: protection and restriction

Watt's patent, extended by Parliament to 1800, protected the partnership's investment and gave it time to develop a business. It also had costs. Watt was cautious about high-pressure steam, regarding it as dangerous, and the patent discouraged others from developing alternatives that used the same ideas. Some historians argue that it delayed high-pressure engines until the patent expired.

The tension between protecting investment and enabling others to build on it remains central to intellectual property decisions today.

## Trevithick and Evans: high pressure and portability

Once Watt's patent expired, **Richard Trevithick** in Cornwall and **Oliver Evans** in Philadelphia built engines that used steam at much higher pressures and exhausted it to the atmosphere, without a condenser.

High pressure changed everything. Force is pressure multiplied by area, so higher pressure gives the same power from a much smaller cylinder. Without a condenser, air pump, cooling water and large beam, the engine became compact and light enough to move. Trevithick built steam road carriages and, in 1804, a locomotive that hauled loads on a tramway in Wales. Trevithick's engines also connected the piston more directly to a crank, an arrangement that became the basis of later reciprocating engines.

Evans built high-pressure engines that drove mills and factories across the United States, after earlier developing a highly automated flour mill that moved grain through the process with minimal labour.

## Safety: designing protection that cannot be defeated

High-pressure boilers store a great deal of energy, and they exploded, killing people. An early lesson came when an operator tied down the safety valve on one of Trevithick's boilers and it burst. Trevithick's response was to fit a second safety valve that the operator could not interfere with, and to use a **fusible plug**: a piece of lead set into the boiler that melts and releases steam if the water level falls and the plate overheats.

The principle still applies: a protective device that a person under production pressure can defeat is not truly protective. Through the nineteenth century, repeated boiler explosions led to inspection regimes, design codes and insurance-driven engineering standards, and modern pressure equipment regulation descends from them.

## Lessons for engineering and business today

### Judge technology by the comparison that matters now

The Newcomen engine was wasteful but transformative because it was compared with the alternatives available at the time. New technologies often look poor against mature competitors on the wrong metric, or impressive on a metric that customers do not yet value. Ask what the realistic alternative is and what the customer actually measures.

### Most progress is improvement, not invention

Savery, Newcomen, Smeaton, Watt and Trevithick each built on predecessors. Smeaton added no new principle yet produced large gains through precision and workmanship. Businesses often underinvest in improving existing products and processes while chasing breakthroughs.

### Find where the losses really are

Watt's insight came from understanding the physics of the losses, not from adding more fuel or a bigger cylinder. Many modern problems, in energy use, quality or delivery, yield to the same approach: measure, identify where value is lost and redesign that part.

### Measure performance

Smeaton's experiments and Watt's stroke counters and horsepower made performance comparable and saleable. Without measurement, improvement claims are opinions.

### Sell the value, not the machine

Boulton and Watt priced their engines on savings, sharing risk and reward with customers. Value-based pricing remains powerful where results can be measured and attributed.

### Capability depends on the supply chain

Watt's engine needed Wilkinson's accurate boring. Breakthroughs often depend on suppliers' capabilities as much as on the designer's idea.

### Make safety protection robust

Trevithick's undefeatable safety valve and fusible plug are early examples of designing protection that does not depend on people resisting production pressure.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. A supplier of variable speed drives and high-efficiency motors offers to retrofit a manufacturer's compressed air system, which uses about 400,000 kWh of electricity a year. The supplier's engineers estimate that the retrofit will reduce consumption by about 25%, or 100,000 kWh a year. At an assumed electricity price of 25 cents a kilowatt-hour, that is about $25,000 a year.

**Conventional sale.** The supplier quotes $60,000 for the equipment and installation. The manufacturer is unsure whether the savings will really materialise and is reluctant to commit capital.

**Savings-based offer, in the Boulton and Watt tradition.** The supplier instead offers to install the equipment for $20,000 and take one-third of the measured savings for five years. If the savings are as estimated, the supplier receives about $8,333 a year, or about $41,700 over five years, a total of about $61,700, slightly more than the conventional price in exchange for carrying the performance risk. The manufacturer keeps two-thirds of the savings from the first year, about $16,700 a year, and pays less up front.

**Measurement.** Both parties agree a baseline from a month of metered data, a method for adjusting for production volume and a metering arrangement, much as Boulton and Watt used stroke counters. If savings fall short, the supplier earns less; if they exceed expectations, both benefit.

```figure
type: compare
title: Selling a compressed air retrofit on measured savings

caption: Estimated saving of 100,000 kWh, about $25,000 a year; the manufacturer keeps two-thirds from the first year.
- Upfront | $60,000 | $20,000

- Total to the supplier | $60,000 | About $61,700 if savings are as estimated
- *Performance risk | Carried by the manufacturer | Carried by the supplier
```

The example shows how the principle of sharing measured savings can still unlock investment when customers doubt claims and suppliers are confident in their technology.

## Applying these lessons in an Australian business

- **Compare new options with realistic alternatives**, using the metrics customers value now.
- **Invest in steady improvement** of existing products and processes, not only in new inventions.
- **Measure where losses occur** before redesigning.
- **Build measurement into products and services**, so performance can be demonstrated.
- **Consider value-based or savings-based pricing** where results can be measured.
- **Understand the supplier capabilities** your innovations depend on.
- **Weigh protection against openness** in intellectual property decisions.
- **Design safety protection** that cannot easily be bypassed.

## Questions worth considering

- Against which alternative will customers judge our product or improvement?
- Where do the biggest losses in our process actually occur?
- What could we gain from better precision and workmanship rather than new technology?
- How do we measure and demonstrate the value we deliver?
- Would sharing risk and reward with customers help them adopt our offering?
- Which of our safety protections could be defeated by someone under pressure?

## Bringing it together

The steam engine became world-changing through a century of improvement by people who combined existing parts, measured performance, understood where losses occurred, built on suppliers' capabilities and took calculated risks. Newcomen showed that a wasteful machine can still transform an industry when compared with what came before; Smeaton showed the power of measurement and workmanship; Watt showed the value of understanding losses and selling savings; Trevithick showed both the benefits of challenging assumptions and the need for safety that cannot be defeated. These remain practical lessons for engineers and businesses improving products and processes today.
