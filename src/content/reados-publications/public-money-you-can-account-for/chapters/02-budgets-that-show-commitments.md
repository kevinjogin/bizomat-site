This is an illustration. At the end of October, the Asset Programs Branch's draft monthly report said the branch had spent A$620,000 of its A$2,100,000 suppliers budget. That is 29.5% of the money with a third of the year gone, and it looked comfortable. It was not. Another A$910,000 was already committed through open purchase orders and contract call-offs, and the site coordinators expected to need A$720,000 more before 30 June. The branch was heading for a A$150,000 overspend.

A report that counts only invoices paid describes the past. A business manager needs a report that shows what the branch has already promised to pay and what it still expects to spend, early enough for someone to act. That takes a budget built from its drivers, phased by month, and reported as four numbers with an honest forecast.

## Build the budget from what drives it

This is an illustration. The branch's departmental budget for 2026–27, the financial year from 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027, is A$6,400,000: employees A$3,900,000, suppliers A$2,100,000 and minor capital A$400,000. It is an internal budget, so it is kept exclusive of GST, and every budget amount that follows is GST-exclusive. Procurement thresholds and NRIO's delegations, by contrast, include GST. Ask your finance area how GST appears in your own reports.

A budget that is useful in March is built from what drives each line, not from last year's total plus a percentage.

Employees come from the positions in the branch, the expected cost of each, and the dates of vacancies and planned recruitment. Arun does not guess position costs. He takes them from the finance area's costings and notes the date he received them.

Suppliers come from contracts and their schedules, the planned maintenance program for the 46 facilities, an allowance for reactive repairs based on the condition of the sites, site services contracts, and travel and training. The branch's A$2,100,000 has four components: planned maintenance A$1,150,000, reactive repairs A$450,000, site services contracts A$300,000, and travel, training and minor services A$200,000.

Minor capital covers named small capital works, each with its own approval. What counts as capital rather than an expense is set by the finance area's policy. Ask them rather than guessing.

Write the assumptions down beside the numbers. When someone asks in March why reactive repairs were budgeted at A$450,000, the answer should be in the file, not in someone's memory. That is the APS Value of Stewardship in practice: institutional knowledge that outlasts the person who built the budget.

## Phase it by month

An annual budget does not say when the money will be spent. Spread evenly, the suppliers line would be A$175,000 a month, or A$700,000 by 31 October. The branch's real pattern is different. July starts slowly while new orders are raised. December and January are quieter, with the holiday shutdown and wet-season access limits at some remote sites. March to June are busier as planned works are finished before the year ends.

This is an illustration. Arun phases the suppliers line with Tom and the Facilities Director, using the maintenance schedule and the contract calendars. The phased budget to 31 October is A$650,000, not A$700,000. Phasing lets the branch compare what it has spent with what it expected to have spent by now, rather than with a twelfth of the year for each month.

## The four numbers

Every budget line needs four numbers. This is an illustration, using the suppliers line at 31 October.

- Budget: the amount allocated to the line for the year, A$2,100,000.
- Actuals: what has been paid, A$620,000.
- Commitments: approved and ordered, but not yet invoiced, A$910,000. These are open purchase orders and contract call-offs.
- Forecast: further spending expected before 30 June that is not yet committed, A$720,000.

Two results follow. The available balance is the budget less actuals and commitments: A$2,100,000 − A$620,000 − A$910,000 = A$570,000. The forecast outturn is actuals plus commitments plus forecast: A$620,000 + A$910,000 + A$720,000 = A$2,250,000. The variance is A$2,250,000 − A$2,100,000 = A$150,000 over budget. As a share of the line, that is A$150,000 ÷ A$2,100,000 = 7.14%, or 7.1% rounded to one decimal place.

Read the two results together. A$570,000 is left to commit, and the branch expects to need A$720,000. The gap between them is the A$150,000 overspend, visible eight months before it would happen.

Commitments are not estimates. Each one is a commitment of relevant money that a delegate has approved, and section 18 of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) requires the approver to record the approval in writing as soon as practicable. From that day the money is spoken for, whether or not an invoice has arrived. A report that leaves commitments out leaves out money the branch has already agreed to pay.

## Why an invoices-only view misleads

Invoices lag the work. A contractor finishes a job, sends an invoice some weeks later, and payment follows once the invoice has been checked. An invoices-only report is always looking at last month's work, or the month before.

This is an illustration. Three readings of the same suppliers line at 31 October show how far the views drift apart.

Paid against the year: A$620,000 is 29.5% of the line, with 33.3% of the year gone. It looks under.

Paid against phasing: A$620,000 against a phased A$650,000 is A$30,000 under. It still looks under. But A$95,000 of planned maintenance was delivered in September and October and has not yet been invoiced. Counting it, spending to date is A$620,000 + A$95,000 = A$715,000, which is A$65,000 ahead of phasing.

Run rate: A$620,000 over four months is A$155,000 a month. Multiplied by 12, that gives A$1,860,000, apparently A$240,000 under budget. The full view says A$150,000 over. The two views differ by A$240,000 + A$150,000 = A$390,000.

Paid and committed together come to A$1,530,000, or 72.9% of the line after four months. That number, not 29.5%, tells the Branch Manager how much room is left.

Ask your finance area how NRIO's system shows work that has been delivered but not yet invoiced. Some systems treat it as an accrued expense; others leave it in commitments until the invoice arrives. Either way, the business manager's report should never count it as money still free to spend.

## Explaining a variance

A variance explanation is useful when it answers four questions: how much, why, whether it is timing or permanent, and what happens next and who does it. Compare two versions for the same line.

Weak: "Suppliers underspent to date due to timing."

Strong: "Suppliers paid to date are A$30,000 (4.6%) below phasing. This is timing: A$95,000 of planned maintenance from September and October has been delivered and not invoiced. Including it, spending is A$65,000 ahead of phasing. Tom is chasing the invoices. The forecast overspend for the year is reported separately below."

The weak version is not false, but it hides the fact that matters. The strong one gives the reader the cause, the size and the owner. It also stops a timing variance being mistaken for spare money.

For the year as a whole, the explanation starts the same way: "Forecast outturn A$2,250,000, A$150,000 (7.1%) over budget." It then names the cause, says the variance is permanent unless the branch acts, and says when options will reach the Branch Manager. The exercise at the end of this chapter breaks the line into its components to find that cause. A Branch Manager who reads only that a variance is "under review" has no warning and no decision to make.

## Forecasting the rest of the year

Build the forecast from the firmest numbers outward. Commitments come first. Tom checks each open order and call-off with the people managing the work, to confirm it will be delivered and invoiced this year. Planned works not yet ordered come next, from the maintenance schedule and the branch's own plans. Estimates come last: the site coordinators' view of repairs still to come, site by site, based on the condition of their facilities and the pattern so far.

Each forecast line needs an owner and a written assumption. Each month, compare the forecast with last month's and explain the movement. A forecast that equals the budget on every line usually means nobody forecast at all.

This is an illustration. The A$720,000 forecast includes, within its A$200,000 for planned maintenance, the facility condition survey of all 46 sites that Lena expects to approve in early November (Chapter 3). The survey's A$180,000 price includes GST; like everything in this budget, the forecast holds it exclusive of GST. In all, the branch still expects to pay A$910,000 + A$720,000 = A$1,630,000 between November and June. Arun spreads it by month from the contract calendars and the coordinators' estimates. November is heavy, because the A$95,000 backlog of invoices arrives then. From November the forecast runs above the phased budget, and the gap widens to A$150,000 by 30 June.

The employees line is forecast differently. Most of it is known: the people in the positions, paid each pay period. The uncertain parts are vacancies, recruitment dates and leave. A vacancy left open for three months creates an underspend on employees, and it is tempting to treat that as spare money for suppliers. Whether money can move between lines is a question for the finance area and NRIO's instructions, not a branch decision.

A forecast is advice. Prepare it with the care and diligence that section 25 of the PGPA Act expects of every official, and give it straight when the news is unwelcome. The earlier an overspend is reported, the more options the Branch Manager has.

## What the monthly report shows

The branch's monthly report fits on one page. For each line it shows the budget, the phased budget to date, actuals, commitments, the available balance, the forecast, the forecast outturn and the variance. Beside each variance sits a short explanation in the form above. A final column shows how the forecast outturn moved since last month, because a forecast that creeps up a little every month tells its own story.

Arun builds the report from the finance and purchasing systems on the third working day of the month. Lena reviews it, checks every explanation against the numbers, and sends it to the Branch Manager and the Facilities Director together, so both read the same version.

## Year-end

The year ends on 30 June 2027. Three things need attention from about March.

Open commitments: which orders will be delivered by 30 June, and which will not? Ask your finance area how NRIO treats commitments that run into the next year, and goods or services received but not invoiced at 30 June. Do not assume either way.

Orders no longer needed: close them. That keeps the commitment figure true and releases the money.

A late rush to spend: buying in June because money is left over is hard to reconcile with proper use, which section 8 of the PGPA Act defines as efficient, effective, economical and ethical. If the forecast shows an underspend, report it early so the agency can decide what to do with the money.

The year's actual pattern also becomes the starting point for next year's phasing. Keep the monthly forecasts. They show where the estimates were wrong, which is the most useful thing to know when the next budget is built.

## When it goes wrong: the report that counted only invoices

This is an illustration. The October report nearly went out in its old form. Arun prepared it from the branch's long-standing template, which drew only on payments. It said "A$620,000 spent, 29.5% of budget, on track". A copy went to the Facilities Director before Lena had reviewed it.

The Facilities Director replied within the hour. With so much apparent room in the budget, they planned to ask the site coordinators to bring forward repainting at four sites, estimated at A$120,000. Had that gone ahead, the forecast overspend would have grown from A$150,000 to A$150,000 + A$120,000 = A$270,000, which is 12.9% of the line. The branch would also have found out later, with fewer options left.

Lena corrected the report the same day and rang the Facilities Director to explain. The repainting was not ordered.

Nobody acted dishonestly. The template had always worked that way, and nobody had asked what it left out. What should have happened is clear. The template should show budget, actuals, commitments and forecast for every line, with commitments drawn from the purchasing system rather than from memory. The business manager should review the report before anyone outside the branch sees it. And any decision to spend more should be tested against the available balance and the forecast, not against payments to date. A delegate approving the repainting would have been expected to check that the money was there. Lena rebuilt the template that week.

## Exercise: rebuild the suppliers line position

This is an illustration. Here is Arun's data for the suppliers line at 31 October, GST-exclusive.

| Component | Budget | Paid | Committed | Forecast further spend |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Planned maintenance | A$1,150,000 | A$330,000 | A$600,000 | A$200,000 |
| Reactive repairs | A$450,000 | A$170,000 | A$110,000 | A$330,000 |
| Site services contracts | A$300,000 | A$80,000 | A$140,000 | A$90,000 |
| Travel, training and minor services | A$200,000 | A$40,000 | A$60,000 | A$100,000 |

For each component and for the line as a whole, work out the available balance, the forecast outturn and the variance against budget. Work out the share of the line paid, and the share paid or committed. Then write a variance explanation for the line that the Branch Manager could act on.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. The available balance is budget less paid less committed. The forecast outturn is paid plus committed plus forecast. The variance is the outturn less the budget.

| Component | Available balance | Forecast outturn | Variance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Planned maintenance | A$1,150,000 − A$330,000 − A$600,000 = A$220,000 | A$330,000 + A$600,000 + A$200,000 = A$1,130,000 | A$20,000 under |
| Reactive repairs | A$450,000 − A$170,000 − A$110,000 = A$170,000 | A$170,000 + A$110,000 + A$330,000 = A$610,000 | A$160,000 over |
| Site services contracts | A$300,000 − A$80,000 − A$140,000 = A$80,000 | A$80,000 + A$140,000 + A$90,000 = A$310,000 | A$10,000 over |
| Travel, training and minor services | A$200,000 − A$40,000 − A$60,000 = A$100,000 | A$40,000 + A$60,000 + A$100,000 = A$200,000 | nil |
| Suppliers line | A$570,000 | A$2,250,000 | A$150,000 over |

The totals check: A$220,000 + A$170,000 + A$80,000 + A$100,000 = A$570,000, and A$160,000 + A$10,000 − A$20,000 = A$150,000 over. The share paid is A$620,000 ÷ A$2,100,000 = 29.5%. The share paid or committed is A$1,530,000 ÷ A$2,100,000 = 72.9%. Reactive repairs are A$160,000 ÷ A$450,000 = 35.6% over their allowance. Percentages are rounded to one decimal place.

The explanation Lena sends: "Suppliers: forecast outturn A$2,250,000 against a budget of A$2,100,000, A$150,000 (7.1%) over. The cause is reactive repairs, forecast at A$610,000 against an allowance of A$450,000, A$160,000 (35.6%) over, because roofs and plumbing at older sites are failing faster than the allowance assumed. A price rise of A$10,000 on a renewed site services contract adds to it, and planned maintenance is A$20,000 under. The variance is permanent unless the branch acts: A$570,000 remains uncommitted against A$720,000 of forecast need. Options to close the gap will reach the Branch Manager at this month's meeting."

Chapter 3 turns to the moment each of those commitments is created: the approval.
