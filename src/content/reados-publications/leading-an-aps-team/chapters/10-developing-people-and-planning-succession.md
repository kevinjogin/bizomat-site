A team can follow every rule in this book and still fail if what it knows sits in one person's head. Development is how people grow into bigger work. Succession is how the work carries on when someone takes leave, moves on or is promoted. They are the same job seen from two ends, and both belong to the manager.

This is an illustration. It is April 2027, the mid-point of NRIO's performance cycle (Chapter 5). Hannah holds a career conversation with each person, separate from the mid-point review. Marcus Tan, nine years at NRIO, tells her he wants to move into an EL1 role within two years and will apply when suitable roles are advertised. Hannah is glad for him. She also sees how much of the phones and email team runs through him alone.

## Development starts with a career conversation

Section 50 of the [Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) (Compilation No. 7) lists what a supervisor does to uphold the Employment Principle of effective performance. One item is "conducting, at least annually, career conversations that deal with the APS employee's performance, potential, aspirations, organisational fit and future opportunities" (s 50(b)). Section 51(c) gives the employee's side: "participating constructively in the Agency's performance management processes, including career conversations". At agency level, section 49(1)(a)(ii) has the agency head ensure performance policies that "proactively identify, foster and develop APS employees to fulfil their potential".

Hold the conversation apart from any rating, so this month's numbers do not crowd it out. Ask what the person wants to be doing in a year and in three, which work they enjoy and which they avoid, what they would like to learn and what would help. Listen more than you talk. Record the actions you agree, not the whole conversation, and promise only what you control (Chapter 1).

An aspiration is personal information. Marcus's wish to move on is his to share, not the team's to plan around in public. A good succession plan is written about roles and tasks, so it never needs to say who might leave.

This is an illustration. Hannah's conversations gave each person one development goal. Two matter for this chapter. Sophie Grant, who has coached Ruby as her buddy (Chapter 4), wants to see the correspondence and complaints side of the section. Mei Wong, now working three days a week (Chapter 9), wants to keep growing on those days and to try some team-leader work.

## A development plan names the work

Capability means what a person can actually do in a role: the skills, knowledge and judgement the work needs. The best guide to what a role needs is its duty statement and the work level standards for its classification, which section 50(c) of the Directions already links to each performance agreement. Start from the work, then find the learning.

Most of that learning happens on the job: a stretch task with a safety net, shadowing someone who does the work well, writing the procedure for a task, a short acting period, and feedback on each. Courses help when they serve a goal. A course with no task to apply it to is usually forgotten.

The Stewardship value describes an APS that "builds its capability and institutional knowledge" ([Public Service Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) s 10(6)). Section 17A(c) of the Directions says upholding it requires, among other things, supporting and contributing to "a workplace culture that sustains core knowledge, expertise, and standards of professionalism". In a section of nine people, development is how that value is met.

This is an illustration. Sophie's plan reads: goal, to run the complaints register and the daily allocation of correspondence for a week, with Leila checking, by the end of August; capability, NRIO's complaint categories, the acknowledgement and response standards (Chapter 5) and the allocation rules; learning, two hours a week alongside Leila from Monday 17 May, then a week running both tasks under supervision; support, Leila; evidence, the register and allocation log for that week; review, at Sophie's one-to-one in early September.

## Find the work only one person can do

A critical task is work that must be done on time for the section to meet its standards or obligations. List the critical tasks in each key role, name who else can do each one now, and mark the gaps. Task coverage is the number of critical tasks with a trained back-up divided by the number of critical tasks.

This is an illustration. Marcus's role has six critical tasks. Only two have anyone else able to do them: Hannah samples calls, and Sophie coaches new officers. The weekly roster, the morning queue check, the phone system's queue messages and settings, and the monthly call result run through Marcus alone. Only Marcus and NRIO's IT area hold administrator access to the phone system.

Look at your own role too. In a small section the manager is often the only back-up for the team leaders, which makes the manager a single point of failure twice over.

A written procedure for each single-person task is the cheapest control. It belongs in the records system, not a personal folder, so the knowledge stays when people move. Section 17A(e) of the Directions adds "ensuring complete, accurate, and appropriately accessible recordkeeping of key actions and decisions".

## Acting: open, fair and merit-aware

Acting is a temporary assignment of duties at a higher classification. The agency head may determine an employee's duties (Public Service Act s 25), and the Directions say that "the temporary assignment of duties to an APS employee at a higher classification" is not a promotion (s 6, note). The merit-based selection process the Directions require for engagement and promotion (ss 23 and 24) therefore does not apply to it as such.

The Employment Principles still do. The APS "makes fair employment decisions with a fair system of review" and provides workplaces "free from discrimination, patronage and favouritism" (Public Service Act s 10A(1)(a) and (f)), and an agency head "must uphold and promote" the principles (s 12). Review offers little protection here: action relating to the determination of an employee's duties is not a reviewable action under the Public Service Regulations 2023 unless it falls within narrow exceptions, such as a reduction in classification (s 37(2), table item 11). A fair process and a clear record are the main safeguards.

Who acts also shapes later selections. The Directions' definition of work-related qualities includes "the person's standard of work performance" and "the person's potential for further development" (s 5). Acting builds evidence of both, so repeatedly choosing the same person gives them a head start that others never had.

A merit-aware process borrows the logic of section 24 without its formality. Decide what the acting period needs before anyone is chosen. Tell every eligible officer in the section, part-time staff included. Use a short work sample and a conversation. Record the reasons for the choice and share them with those not chosen. Rotate opportunities over time. Check your agency's policy and enterprise agreement on higher duties and any allowance; this book gives no figures.

## Succession plans the role, not an heir

A succession plan says how each critical role keeps working when its holder is away or leaves: a trained back-up for each critical task, written procedures, people ready to act, and the development that gets them there. It plans for the role. It does not name a successor, and it cannot promise anyone a job.

If Marcus does leave, an ongoing APS 4 or APS 5 officer selected for his position would be promoted, and engagement and promotion decisions must be based on merit (s 10A(1)(c)). All eligible members of the community must have a reasonable opportunity to apply (s 10A(2)(a)), and the selection must meet the Directions, including notice in the Public Service Gazette (Directions s 25). Acting experience is evidence a candidate can offer, not an entitlement. A promotion to APS 6 falls in Group 6, so promotion review may apply (Chapter 3).

This is an illustration. Under NRIO's delegations the Director approves higher duties on Hannah's recommendation. Hannah's plan covers both team-leader roles, because a gap in either stops the section. It runs from the April conversations to the end of the performance cycle on Thursday 30 September 2027, and it uses two leave periods already booked: Marcus's three weeks from Monday 5 July and Leila's two weeks from Monday 13 September.

## Develop yourself as a manager

Section 50(f) of the Directions lists "working to improve the supervisor's capability in effectively managing the performance of APS employees, including through appropriate training". At agency level, section 49(1)(f) has the agency head support supervisors, "including by providing appropriate training in performance management". A new manager can ask for that support and should use it.

This is an illustration. In Hannah's own career conversation with the Director, they agreed three things. First, what the year had taught her to keep doing: checking her authority before acting, writing down facts rather than conclusions, and answering people on time. Second, one stretch goal for 2027–28: leading the section's workforce plan for the year with the Director, starting from the workload numbers. Third, support: the People Branch's training for managers on performance and conduct, and a monthly conversation with an experienced EL2 in another branch.

Hannah also planned her own cover. When she is on leave, Marcus and Leila act as Client Services Manager in turn, chosen and recorded in the same way. A temporary assignment from APS 6 to EL1 is not a promotion either, but it is valuable experience for both.

## When it goes wrong: the team leader who left

This is an illustration. Suppose that in March 2027, before any of this planning, Marcus had won an EL1 role in another agency and left after two weeks' notice.

Four of his six critical tasks had no back-up. Hannah would take the roster, the morning queue check and the monthly call result on top of her own job, at about six hours a week. The phone system would have no administrator in the section, so when a facility closed unexpectedly, the recorded message would keep giving callers old information until IT could change it. Nobody had acted in the role, so nobody was ready to.

An APS 6 vacancy needs its own merit process; the APS 5 merit list could not fill it (Chapter 3). Daniel's position took more than 15 weeks to fill, from late October to Tessa's first day on 15 February. At six hours a week, 15 weeks is 15 × 6 = 90 hours of Hannah's time, taken from the work only she can do, such as the mid-point reviews in April.

The controls are simple, and none needs Marcus to leave first: a back-up for every critical task, written procedures, a second administrator, and a pool of people who have acted and been chosen fairly.

### Worked answer

Coverage now. Marcus's role: 2 of 6 tasks, 2 ÷ 6 = 33.3 per cent. Leila's role: 2 of 5 (tasks 9 and 11), 2 ÷ 5 = 40.0 per cent. Both roles: 4 of 11 = 36.4 per cent. Every covered task depends on Hannah or Sophie, and Hannah appears three times.

The quickest gains come from the team leaders backing each other up. Leila learns tasks 1, 2, 3 and 6; Marcus learns tasks 7, 8 and 10. That is 4 + 3 = 7 tasks to transfer. With the four tasks already covered, all 11 then have a trained back-up by 30 September: (4 + 7) ÷ 11 = 100 per cent.

| Step | What | By |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Procedures | Marcus writes tasks 1, 2, 3 and 6; Leila writes 7, 8 and 10; both go in the records system | Fri 21 May |
| Second administrator | Hannah asks IT to give Leila access to the phone system, after training | Fri 28 May |
| Cross-training | Leila and Marcus each run the other's tasks for a week, with the owner checking | Fri 2 Jul |
| Pool shadowing | Mei with Marcus, Sophie with Leila, two hours a week each for the 7 weeks from 17 May | Fri 2 Jul |
| Acting, July | Mei acts in Marcus's role on her three days; Leila covers his critical tasks on Mondays and Fridays | Mon 5 to Fri 23 Jul |
| Acting, September | Sophie acts in Leila's role for the two weeks; Marcus covers anything she escalates | Mon 13 to Fri 24 Sep |
| Review | Coverage, procedures and acting reports, then into each person's career conversation | Thu 30 Sep |

The arithmetic behind the acting rows. Marcus's leave is 3 weeks × 5 days = 15 working days: Mei works 3 × 3 = 9 of them and Leila covers the other 3 × 2 = 6. Leila's leave is 2 × 5 = 10 days, all acted by Sophie. So Mei acts for 9 days and Sophie for 10, close to equal. Shadowing costs 2 people × 2 hours × 7 weeks = 28 hours, which Hannah books in the roster: 28 ÷ 7,140 = 0.39 per cent of the section's annual handling capacity with Mei at three days a week (Chapter 9).

The fairness checks. The expression of interest went to every officer, and the requirements were set before anyone applied. Mei's three days did not count against her; the plan was shaped around them. The reasons for each allocation are on file: Sophie's period matches her development goal, and Mei's fits her days, because the roster is built a week ahead and Leila covers the Mondays. The next acting period goes first to whoever has acted least. Nothing in the plan mentions Marcus's career goal, and nothing promises either officer a promotion. Hannah's own leave is covered by Marcus and Leila in turn.

This book began with the rules behind every people decision and ends with the people who will make those decisions next. The method has been the same in every chapter. Find the rule and the person who holds the power. Turn the work into hours before arguing about people. Recruit on merit, set expectations that two people could check, and raise problems early, privately and on the record. Report suspected misconduct rather than investigating it. Treat aggression and workload as safety risks. Answer requests on time and with reasons. Build a team that does not depend on any one person, its manager included. Hannah's first year was demanding but not unusual. What carried her through was that each decision could be explained, checked and handed on.
