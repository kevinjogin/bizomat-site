Owners need three views of the same business: what work has been earned, what costs belong to that work and what cash has actually moved. Combining them in one mental number creates false confidence.

## Revenue versus receipts
An invoice may record a sale before a customer pays. A cash receipt may settle an invoice issued in an earlier period. A customer deposit may create a future delivery or refund obligation. Track **invoice issue date**, **due date**, **amount received**, **balance outstanding** and **follow-up status**. The exact timing of revenue recognition depends on the applicable accounting basis and circumstances.

## Cost categories
**Variable costs** generally change with volume: materials, packaging, transaction fees and piecework where relevant. **Fixed or committed costs** may include rent, basic subscriptions and certain overheads. In practice some costs are semi-variable or stepped, such as adding another shift or licence tier. Classify based on the decision being analysed rather than assuming every cost behaves perfectly.

Separate operating expenses from major equipment purchases and financing flows. A new machine may create a large cash outflow without becoming an equally large immediate profit-and-loss expense. Debt repayments include principal, which typically affects cash but not operating profit in the same way as interest. Use proper accounting records for authoritative treatment.

## A simple profit illustration
For a month with **$30,000 of sales**, **$12,000 of materials** and **$13,000 of other recognised business expenses**, simplified profit before tax is:

**$30,000 − $12,000 − $13,000 = $5,000.**

That does not mean $5,000 is safe to withdraw. The business may have overdue invoices, tax payments, loan principal, purchases of assets or future delivery commitments.

## Improve the monthly review
Compare actual to budget and previous periods. Identify the top three costs by value and ask whether each creates necessary value, whether the terms can be improved and whether the activity is profitable once direct costs and required labour are included.

Track gross margin or contribution margin consistently. **Gross profit** and **contribution** are not interchangeable unless the expense classification matches. Clearly define the metric on every dashboard so trends remain comparable.

## Practical owner review
Ask: How many invoices are overdue? What is the ageing of receivables? How much of reported sales have been collected? Which costs rise faster than revenue? Are discounts eroding contribution? Are tax and cash commitments separately funded?

## Exercise
Take the last three complete months. Prepare one table with **sales recorded | cash received | direct costs | overheads | profit before tax | closing cash | unpaid invoices**. Explain every large difference between profit and changes in cash. If you cannot reconcile it, that is an immediate bookkeeping improvement task.

## Extended study: applying the method

### Understand the difference between transaction dates

A sale can have an order date, fulfilment date, invoice date, payment due date and actual collection date. These may fall in different accounting periods. Maintain a reconciliation of orders, invoices, credit notes, receipts and outstanding balances. Credits, returns and discounts should be recorded rather than silently netted in a way that hides their causes. In a service business, work in progress and milestone acceptance may further complicate when revenue is recognised. The business's chosen accounting policies and applicable tax basis determine the formal entries, so operational dashboards should disclose their definitions rather than pretending one timestamp tells the whole story.

### Cost behaviour and decisions

When deciding whether to accept one additional order, identify costs caused specifically by that order. When deciding whether to maintain a business line for a year, include the staff, space, systems and management needed to sustain it. One-time investments should be reviewed across their useful life and cash commitment, not treated like consumables. Insurance may appear fixed over a year but change when risk exposure crosses a threshold. Software may be charged per user, making it stepped rather than perfectly fixed. Classification should follow the question being asked and be reviewed whenever operations change.

### The owner's labour and drawings

In a sole-trader business, personal drawings are generally not a wage expense for profit calculation. Yet a business model that only appears viable because the owner provides unlimited unpaid hours is economically fragile. Track owner delivery and management hours separately; compare a realistic replacement cost for planning, without confusing that management estimate with an accounting expense. If the owner hopes to sell the enterprise, buyers may ask what earnings remain after replacing the founder's work. Be explicit about the distinction among accounting profit, available cash, personal withdrawals and sustainable earnings.

### Reconcile profit to cash movement

Begin with profit for a period, then examine non-cash charges, changes in receivables, inventory, payables, taxes owed, asset purchases, loan principal and financing transactions. A simple comparison is informative even without preparing formal cash-flow statements. If profit is $6,000 but receivables rise by $10,000 with everything else unchanged, cash from operations may be under pressure despite healthy reported income. Conversely, collecting old debts can increase cash without producing new current-period profit. Use a qualified bookkeeper or accountant to handle detailed financial statements and classification.

### Analyse profitable versus unprofitable work

A customer bringing high revenue can still impose expensive customisation, rushed freight, late payments, warranty claims or repeated support. Estimate contribution by customer or order using a consistent method. Include legitimately attributable direct costs and annotate uncertain allocations. Then examine factors behind poor performance. Better scope, minimum order sizes, deposits, lead times or pricing can sometimes improve economics without increasing total volume. Avoid cutting a customer solely because a crude overhead allocation shows a loss; separate genuine incremental cost from accounting allocations that would remain after the customer left.

### A monthly close routine

Set a fixed closing date. Reconcile bank accounts and payment providers, enter supplier bills, confirm sales invoices and credits, review unpaid customer balances, reconcile payroll and statutory liabilities where applicable, identify unusual purchases and explain material variance against budget. Record who prepared and approved the review. Keep a commentary line beside each important number: reason, action and owner. A modest, consistently maintained monthly close is more useful than a complicated dashboard with inaccurate or stale inputs.

### Diagnostic scenario and worksheet

Assume current-month invoices of $42,000, customer payments of $27,000 and cash operating outflows of $31,000. Ignoring other movements, cash fell by $4,000 even though invoice value exceeded payments by $15,000. That alone cannot tell us profit because invoices are not identical to recognised revenue and outflows are not identical to expenses. The first questions are: what aged receivables remain, what costs belong in the period and which cash outflows concern assets, debt or taxes? Prepare four linked schedules rather than inventing a single margin from bank transactions.
