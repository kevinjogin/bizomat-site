Profit is an accounting measurement. Cash is what pays suppliers, staff, lenders and shareholders. A good financial review needs both.

## Operating cash flow

Operating cash flow measures the cash produced or used by normal operations after working-capital movements. It is not identical to net profit.

A business expanding rapidly may have to purchase materials and extend credit to customers. That can absorb cash even while sales and accounting profit grow. This is not automatically bad, but it deserves explanation.

## Capital expenditure

Capital expenditure, often shortened to **capex**, is money invested in longer-lived assets such as machinery, buildings and production equipment. Some projects maintain existing operations; others increase future capacity.

A manufacturing company cannot simply stop maintaining plant and still expect safe, reliable production. The important distinction is between spending required to protect existing earning power and investment intended to create additional earning power.

## Free cash flow: a useful starting measure

A common simplified calculation is:

**Free cash flow ≈ Operating cash flow − Capital expenditure**

Consider two fictional businesses:

| Annual cash measure | Stable Services | Machinery Works |
|---|---:|---:|
| Operating cash flow | $2.0m | $2.0m |
| Capital expenditure | $0.3m | $1.5m |
| Simplified free cash flow | $1.7m | $0.5m |

Both generate the same operating cash flow, but the amount remaining after capital expenditure differs. This alone does not prove that Stable Services is better; Machinery Works might be building profitable new capacity.

## Ask what the spending achieves

Review several years rather than one. Look for repeated spending that merely replaces worn-out assets. Evaluate whether larger investments subsequently lead to increased sales, margins, production capacity or returns.

If you are reading a manufacturing annual report, ask the operational questions too: Is a new plant necessary? How old is the equipment? Are breakdowns rising? Is the business spending to reduce unit costs or simply to avoid falling behind?

### Key takeaway

**A business is more financially flexible when it consistently generates cash after the investment needed to sustain its operations.**

### Quick reference

- Compare net profit with operating cash flow.
- Identify large movements in receivables, inventory and payables.
- Examine recurring capital expenditure separately from major expansion projects.
- Treat simplified free cash flow as a starting measure, not a complete valuation.
