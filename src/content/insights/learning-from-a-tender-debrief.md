For a business that wins work through tenders, losing is part of the job. Even strong bidders lose more tenders than they win, and a small engineering, construction or services business may submit dozens of proposals a year. Each one costs estimating time, technical input, management review and sometimes design work. A business winning one bid in five is spending most of its tendering effort on work it does not get.

What separates businesses that improve from those that keep repeating the same results is usually not the quality of any single proposal. It is whether they learn systematically from the outcome. A losing bid contains information: about the buyer's priorities, about competitors, about the business's own price and presentation, and about whether it should have bid at all. Most of that information is lost because nobody asks for it, or because feedback is collected and never used.

This article explains how suppliers can obtain useful feedback through tender **debriefs**, the questions worth asking, how to interpret what they hear and how to combine feedback across many bids into a **win-loss analysis** that improves future bid decisions. It is general information, written for suppliers to private and public sector buyers.

## Why debriefs are worth the effort

A **debrief** is a conversation or written explanation, usually offered by the buyer after a tender is decided, about how the supplier's tender was assessed. Government buyers often offer debriefs as standard practice, and many procurement frameworks encourage them. Private buyers are not obliged to, but many will if asked politely and promptly.

Debriefs are valuable for reasons that go beyond the single tender:

- **They reveal how buyers actually evaluate.** The weightings in a tender document describe intention; a debrief shows what really decided the outcome.
- **They correct internal myths.** Teams often assume they lost on price. Debriefs frequently show otherwise.
- **They build relationships.** A professional, curious debrief conversation leaves a better impression than silence or complaint, and buyers remember suppliers who want to improve.
- **They inform the next bid decision.** Feedback may show that the business was never competitive for that type of work, or that it came close and should bid again.

## Ask early and set the right tone

Request a debrief soon after the result is announced, while the evaluation is fresh in the buyer's mind. Keep the request short and positive: thank the buyer for the opportunity, say you would value feedback to improve future submissions and ask whether a short meeting or call is possible.

Tone matters. A debrief is not an appeal. If the conversation starts with an argument about the decision, the buyer will become guarded and the business will learn little. If there is a genuine concern about the fairness of the process, it should be raised separately and carefully, using any complaint mechanism the buyer provides, rather than turning the debrief into a dispute. Most government procurement frameworks have formal complaint processes, and they are the right channel for serious concerns.

## Prepare before the conversation

A good debrief is prepared, not improvised:

- **Re-read your submission and the tender documents.** Know what you said and what the buyer asked for.
- **Write down your own assessment first.** Where did you think you were strong and weak? Comparing this with the buyer's view is one of the most useful parts of the exercise.
- **Decide who attends.** Usually the bid lead and someone responsible for the technical or commercial content. Keep it small.
- **Prepare specific questions.** General questions get general answers.

## Questions worth asking

Buyers will not share competitors' prices or confidential information, and should not. Within that limit, the following questions usually produce useful answers:

| Area | Questions to ask |
|---|---|
| Overall | How did our submission rank overall, and roughly how far were we from the preferred tenderer? |
| Criteria | How did we score against each criterion? Where did we lose the most ground? |
| Evidence | Which of our claims were most convincing, and which needed stronger evidence? |
| Methodology | Did our delivery approach address your main concerns? Was anything unclear or missing? |
| Price | Was our price competitive, within the range, or well outside it? Did any part of our pricing raise questions? |
| Risk and terms | Did our qualifications, exclusions or departures from the contract affect the assessment? |
| People | Were our nominated personnel and their experience persuasive? |
| Presentation | Was the submission easy to evaluate? Was anything hard to find? |
| Future | What would a stronger submission from us look like next time? Are there upcoming opportunities we should know about? |

Listen more than you speak. Take notes. Thank the buyer for its time, and avoid defending the submission point by point. The goal is understanding, not winning the argument.

## Interpreting feedback honestly

Feedback is useful only if it is interpreted honestly. A few patterns are common:

**"Your price was high."** This may be true, or it may be a polite way of saying the buyer did not see enough value to justify the price. Ask whether the price was outside the competitive range or simply higher than the preferred tenderer, and whether stronger evidence of value would have changed the outcome.

**"Your submission was good but another was better."** Push gently for specifics. Which criteria separated the two? Was it experience, methodology, people, risk or price?

**"You did not demonstrate relevant experience."** Often the business had the experience but described it poorly: no specific projects, no outcomes, no referees, or examples that did not match the buyer's situation.

**"Your qualifications concerned us."** Exclusions and departures from the contract may protect the business, but they can make the tender look riskier or harder to compare. Consider whether each qualification is necessary, and whether it could be priced or explained instead.

**"The submission was hard to evaluate."** Evaluators score what they can find. A response that does not follow the buyer's structure loses marks it may have deserved. The article [A tender response is a decision package](/insights/writing-tender-responses-buyers-can-evaluate/) explains how to structure responses around the buyer's criteria.

Record the feedback in writing on the day, including what was said and what the team concluded.

## Debrief your wins too

Most businesses only ask for feedback when they lose, which means they learn what goes wrong but not what works. A short conversation after a win is just as valuable. Ask the buyer what made the difference: which parts of the submission were most persuasive, what reassured them about risk and what they will be watching most closely during delivery. The answers tell the business what to keep doing in future bids, and they set useful expectations for the contract itself. A buyer who says "your shutdown staging plan was the deciding factor" has also told the delivery team where its attention must go.

## Debriefs with government buyers

Government buyers in Australia generally operate under procurement rules that emphasise fairness, transparency and value for money, and many offer debriefs to unsuccessful tenderers as standard practice. A few points help suppliers use them well:

- **Check the timeframe.** Some processes set a period after notification within which a debrief must be requested.
- **Expect a structured format.** Government debriefs often follow a template that explains scores against criteria without disclosing other tenderers' information.
- **Separate learning from complaint.** If there is a genuine concern about the process, use the agency's complaint mechanism rather than the debrief, and be aware that complaint processes may also have time limits.
- **Ask about future opportunities.** Agencies often publish forward procurement plans and may explain how to register for panels or future approaches to market.

## Turn lessons into a bid improvement plan

Feedback only helps if someone owns the response. After each debrief, record one to three concrete actions, such as adding a case study for a sector, strengthening a safety management section, developing a personnel profile or adjusting how qualifications are presented, and assign an owner and a date. Review the list each quarter. Over time, the business builds a library of improved content, evidence and templates that raises the quality of every future submission without increasing the effort for each one.

## From individual debriefs to win-loss analysis

A single debrief helps with one buyer. The larger benefit comes from combining results across many bids in a simple **win-loss analysis**. The aim is to see patterns that no single tender reveals.

### Record every bid consistently

For each tender, record a small set of facts:

- buyer, sector and type of work;
- estimated value and duration;
- how the opportunity was found and whether there was prior contact with the buyer;
- number of competitors, where known;
- estimated cost of preparing the bid;
- the business's assessment of fit before bidding;
- outcome, rank where known, and main reasons from the debrief; and
- whether the price was within the competitive range.

A spreadsheet is enough. Consistency matters more than sophistication.

### Look for the patterns

After a year or two, the record usually shows several clear patterns:

- **Win rates differ sharply by type of work or buyer.** Most businesses win much more often where they have prior relationships and directly relevant experience.
- **Unsolicited tenders rarely win.** Bids responding to public advertisements without any prior contact often have low success rates, especially for larger contracts.
- **Some losses are always on the same criterion.** Repeated feedback about methodology, safety systems or personnel points to a capability or presentation gap.
- **Price is less often the reason than people think.** Many losses turn on evidence, risk or relevance.
- **Bid cost is high relative to likely return.** Some categories of work consume large bid effort for little success.

### Use the patterns in the bid decision

The most valuable output of win-loss analysis is a better **bid or no-bid decision**. Bidding less often, on opportunities where the business has a real chance, usually increases both the win rate and the quality of each submission. The [qualifying opportunities](/insights/qualifying-opportunities/) article describes how to judge whether a sales opportunity is worth pursuing, and the same discipline applies to tenders.

A simple bid decision can score each opportunity on factors such as relationship with the buyer, relevant experience, capacity to deliver, competitive position, price risk, contract terms and strategic value, and compare the expected return with the cost of bidding.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The business, figures and results are hypothetical.

A small electrical and controls contractor reviews its tendering over two years. It submitted 46 tenders and won 8, a win rate of about 17%. Its estimating manager calculates that the average bid cost about $3,500 in staff time, so the business spent roughly $161,000 on tenders, or about $20,000 for each contract won.

Sorting the results reveals a sharper picture:

| Category | Tenders | Wins | Win rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Existing clients, maintenance and upgrades | 12 | 6 | 50% |
| New clients, food and beverage sector, with prior contact | 10 | 2 | 20% |
| Public advertisements, no prior contact | 24 | 0 | 0% |

Debrief notes show that in the public tenders, the business repeatedly lost on "demonstrated experience with similar projects" and "methodology", not on price. Its submissions used generic project descriptions and a standard method statement.

The business makes three changes. It stops responding to public advertisements unless it has directly relevant experience and has spoken with the buyer before closing. It builds a library of detailed project case studies, with outcomes and referees, organised by sector. And it rewrites its methodology templates to be tailored for each bid. In the following year, it submits 24 tenders and wins 8, a win rate of about 33%, while spending roughly half as much on tendering.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Ask for a debrief every time.** It costs little, and buyers who are asked often respond.
- **Keep debriefs constructive.** Raise genuine process concerns separately, through the proper channel.
- **Record outcomes consistently.** A simple log of every bid is the foundation of improvement.
- **Review the log twice a year.** Look for patterns by buyer, sector, source of the opportunity and reason for loss.
- **Tighten the bid decision.** Decline opportunities where the business has little chance, and redirect the effort to better ones.
- **Fix recurring weaknesses.** Build evidence libraries, refine methodologies and develop key personnel profiles where feedback points.
- **Share lessons.** Make sure the people who prepare bids hear the feedback, not just the manager who attended the debrief.

## Common mistakes

- **Not asking.** Many suppliers never request feedback, especially after a loss they found disappointing.
- **Arguing in the debrief.** Defensiveness shuts down the conversation and damages the relationship.
- **Assuming it was price.** Without feedback, teams often misattribute losses to price and cut margins unnecessarily.
- **Collecting feedback and filing it.** Feedback that does not change the next bid has no value.
- **Bidding on everything.** A low win rate across many bids consumes effort better spent on fewer, stronger submissions.
- **Ignoring wins.** Wins contain lessons too. Ask successful buyers what made the difference.

## Questions to ask

- Did we request a debrief for every tender we lost, and for those we won?
- What did the buyer say separated us from the preferred tenderer?
- Which criteria do we repeatedly lose marks on?
- How does our win rate vary by buyer, sector and how the opportunity was found?
- How much does each bid cost us, and how much does each win cost us in total bid effort?
- Which opportunities should we stop bidding for?
- What evidence, case studies or capabilities would most improve our next submissions?

## Bringing it together

Losing a tender is expensive only if nothing is learned from it. Debriefs, requested promptly and conducted constructively, reveal how buyers really evaluate and where a submission fell short. Recording every bid and its outcome turns individual feedback into a win-loss analysis that shows where the business wins, where it rarely does and why. The most valuable result is usually not a better proposal but a better decision about which tenders to pursue: fewer bids, chosen more carefully, prepared more thoroughly and won more often.
