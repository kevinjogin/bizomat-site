Work changes constantly. New software replaces familiar systems. Customers change how they buy. Automation reshapes jobs on the factory floor. A new manager arrives with different expectations. A team becomes younger, more digital or more diverse. A business that once thrived on one product has to find another.

People who adapt to these changes thrive. People who resist them often find themselves left behind, however skilled they were in the old way of working. A line often attributed to Charles Darwin, though actually a later paraphrase of his ideas, makes the point: it is not the strongest or the most intelligent who survive, but those most responsive to change.

Adaptability is not a fixed trait that people either have or lack. It is a set of capabilities that can be developed. This article explains three kinds of flexibility that make up adaptability, offers practical ways to build each, describes a four-part cycle for personal development (adapt, apply, evolve and grow) and shows how the way you interpret setbacks shapes your response to them.

## Why adaptability matters more than ever

Consider a few common situations:

- A supervisor who has always written project plans on paper is asked to lead a team of younger staff who expect plans in shared digital tools, messages and online dashboards.
- A machinist who has programmed by hand for twenty years is asked to use CAM software.
- A sales representative who relied on in-person visits must now win business through video calls and online channels.
- A business owner whose main customer moves production overseas must find new markets.

In each case, technical skill alone is not enough. Success depends on being willing and able to change how one works.

## Three kinds of flexibility

Adaptability can be understood as three related kinds of flexibility.

### 1. Cognitive flexibility

Cognitive flexibility is the ability to think in different ways, consider different strategies and switch approaches when the situation demands it. People with strong cognitive flexibility:

- Learn from past experience without being trapped by it.
- Recognise when an established method has stopped working.
- Consider several options before deciding.
- Integrate new information quickly.

It shows up in strategy, decision-making and everyday problem-solving. A manager who notices that the weekly paper report is no longer read, and switches to a short dashboard, is showing cognitive flexibility.

### 2. Emotional flexibility

Emotional flexibility is the ability to understand and manage your own emotions and those of others during change. People with strong emotional flexibility:

- Acknowledge their own discomfort with change without being controlled by it.
- Recognise and respect the emotions of people affected by change.
- Stay open to conversation rather than shutting it down.

A leader who lacks emotional flexibility may dismiss people's concerns about a change, refuse to discuss them and push ahead regardless. That usually increases resistance. Adaptation is a two-way process between leaders and the people experiencing change.

### 3. Dispositional flexibility

Dispositional flexibility is a general orientation that is both realistic and optimistic. People with strong dispositional flexibility:

- See difficult situations clearly, without denial.
- Do not trust any situation blindly, nor view it as entirely negative.
- Treat change as a source of opportunity as well as risk.

Consider an owner who has to close a business. It is a painful decision, and sadness and low morale are natural. A dispositionally flexible person accepts the reality, grieves the loss, but does not see the situation as hopeless. They recognise that their skills and experience can build something new.

## Ways to become more adaptable

### Be curious

- Ask questions whenever you do not understand something.
- Explore new ideas before deciding whether they are useful.
- Let yourself be surprised by what you learn.

### Have a backup plan

Plan B and plan C reduce the fear of change. If you know what you will do if the first approach fails, you can commit to trying it with less anxiety.

### Build a support system

You do not have to manage change alone. Family, friends, mentors, colleagues and professional networks provide perspective and encouragement. Employers can help by encouraging peer support during major changes.

### Immerse yourself in new environments

When you face something new, such as a new role, team, technology or market, engage with it fully: meet the people, learn the language and try things rather than watching from a distance.

### Change your thought patterns

Watch for thoughts such as "we've always done it this way" or "that won't work here". They are signs of rigid thinking. Ask instead: "What would we do if we were starting today?"

### Take calculated risks

Nobody reaches the top without taking some risk. Step outside your comfort zone and take on challenges, but thoughtfully. Putting everything at stake on a single bet is not courage. It is recklessness.

### Embrace new experiences

Treat each experience, including failures, as a source of learning. Build a learning culture around you, whether you lead a company or work alone.

### Change one routine each day

A simple practice: change at least one small routine every day, such as your route to work, the time you exercise, what you have for breakfast or the order in which you tackle tasks. Small, low-stakes changes train your comfort with larger ones.

## A four-part development cycle: adapt, apply, evolve, grow

Adaptability is the first of four related capabilities in a useful cycle of personal development.

### Adapt

Respond to changes in your environment, as described above.

### Apply

Knowledge is valuable only when it is applied. "Applying yourself" means committing fully to your work and using your abilities effectively. Many capable people never realise their potential because they are not trained properly, lack confidence or work in environments where they hold back. An introverted engineer with excellent ideas may stay silent in meetings with an intimidating manager, so the business never benefits.

Ways to apply yourself:

- **Discover new skills.** You only find out what you are capable of by fully applying yourself, often discovering abilities you did not know you had.
- **See opportunities.** People who engage fully notice openings for new roles, projects and improvements, and pay closer attention to detail.
- **Grow your network.** Connections create opportunities and responsibilities that draw out your capabilities.
- **Value yourself.** Believe in your ability, and your contributions will start influencing important decisions.
- **Reinvent yourself.** As you recognise your capabilities, look for new ways to handle your roles and responsibilities.

### Evolve

To evolve is to develop gradually through applying what you learn. If you read an article or watch a training video and then forget it, nothing changes. If you apply it, you evolve, and you remember it far longer.

Ways to evolve:

- **Model successful people.** Observe how people you admire behave, think and work, and adapt what suits you.
- **Make small improvements.** You cannot change your whole personality at once. Pick one area, such as confidence, listening or organisation, and improve it step by step.
- **Ignore the fear of disapproval.** The question "will people dislike me if I change?" holds many people back. Some people may not like it. Focus on becoming who you want to be.

### Grow

Growth is ongoing development, personal and professional. It should be steady rather than sudden: rapid growth without foundations often collapses.

Habits that support growth:

- **Show kindness** to people around you. Kindness builds patience, empathy and relationships.
- **Meditate or reflect** for a few minutes each day to calm your mind and focus on your goals.
- **Read something every day.**

## The power of interpretation

A key part of adaptability is how you interpret events. An event has no meaning in itself. People give it meaning through their interpretation, and their interpretation shapes what they do next.

Two siblings lose a parent. One says, "My life is over." The other says, "I need to become more responsible now." Two business owners lose their largest client. One says, "My business is finished." The other says, "This is the push I needed to diversify and take the business to the next level." The event is the same. The interpretations, and therefore the actions, differ.

### Three principles

1. **Don't see things as worse than they are.**
2. **Try to see things as they are.**
3. **Look for how things could become better than they are.**

### Goal versus problem

You cannot focus fully on a goal and a problem at the same time. When you focus on the problem, you lose sight of the goal. When you focus on the goal, the problem becomes something to work through rather than a wall. Acknowledge problems honestly, then turn your attention to the goal and the actions that move you towards it.

### A three-by-three exercise

When something negative happens:

1. **Find three positive messages** in it. What opportunity, warning or lesson does it contain?
2. **Identify three things you have learned** from it.
3. **Define three actions** you will take in response.

This is not about pretending bad things are good. It is about choosing an interpretation that leads to constructive action. Like any framework, it works only when practised consistently.

## Adaptability for leaders

Leaders shape how adaptable their organisations become:

- **Explain why change is needed**, not just what is changing.
- **Acknowledge the emotional side of change**, and give people space to raise concerns.
- **Adapt your own style** to your team. A leader who expects everyone to work exactly as they did twenty years ago will struggle to lead a modern team.
- **Model learning.** When leaders visibly learn new tools and admit what they do not know, others feel safer doing the same.
- **Reward experimentation**, including thoughtful attempts that do not succeed.
- **Provide support**: training, time, resources and peer help during transitions.

## A worked example

A production supervisor with 25 years' experience is asked to lead the introduction of a digital production-tracking system. His first reaction is resistance: the paper system works, he is not comfortable with technology and he worries about looking incompetent in front of younger team members.

He talks to a mentor, who helps him use the three-by-three exercise. Positive messages: the plant is investing in its future, he has been trusted to lead the change and he can learn a valuable new skill. Lessons: his resistance stems mainly from fear of looking foolish, and his team's knowledge of the process is what will make the system work. Actions: attend vendor training early, ask a technically confident team member to be his co-champion, and run the paper and digital systems side by side for a month.

He also practises emotional flexibility with his team, listening to older operators' concerns and arranging extra training for them. Three months later, the system is running well, he has become its most effective advocate and he uses its data to cut changeover times by 20 per cent.

## Common mistakes

- **Equating experience with expertise.** Experience in the old way of working can become a barrier if it prevents learning.
- **Changing everything at once**, which overwhelms people.
- **Ignoring emotions** during change, your own or others'.
- **Treating adaptability as agreement.** Being adaptable does not mean accepting every change uncritically. It means engaging thoughtfully, raising concerns constructively and adjusting when change is warranted.
- **Waiting for certainty.** Change rarely comes with complete information.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can older workers be as adaptable as younger ones?** Yes. Adaptability depends on mindset and practice, not age. Experienced workers often adapt very effectively once they understand the reasons for change and receive appropriate support, and their experience helps them spot problems others miss.

**How can I tell if I am being adaptable or just giving in?** Adaptability involves judgement. Engage with the change, understand its reasons, raise concerns constructively and then commit to making the agreed approach work, while staying alert to evidence that adjustments are needed.

**Is adaptability something employers can hire for?** Partly. Interview questions about how candidates handled past changes reveal a lot. But adaptability also depends heavily on the environment, so leaders should build cultures that support it.

## Summary

Adaptability, the ability to respond effectively to change, is increasingly essential at work. It combines cognitive flexibility (thinking in different ways), emotional flexibility (managing your own and others' feelings during change) and dispositional flexibility (a realistic, optimistic outlook). Build it through curiosity, backup plans, support systems, immersion in new situations, calculated risks and small daily changes to routine. Continue through a cycle of adapting, applying yourself fully, evolving through small improvements and growing steadily. Choose interpretations of setbacks that lead to constructive action, using three positive messages, three lessons and three actions. Leaders who explain, listen, model learning and support their teams build adaptable organisations.
