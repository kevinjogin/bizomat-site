At Senate Estimates, officials answer questions in public and on the record, often with no warning of what will be asked. Every answer goes into the transcript. A wrong number said with confidence can follow an agency for months, and once the hearing is over, correcting it takes a letter.

The briefing pack stands between the witness and a guess. A brief holds up when the witness can answer from it accurately, can see where the answer stops, and knows when to take the rest on notice.

This is an illustration. On Wednesday 2 June 2027, the Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office (NRIO) appears before a Senate legislation committee at its Budget estimates hearing. The date is fictional. Two days earlier, on Monday 31 May, the Minister decided Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP): 31 grants worth A$9.98 million, 2 of them approved against officials' recommendations. Ravi Menon, the Senior Parliamentary Officer, has built a briefing pack of 38 issue briefs, and one of them has to be rewritten in a day.

## What Estimates is for

Estimates hearings let the Senate examine the government's proposed spending and how agencies administer the money they already have. Senate legislation committees hold them, portfolio by portfolio, at times the Senate sets. A minister who sits in the Senate attends with the officials, often representing a portfolio minister from the other House, and officials answer questions about programs, spending, staffing and administration. This book calls that person "the minister at the table", to keep them apart from NRIO's own Minister.

For an agency, the hearing tests three things: whether its officials know the facts, whether they can say them plainly, and whether what they say matches what the agency has said before, in answers, reports, media releases and on its website. Senators and their staff compare. A figure that differs from the annual report without an explanation is a question in itself.

Evidence at a hearing is part of "proceedings in Parliament". Section 16(2) of the [Parliamentary Privileges Act 1987](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03430/latest/text) defines the term to include "the giving of evidence before a House or a committee, and evidence so given", and section 16(3) limits how courts and tribunals may use those proceedings. Section 12 protects witnesses: no one may influence a person's evidence "by fraud, intimidation, force or threat", or penalise a person on account of evidence given. That protection covers the evidence and the witness. It does not make a departmental brief secret. Whether a particular document is covered by section 16 is a legal question for your legal area, and Chapter 4 says more about privilege.

## Building the pack

A briefing pack is the set of documents the witnesses take to the table. Its core is a set of issue briefs, one for each topic likely to be raised, each short enough to read in the minute before an answer. Around them sit an overview of the agency and its budget, a list of recent and sensitive matters, the answers the agency has given before on the same topics, and a list of the officials in the room and what each can speak to.

This is an illustration. Ravi built NRIO's pack in four weeks. He chose the 38 topics with Clare Whitfield, the Parliamentary and Accountability Manager, and the line areas, from four sources: what Senators had asked NRIO before, what had appeared in the media and the Minister's correspondence, what was new in the Budget, and what NRIO knew was coming but was not yet public. Each line area drafted the briefs it owned, on one template. Ravi checked every number against its source and every brief against NRIO's earlier answers, and the head of each line area cleared that area's briefs before the pack went to the Chief Executive Officer (CEO).


Three habits make a pack work. One template, so the reader finds the same thing in the same place in every brief. One owner per brief, a named officer who can be reached during the hearing. And one version: when a fact changes, the brief is reissued with a new date and the old copy is taken out of the folder. Two versions of a brief at the table is how a witness ends up quoting the wrong one.

## The parts of an issue brief

An issue brief covers one topic. It is written for a reader under pressure, who will look at it for a minute, find the answer and look up again. A useful template has parts like these, in about this order.

```figure
type: steps
title: The parts of an issue brief
caption: The key facts carry the most risk, so each number sits with its definition, its date and its source.
- Key messages | three or four plain sentences the witness can say aloud
- * Key facts | each number with its definition, its as-at date and its source
- If asked | the likely follow-up questions, each with a short, sourced answer
- Background | what happened, in date order, for a reader who needs more
- Said before | earlier evidence, answers, reports and releases on the topic
- Sensitivities | where the line falls: what belongs to the minister, and what is not public
- Owner and clearance | the officer to call, who cleared the brief and who checked the numbers, with dates
```

The key facts are where a brief is most likely to fail. Each number needs three things beside it. Its definition: "applications" can mean received, eligible, assessed or funded, and each is a different number. Its date: a reader needs to know whether the number could have changed since. Its source: a document the checker can open, which also tells the witness that someone did.

The "if asked" answers are what save a witness. A good brief anticipates the second question, because that is where a witness who has handled the first one well is most tempted to improvise. Write each answer as the sentence the witness would say.

"Said before" protects consistency. If the agency gave a different number last time, the brief says why, so that the witness can explain the difference instead of discovering it at the table.

## Numbers that survive a follow-up question

An invented number is easy to catch in a check. The harder error is a true number attached to the wrong words.

This is an illustration. RCFP Round 1 has several numbers that sound alike. 186 applications were received. Of those, 9 were ineligible and were not assessed, so 186 − 9 = 177 were assessed against the published criteria. The Minister approved 31 grants, worth A$9.98 million. Officials had recommended 29 of them (31 − 2 = 29). The other 2 were approved against officials' recommendations.

```figure
type: funnel
title: RCFP Round 1 numbers that sound alike
caption: This is an illustration. Each stage is a different number with a different meaning. "Assessed" is 177, not 186.
- Applications received | 186
- Assessed against the criteria | 177 | 9 ineligible, not assessed
- Grants approved | 31 | A$9.98 million
- * Approved against officials' recommendations | 2 | A$400,000
```

Even a simple question can have two right answers, depending on the base. "What share of applications succeeded?" could mean 31 ÷ 186 = 16.7 per cent of applications received, or 31 ÷ 177 = 17.5 per cent of applications assessed. Both are true. A witness who gives one when the published record used the other has started an argument about which is right. The brief should give one, name its base, and match the base the agency has already published.

Four rules keep numbers safe:

- Write each number as the sentence the witness will say, with its definition in it: "177 applications were assessed", not "177".
- Use the same rounding as the published record. If the announcement said A$9.98 million, so does the brief.
- Give the as-at date for anything that changes, such as payments made or agreements signed.
- Have a second person check every number against its source, including any number changed late.

## The line officials do not cross

The usual practice at Estimates is that officials explain the government's policy and how the agency administers it. They do not argue for or against the policy, and they do not give personal opinions on it. Questions about the merits of a policy choice, or the reasons for a minister's decision, go to the minister at the table. The Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet publishes the *Government Guidelines for Official Witnesses before Parliamentary Committees and Related Matters*. Read the current version before you appear; this book does not summarise it.

The line runs both ways. It does not let a witness decline a factual question because the subject is political. The number of applications, the dates, the process and what the records show are facts the agency owns, and declining to give them only invites the next question.

This is an illustration. For the RCFP, the line is clear in principle. Officials can describe the process: the published guidelines, the eligibility check, the assessment, the fact that officials made recommendations, and the decisions and their date. The Minister made the decisions and recorded reasons for them, and those reasons are for the minister at the table to give. An official who guesses at a Minister's reasons crosses the line twice: into policy, and into facts the official does not know.

Some questions ask what officials advised, or what was said to the Minister's office. Section 13(6) of the [Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) says an APS employee must maintain "appropriate confidentiality" about dealings with a Minister or a Minister's staff. Whether advice is disclosed is a matter for the minister, and the Senate has its own orders on how a claim not to disclose information is to be made. Know where your agency's guidance on those orders is, and do not improvise a claim at the table. The sensitivities part of each brief should say, in plain words, where the line falls on that topic.

## Take it on notice rather than guess

Taking a question on notice means telling the committee you will answer it in writing later. It is a normal part of a hearing. The committee sets the date by which it wants the answer, and the agency drafts, checks and clears the answer like any other.

The Code of Conduct makes the choice simple. Section 13(9) says an APS employee "must not provide false or misleading information in response to a request for information that is made for official purposes". An honest "I don't have that figure with me, and I'll take it on notice" is neither false nor misleading. A confident guess may be both, and if it is wrong it has to be corrected.

Taking a question on notice is not a place to hide. Using it for a fact that is in the brief frustrates the committee and looks evasive, and every question taken on notice is work the agency must finish by the committee's date.

```figure
type: decision
title: Answer now or take it on notice
question: Can you answer from a current, sourced fact in front of you?
- Yes | Answer, and say what the number covers | "177 applications were assessed; 9 were ineligible"
- Partly | Give the part you can source, and take the rest on notice | say clearly which part is which
- No, or the brief may be out of date | Take it on notice | the committee sets the date for the answer
- * You realise an earlier answer was wrong | Correct it at the table if you can | if not, in writing as soon as possible (Chapter 10)
caption: This is an illustration. The rule is NRIO's own. Taking a question on notice is a normal part of a hearing; a confident guess is the risk.
```

When a question is taken on notice, someone must record exactly what was promised. A promise noted loosely is answered loosely. Record the Senator's name, the exact words of the question, the witness who took it and the time, and check the record against the transcript when it is published.

## On the day

This is an illustration. The CEO sat at the table with the Chief Finance Officer and the Director, Regional Programs. Sam Reid, who manages the RCFP, and the owners of the other likely topics sat behind them. Ravi ran the room: he kept the pack in order, passed notes forward and logged each question taken on notice. Lucy Tran watched the broadcast from the office and kept a second log, so that the two could be reconciled that evening. Clare watched for anything the Minister's office needed to know before the next morning.

By the end of the round the committee had asked NRIO 46 questions on notice, 19 of them about the RCFP. It set Friday 16 July 2027 as the date for answers; that date is fictional. Chapter 3 follows those questions.

## When it goes wrong: "every application was assessed"

This is an illustration. Late in the questions on the RCFP, a Senator asked whether the 2 projects the Minister approved against officials' recommendations had been assessed in the same way as the others. The CEO answered: "Every one of the 186 applications was assessed." That was wrong. All 186 had been checked against the eligibility criteria, 9 were ineligible, and those 9 were never assessed. Only 177 were.

The brief had the right numbers in the wrong place. Sam's team rewrote the RCFP brief on Tuesday 1 June, after the Minister announced the decisions, to cover the 31 grants. To fit the new material on the front page, the key facts shrank to one line: "186 applications received; 31 grants approved, A$9.98 million." The 9 ineligible and the 177 assessed were still there, but in the background on page three. Because the hearing was the next morning, the rewritten brief went to the CEO that evening without Ravi's number check. At the table, under pressure, the CEO joined "186" to "assessed".

Clare spotted the error that evening. The cost was a wrong statement on the public record about the fairness of a round that Senators were already testing. It had to be corrected in writing, and a corrected number invites a Senator to test every other number in the round. Chapter 10 follows the correcting letter, which went to the committee on Friday 4 June 2027.

Three controls would have caught it. First, the key facts must carry the distinctions, not only the headline: received, ineligible, assessed and approved, each with its number. Second, a late change gets the same second-person check as an early one; at NRIO, no reissued brief now goes forward until Ravi or Lucy has checked its numbers, however late it is. Third, the predictable follow-up gets a ready sentence: "All 186 applications were checked for eligibility. Nine were ineligible. The other 177, including the two approved against officials' recommendations, were assessed against the published criteria." With that sentence on the front page, the CEO would not have had to compose one at the table.

## Exercise: brief the two approvals

This is an illustration. Write the issue brief on the 2 RCFP grants approved against officials' recommendations, as it should have stood on the evening of Tuesday 1 June 2027. Use the parts above. You have these facts:

- 186 applications were received; 9 were ineligible and not assessed; 177 were assessed.
- On Monday 31 May 2027 the Minister approved 31 grants worth A$9.98 million.
- 2 of the 31 were approved against officials' recommendations: Corran Bay Progress Association Inc., A$140,000, in the Minister's own electorate; and Yarrowmere Showground Committee Inc., A$260,000.
- The Minister announced the decisions on Tuesday 1 June 2027. The program area says both decisions are being reported as the Commonwealth's grants rules require.

Include the numbers a Senator is likely to ask for, with the arithmetic, and mark the line the witness should not cross.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. One version of the brief follows.

**RCFP Round 1: grants approved against officials' recommendations.** Owner: Sam Reid, Regional Programs. Cleared by the Director, Regional Programs; numbers checked by Ravi Menon; both on Tuesday 1 June 2027.

**Key messages.**

- On 31 May 2027 the Minister approved 31 Round 1 grants worth A$9.98 million.
- Officials had recommended 29 of them. The Minister also approved 2 that officials had not recommended.
- All 186 applications were checked for eligibility. Nine were ineligible. The other 177, including those 2, were assessed against the published criteria.
- Both decisions are being reported as the Commonwealth's grants rules require.

**Key facts.**

| Fact | Number | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Applications received | 186 | Round 1 assessment report |
| Ineligible, not assessed | 9 | Eligibility record |
| Assessed against the criteria | 177 (186 − 9) | Round 1 assessment report |
| Grants approved | 31, A$9.98 million | Minister's decision record, 31 May 2027 |
| Recommended by officials and approved | 29 (31 − 2) | Decision record |
| Approved against officials' recommendations | 2, A$400,000 (A$140,000 + A$260,000) | Decision record |
| Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. | A$140,000; in the Minister's electorate | Decision record |
| Yarrowmere Showground Committee Inc. | A$260,000 | Decision record |
| The 2 as a share of the approved value | 4.0% (400,000 ÷ 9,980,000) | Calculated from the above |

**If asked.**

- Were the 2 assessed like the others? Yes. Both were among the 177 assessed against the published criteria.
- Why did the Minister approve them? The Minister recorded reasons for each decision. Those are for the minister at the table.
- What did officials advise? Officials did not recommend either for funding. Questions about the content of advice to the Minister go to the minister at the table.
- Is Corran Bay in the Minister's electorate? Yes.
- Have the decisions been reported? They are being reported as the grants rules require. The program area will confirm the status on the morning of the hearing.

**Said before.** Nothing yet. The decisions were announced on 1 June 2027.

**Sensitivities.** Do not give, guess at or comment on the Minister's reasons. Do not describe either decision as right or wrong. Do not say "all 186 were assessed". The ranked list and the advice to the Minister are not public.

This version holds up for four reasons. Every number has its definition and its source, and the sums are shown: 186 − 9 = 177; 31 − 2 = 29; A$140,000 + A$260,000 = A$400,000; and A$400,000 ÷ A$9,980,000 = 0.0401, or 4.0 per cent of the approved value. The question that caught the CEO is answered in the key messages, not on page three. The line not to cross is written down for the topic where it is most likely to be tested. And the brief records who checked the numbers and when, so a late change without a check would show.
