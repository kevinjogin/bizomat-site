A costed option is not yet a program. It becomes one when the government decides to fund it and the Parliament appropriates the money. Between those two points sits the Budget process, in which every proposal competes with every other for a limited amount of money and ministers' attention.

This is an illustration. NRIO lodged the bridge assessment proposal and its Cabinet material through the portfolio department on Fri 21 Jan 2028. In March 2028 it was considered by the Cabinet committee that considers Budget proposals. On Tue 9 May 2028, in the 2028–29 Budget, the government agreed a smaller version: 800 bridges rather than 1,200, and A$10.0 million over four years rather than A$14.5 million, with a review before any further tranche, or second stage of funding.

This chapter explains the law and practice behind that path, and what to do with a decision smaller than the request. Much of the process is set by government rules, not law, and is described here in general terms only.

## No money without an appropriation

The [Constitution](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004Q00685/latest/text) starts the story. Section 81 says that all revenue and money raised or received by the executive government "shall form one Consolidated Revenue Fund". Section 83 is the rule every Budget decision rests on: "No money shall be drawn from the Treasury of the Commonwealth except under appropriation made by law."

Three more sections shape those laws. A law appropriating money "shall not originate in the Senate" (s 53), so appropriation Bills begin in the House of Representatives. The Senate may not amend a law appropriating money for the ordinary annual services of the government, though it may ask the House to change it (s 53), and that law "shall deal only with such appropriation" (s 54). No appropriation may pass unless its purpose has been recommended by message of the Governor-General in the same session (s 56). Which Bill carries a program's money is for your CFO area and Finance, not the policy team.

Two practical points follow. First, a Budget decision is a decision of the government; an appropriation is a decision of the Parliament. Until the appropriation is in place the agency has no money for the new program, so ask your CFO area what may be done before then, and do not commit money the entity does not yet have. Each commitment will also need approval under the PGPA Act (Chapter 8 covers ministers' approvals). Second, ask your legal area early whether the program needs any legal authority beyond an appropriation.

The Constitution's text mentions neither the Cabinet nor the Prime Minister. Collective decision-making in Cabinet is convention, as Chapter 7 explains.

## The Charter of Budget Honesty sets the frame

The Charter is Schedule 1 to the [Charter of Budget Honesty Act 1998](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A05333/latest/text), numbered in clauses. Its purpose "is to improve fiscal policy outcomes" (Sch 1 cl 1). It says the government's fiscal policy "is therefore to be set in a sustainable medium-term framework" (cl 4(1)), and that its fiscal strategy is to be based on the principles of sound fiscal management in clause 5. Among them, the government is to "manage financial risks faced by the Commonwealth prudently" (cl 5(1)(a)) and to "ensure that its policy decisions have regard to their financial effects on future generations" (cl 5(1)(e)).

Notice the verb. The Charter says what the government "is to" do, and section 3(2) of the Act says it creates no rights or duties "that are enforceable in judicial or other proceedings". Its force comes from publication and scrutiny. It does not require a surplus; the government specifies its own fiscal objectives and targets (cl 9(1)(d)).

The Charter matters to a proposal in four ways:

- **The reports.** The Treasurer is to release and table a budget economic and fiscal outlook report "at the time of each budget" (cl 10), and a mid-year update "by the end of January in each year, or within 6 months after the last budget, whichever is later" (cl 14(1)). The update is to take into account, as far as possible, all government decisions with a material effect on the outlook (cl 16(2)), including those made since the Budget.
- **The four-year frame.** The budget report presents fiscal estimates for the budget year and the following three financial years (cl 12(1)(a)), which is why Chapter 5 costed Option C over four years.
- **Risks.** The budget report is to include a statement of risks, "quantified where feasible", that may materially affect the fiscal outlook (cl 12(1)(e)), including "publicly announced Government commitments that are not yet included in the fiscal estimates" (cl 12(1)(e)(ii)). A commitment announced before it is in the estimates is reported as a risk.
- **Future costs.** Clause 5(1)(e) is a reason to show costs that continue after the fourth year, such as Option C's register upkeep.

## The Budget cycle, in general terms

The Budget is annual. Most decisions on new proposals are made in the months before it; some are made between Budgets and reported in the mid-year update. The government sets the timetable each year, and the Department of Finance publishes the *Budget Process Operational Rules*. No Act cited in this book fixes the Budget date or the deadlines for proposals. Your Cabinet liaison area and portfolio department will tell you what applies, and when.

Ministers may agree a proposal, agree part of it, defer it or reject it. The decision is announced, the appropriations go to the Parliament, and the agency starts to deliver.

## What a proposal contains

A new policy proposal is the document that asks ministers to fund a new or changed policy. Its format is set by the central agencies and changes from time to time, so use the current template your Cabinet liaison area gives you. Whatever the format, a proposal that can be decided answers these questions.

- **The problem and the objective.** What is wrong, for whom, how big it is and what success would look like (Chapters 1 and 2).
- **The options.** What else was considered, including doing nothing, and why this one is recommended (Chapters 3 and 4).
- **The cost.** By year, split into program and running costs, with assumptions and risks (Chapter 5).
- **Delivery.** Who will deliver it, how, by when, and whether the capability exists (Chapter 9).
- **Performance and evaluation.** How success will be measured, and when it will be evaluated (Chapter 10).
- **Consultation.** Which other agencies have seen it and what they said, in the way your Cabinet liaison area directs (Chapter 7).
- **What a smaller version would do.** Ministers often ask, so answer before they do.

Whether the proposal also needs impact analysis under the *Australian Government Guide to Policy Impact Analysis* is a question to settle early with your Cabinet liaison area, not at lodgement.

## How Budget decisions are made

Ministers decide Budget proposals collectively, in the Cabinet or a committee of it. The responsible minister brings the proposal forward, the portfolio department coordinates it, and the central agencies, including Finance, the Treasury and the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, advise ministers on it. The agency that wrote it rarely sees the discussion, because Cabinet deliberations are confidential (Chapter 7).

Decision-makers weigh a proposal against the government's priorities and fiscal strategy, the evidence and the risks, and every other proposal competing for the same money. A dollar spent on bridges is a dollar not spent elsewhere, unless savings are found to pay for it. Whether savings are required, and how they are found, is set by the government's own rules for that Budget, not by law.

So write for a reader who is choosing, not for one who has already agreed. The proposal should make it easy to say yes to part of it.

## Why proposals come back scaled

A proposal can come back smaller for several reasons:

- **Money.** The total of good proposals is usually larger than the money available, so many are funded in part.
- **Evidence.** Decision-makers may want proof that a model works before they fund all of it. A first tranche tests the model.
- **Delivery risk.** A program that depends on scarce skills, such as bridge engineers, may be safer at a smaller scale.
- **Timing.** Money in later years can be decided later, when more is known.

A proposal designed in units scales cleanly. Option C was built from bridges, at a known rate, and its first two years did not depend on the third, so a decision at 800 bridges needed no redesign. A proposal whose parts only work together leaves decision-makers with all or nothing, and in a tight Budget that usually means nothing.

## The decision of 9 May 2028

This is an illustration. In the 2028–29 Budget on Tue 9 May 2028 the government agreed a smaller version of the proposal:

- **800 bridges** assessed over 2028–29 and 2029–30, 300 and 500;
- **A$10.0 million over four years**: administered A$7.60 million (2.85 and 4.75) and departmental A$2.40 million (1.25, 0.75, 0.30 and 0.10);
- a **review before any further tranche** of the remaining 400 bridges;
- funding from **savings within the portfolio**.

| A$ million | 2028–29 | 2029–30 | 2030–31 | 2031–32 | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Program (administered): assessments | 2.85 (300 bridges) | 4.75 (500) | 0 | 0 | 7.60 |
| Running (departmental) | 1.25 | 0.75 | 0.30 | 0.10 | 2.40 |
| **Total** | 4.10 | 5.50 | 0.30 | 0.10 | **10.00** |

The table adds both ways: 7.60 + 2.40 = 10.00, and 4.10 + 5.50 + 0.30 + 0.10 = 10.00. It matches the scaled costing in Chapter 5's exercise line for line.

Read a decision for exactly what it says. Funding from savings within the portfolio was the government's choice for this decision, not a general rule; other spending in the portfolio was reduced to pay for the bridges, and that is the government's to explain, not NRIO's. The review before any further tranche makes the mid-term review, due Thu 28 Feb 2030, the gate for the other 400 bridges (Chapter 10). Nothing in the decision promises them.

## Caretaker periods and election costings

This section is background, because the rules are often misstated.

The caretaker period for a general election starts "when the House of Representatives expires or is dissolved" (Charter Sch 1 cl 3). How agencies work during it is a matter of convention, described in the *Guidance on Caretaker Conventions* published by the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet. Read the current version when an election is due.

Charter costings exist only in the caretaker period, and only for publicly announced policies. The Prime Minister "may request the responsible Secretaries", the Secretaries of the Treasury and Finance, to cost publicly announced Government policies (cl 29(1)(a)). The Leader of the Opposition, and the leader of a recognised non-government party of at least five members (cl 3), may also ask, but their requests go to the Prime Minister, "who may then agree to refer it to the responsible Secretaries" (cl 29(4)). The Treasury Secretary costs the parts of a policy affecting tax revenue, and the Finance Secretary the parts affecting outlays, expenses and non-tax revenue (cl 30(2)). A request made before polling day, and its costing, are to be released publicly before that day (cl 31(2)).

The PBO, introduced in Chapter 5, offers a second route under the [Parliamentary Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00536/latest/text). Outside the caretaker period, any Senator or Member may ask it to cost "a policy or a proposed policy" (s 64H(2)), and the request may ask for confidentiality (s 64H(3)(d)). During the caretaker period, an authorised member of a parliamentary party may ask it to cost "a publicly announced policy of the Parliamentary party" (s 64J(2)), and an independent member may do the same for their own policy (s 64J(5)). A party cannot use both routes for the same policy: if a Charter request covers the same or a substantially similar policy, the PBO "must not prepare a costing" of it (s 64K(b)).

If your agency is asked for information for a costing, answer accurately, on time and through your agency's channels. Do not cost a party's policy or comment on it.

## When it goes wrong: the plan that assumed a yes

This is an illustration. In late 2026, another NRIO branch prepared a proposal for grants to councils to build rest areas for heavy vehicle drivers, for the 2027–28 Budget. The Minister's office was supportive, and the branch began to treat the proposal as approved. Its business plan for 2027–28 counted the program as funded. In March 2027 it engaged two contractors to draft grant guidelines and a council engagement plan. Its draft performance measures for the next corporate plan included rest areas funded. Some councils were told informally to start preparing sites.

The Budget did not fund the proposal. The contractors had cost A$0.18 million from the branch's departmental budget, money planned for other work. The corporate plan's draft measures had to be withdrawn weeks before publication. Councils had spent staff time on sites that would not be funded, and the branch had to explain to the portfolio department how councils had heard of a proposal before ministers considered it.

Support from a minister's office is not a decision, and neither is a lodged proposal. The control is a habit, not a form:

- plan for three outcomes, agreed, scaled and not funded, and know what each would mean for staff, contracts and stakeholders;
- limit work before the decision to what is cheap and useful whatever the outcome;
- commit no program money, and make no promises, until the decision is announced and the money is appropriated;
- keep the proposal confidential, as Chapter 7 explains.

## Exercise: explain the decision to the team

This is an illustration. It is Wed 10 May 2028, the morning after the Budget. Grace has fifteen minutes with Tom, Amara and Zoe. Prepare her explanation:

1. what the government agreed, in its exact terms;
2. how the decision differs from the proposal, by year and in total;
3. what stays the same for the team;
4. what the team must do differently, including anything the decision does not fund.

Use the decision above and the costing in Chapter 5.

### Worked answer

**What was agreed.** Read the terms out as written: 800 bridges (300 and 500); A$10.0 million over four years, A$7.60 million administered and A$2.40 million departmental; a review before any further tranche of the remaining 400; savings within the portfolio.

**How it differs.** Year by year, against the proposal's 4.10, 5.50, 4.50 and 0.40:

| A$ million | 2028–29 | 2029–30 | 2030–31 | 2031–32 | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Proposed | 4.10 | 5.50 | 4.50 | 0.40 | 14.50 |
| Agreed | 4.10 | 5.50 | 0.30 | 0.10 | 10.00 |
| Difference | 0 | 0 | −4.20 | −0.30 | −4.50 |

The difference adds up: 4.20 + 0.30 = 4.50. Of the A$4.50 million, A$3.80 million is program money for the 400 bridges not yet funded (400 × A$9,500), and A$0.70 million is running money (3.10 − 2.40). The team has 400 ÷ 1,200 = 33.3 per cent fewer bridges and 4.50 ÷ 14.50 = 31.0 per cent less money.

**What stays the same.** The first two years are exactly as proposed. The panel procurement from July to September 2028, the data standard, the register build and the first assessments on Mon 2 Oct 2028 go ahead as planned. Grace says this first.

**What changes.**

- *Councils' nominations.* There are 800 places, not 1,200, so the nomination process must rank priorities across fewer places. Amara drafts the guidance with that in mind.
- *The panel.* Buy it for 800 assessments. Ask the procurement area how the arrangement could allow for a later tranche without promising one.
- *Staff in 2030–31.* The running money falls from A$0.70 million to A$0.30 million: a close-out team of A$0.20 million and register upkeep of A$0.10 million. Any role planned for 2030–31 must be looked at again now, not in 2030.
- *The review.* The mid-term review report, due Thu 28 Feb 2030, is now the gate for the other 400 bridges. It must answer what ministers will ask: bridges assessed against the target, the share of precautionary limits lifted or eased, unsafe bridges found and acted on, and cost per assessment (Chapter 10).
- *Evaluation.* The decision includes no money for the A$0.25 million final evaluation. The A$0.10 million in 2031–32 is register upkeep. Grace raises the gap with the Director, Strategy and Policy this week, with two choices: fund it within NRIO's departmental budget, or seek it with any further tranche.
- *Estimates.* Zoe updates NRIO's estimates to the agreed numbers.
- *Talking about it.* The decision is public; the deliberations are not. Explain what was agreed, do not guess at why it was scaled, and leave questions about other programs to the portfolio department.

**In one sentence.** "We have two years and A$10.0 million to prove the model on 800 bridges. The February 2030 review decides the rest."
