## About this book

Every fabrication business depends on welders, and most find them hard to hire. The usual answer is to train them: take on apprentices, retrain people from other trades, or move existing welders onto new processes and positions. Training is expensive in time, materials and supervision, and its results are uncertain, so it is often done informally, by putting a new person beside an experienced one and hoping. This book is about doing it deliberately, with numbers.

It is for fabrication managers, welding supervisors, trainers and training coordinators. It assumes basic algebra and a working knowledge of welding. It explains how welding skill grows with practice, how to describe that growth with a learning curve, what an hour of practice costs, how to assess trainees and estimate their chances in a qualification test, how skill fades without use, and how to work out whether a training programme pays for itself.

## Early manuals as a starting point

In 1917 and 1918, the United States Army needed thousands of welders for the repair of vehicles, guns and equipment and found almost none available. It set up its own welding schools, and in 1919 the officer in charge of welding instruction for the Ordnance Department, Lorn Campbell Jr., published an *Oxy-Acetylene Welding Manual* written for the instructor as well as the student. He complained that the many new welding classes each taught in a different way, argued for a standard method, and insisted that welding could not be learned by watching but only by practice at the torch. In the same year, the Davis-Bournonville company published the lecture course of its welding institute, which set out the theory a welder needed and stressed that the course must develop manual skill until the welder held the torch correctly without thinking about it.

Their problems were the same as a modern fabricator's: too few welders, too little time, and training that varied with whoever gave it. Each chapter takes up one of their themes and puts numbers on it.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A structural steel fabricator with twenty-two staff makes portal frames, platforms and stairs, welding mainly with flux-cored and solid-wire processes in the shop and manual metal arc on site. It cannot hire enough qualified welders, so it has taken on four trainees: two apprentices, a boilermaker's labourer moving up, and a career changer from the automotive trade. The workshop manager wants to plan their training, cost it, measure their progress and know when each will be ready to weld production work.

The fabricator, its people, costs and results are invented for teaching. They are not KEVOS® work. Real training must meet the applicable qualification standards, apprenticeship arrangements and work health and safety requirements.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 sets out why training pays and what it must achieve. Chapters 2 and 3 cover how skill is learned and the learning curve. Chapter 4 plans a course, and Chapter 5 costs practice. Chapter 6 deals with assessment and feedback, and Chapter 7 with qualification tests and their odds. Chapter 8 covers skill decay and refreshers, Chapter 9 safety training, and Chapter 10 the economics and the training record. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Welder qualification for structural, pressure and other regulated work must follow the applicable standards and specifications, such as AS/NZS 2980 and ISO 9606-1 for the qualification of welders of steels, and be witnessed and recorded as they require. Apprenticeships and nationally recognised qualifications follow their own training packages and rules. Training must comply with the work health and safety regulator's requirements, including Safe Work Australia's model code of practice for welding processes as adopted in each jurisdiction. The learning curves and costs in this book are illustrations; each business must measure its own.
