Once a customer problem is credible, the next question is how suitable customers will encounter your offer. A channel is a route through which that happens: a referral, a search result, a professional event or an existing customer conversation. Choosing channels is a resource decision. Every route consumes time, attention or money before it produces work.

Fieldnote Studio has two people and existing delivery commitments. It cannot maintain a daily video programme, attend every networking event and answer enquiries immediately. Its useful starting question is therefore not which channel could reach the most people. It is which route can produce relevant conversations at a cost the team can sustain.

## Match the route to a buying situation

Describe where a customer is likely to notice the problem. A firm updating its services may already be speaking to its web designer. A manager dealing with repeated misunderstandings may ask a trusted peer. Those situations suggest different routes: a relationship with complementary providers, a practical explanation customers can share, or a page that answers a specific question.

Write the reasoning before choosing the channel. “Our customers use social media” is too broad. “Owners reviewing their service pages ask their web designers about wording, so we will test introductions from two designers” identifies an event, an intermediary and a bounded experiment. It can still be wrong, but the team knows what it is testing.

A referral relationship should clarify what each business does, how introductions happen and whether any payment or other benefit is involved. Do not leave customers guessing about commercial relationships. Obtain permission before passing on contact details and let the customer decide whether to engage.

## Give the enquiry somewhere useful to land

A small service page can answer the questions required for an initial decision: who the service is for, the situation it addresses, what is included, what input the customer must provide and what happens after an enquiry. If price depends on scope, explain the variables rather than creating a false impression of a fixed fee.

Fieldnote describes a bounded document package with an agreed number of outputs and review rounds. It states that service rules must be approved by the customer. This limitation helps a prospect recognise whether they are ready; it also prevents the page from promising to resolve management disagreements through writing alone.

Make the next action proportionate. An initial enquiry does not need every company document. Ask for the business context, the immediate problem and a way to respond. Explain what information is useful and discourage the submission of sensitive customer records. Test the page on a phone and check that the enquiry route works. A polished description cannot compensate for a broken form.

## Count progress towards work

Channel activity and commercial progress are different measures. Publishing four posts records effort. Receiving six enquiries records a response. Confirming that two enquiries fit the offer records something closer to a sales opportunity. Keep those measures separate.

For a two-week experiment, Fieldnote records the following fictional results:

| Route | Team time | Enquiries | Enquiries that fit the service | Agreed next conversations |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Introductions through complementary providers | 5 hours | 4 | 3 | 2 |
| A practical guide shared with an existing audience | 7 hours | 6 | 2 | 1 |

The guide produced more enquiries, while introductions produced more suitable conversations during this short period. Neither result proves a permanent winner. The guide may keep working after publication; introductions may depend on one unusually helpful contact. Two weeks and ten enquiries are too little evidence for a dependable conversion forecast.

Record enough context to interpret the numbers: the time window, the audience, the definition of fit and whether opportunities have had time to progress. Ask customers how they heard about you without assuming they used only one route. Someone may read a guide after a referral and later search for the business name. A simple record can retain both influences rather than assigning false precision to one source.

## Include the cost of your attention

At an illustrative planning value of A$85 per hour, five hours represents A$425 of team time and seven hours represents A$595. These are internal economic estimates, not necessarily cash payments. They make an invisible cost visible.

Do not yet divide that cost by sales if the enquiries are still open. Later, compare a mature group of enquiries with the contribution earned from its completed work. Include relevant direct spending and the time spent preparing material, responding and following up. A channel with cheap clicks can be expensive if most enquiries require lengthy explanations before being declined.

Also check the effect on delivery. If a successful campaign creates more suitable work than the team can handle, the response is not automatically to accept everything. Adjust availability, scheduling or the offer. The capacity test in Chapter 5 applies regardless of how attractively the enquiry arrived.

## Respect permission when making contact

For Australian commercial electronic messages, consult the ACMA's [guidance on avoiding spam](https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam). Consent, identification of the sender and a working unsubscribe mechanism are central requirements. A publicly visible address is not blanket permission to send marketing. Keep evidence of consent and implement withdrawal requests through the actual sending process. This short note is not a complete compliance assessment; check the rules for the message, recipient and jurisdiction involved.

Operationally, separate a requested response to an enquiry from permission for an ongoing marketing list. Do not silently turn one into the other. A spreadsheet of contacts is still a record of people whose expectations matter.

## Exercise: define a channel experiment

Choose one route you can maintain for a defined period. Write the customer situation, the material or conversation you will offer, the time and spending limit, and the signal that would justify continuing. Include a stopping condition, such as repeatedly reaching the wrong type of customer.

End the experiment with a decision: continue, change the message, change the audience or stop. Preserve what you learned even when the channel fails. The next chapter follows a suitable enquiry into a conversation that produces a clear decision record.
