Skills and results matter most in business, but how people conduct themselves strongly influences whether others trust them, want to work with them and remember them positively. A capable engineer who interrupts constantly, an excellent salesperson who sends careless emails, or a talented manager who checks their phone throughout meetings all undermine their own effectiveness. Good manners and professional presence are not about rigid rules or formality. They are about respect for others, and they create a good impression at very low cost.

This article covers practical etiquette in the situations business people face most often: dealing with customers, phone calls, email, shared workspaces, meetings, business meals, interviews and personal appearance. Conventions vary between countries, industries and organisations, so treat these as general guidance and adapt to your context.

## Customer etiquette

Customers are the lifeblood of any business, and patience is the most important quality in dealing with them. Good practice includes:

- **Do not take comments or insults personally.** Frustrated customers are usually reacting to a situation, not to you.
- **Do not interrupt.** Let customers explain fully before responding.
- **Stay focused** on understanding and solving their problem.
- **Show empathy**: acknowledge their frustration and their situation.
- **Take responsibility for mistakes** rather than making excuses.
- **Use courteous language consistently**: "please", "thank you", "you're welcome" and "I'm sorry for the inconvenience".

## Phone etiquette

- **Introduce yourself** with your name and business when calling, then ask how you can help, or state the purpose of your call.
- **Do not eat while on the phone.** The other person can hear it.
- **Take calls in appropriate places.** Avoid noisy or private spaces such as bathrooms.
- **Avoid speakerphone** in shared spaces. It reduces privacy for you and the caller, and can disturb others.
- **Return missed calls promptly.** If the person does not answer, leave a clear message rather than calling repeatedly.
- **Use an earpiece or headset** for long calls in shared workplaces, and keep your voice down.

## Email etiquette

Email is often the first impression a customer or colleague receives. Good emails are:

- **Clearly titled**: a short, specific subject line that tells the reader what the email is about.
- **Properly addressed**: use the recipient's name and the level of formality that suits the relationship and culture.
- **Polite**: stay courteous even when you are frustrated, for example when complaining about a defective product.
- **Concise**: two or three short paragraphs are usually enough. Long emails are skimmed or ignored.
- **Clear**: use simple language and state what you need and by when.
- **Error-free**: proofread for spelling, grammar and missing attachments. Errors look careless.
- **Properly closed**: end with an appropriate sign-off and your contact details.

Read every important email once more before sending, and pause before replying to anything that makes you angry.

## Shared workspace etiquette

- **Respect privacy**: knock or ask before entering someone's office or workspace.
- **Do not interrupt** people who are concentrating unless it is urgent.
- **Do not look over people's shoulders** at their screens or papers.
- **Keep your voice down** in open-plan areas.
- **Keep shared spaces tidy**, including kitchens, meeting rooms and equipment.

## Meeting etiquette

Meetings are where people observe each other most closely. Good practice:

1. **Be on time.** Lateness signals disrespect for others' time, however senior you are. If you are unavoidably late, enter quietly, apologise briefly and focus on the meeting.
2. **Be appropriately presented**, following the dress expectations for the meeting, whether formal or casual.
3. **Silence your phone** and avoid checking messages or social media.
4. **Come prepared**: read the agenda and papers, and bring what you need.
5. **Do not interrupt.** Wait for the speaker to finish, then make your point politely. A meeting is a discussion, not an argument.
6. **Stay on topic.** Respect the agenda and the time available.

## Business meal etiquette

Business meals with customers, partners or colleagues are about the relationship as much as the food.

- **Put your phone away.** Constantly checking phones at the table, sometimes called "phubbing", is disrespectful.
- **Follow the host's lead**: wait for the host to sit and to begin eating, or to signal that you should start.
- **Place your napkin on your lap** when you sit down.
- **Sit upright** and engage in conversation.
- **Remember the layout**: in Western place settings, your bread plate is on the left and your drinks are on the right. A simple trick is to make a lowercase "b" with your left hand and a "d" with your right.
- **Ask for dishes to be passed** rather than reaching across others.
- **Do not talk with your mouth full.**
- **Be gracious about food**: thank your host and compliment the meal. If you cannot eat something for dietary, religious or health reasons, decline politely without lengthy explanation.
- **Follow local customs** for eating with hands or utensils. In formal restaurants in Australia, utensils are usual.
- **Excuse yourself simply** if you need to leave the table.
- **Signal that you have finished** by placing your knife and fork together on the plate.

Wash your hands before and after eating, and be mindful of alcohol at business functions.

## Lift and shared space courtesy

Professional behaviour does not stop at the office door. In lifts, corridors and car parks:

- Let people exit before you enter.
- Hold the door for others, and help people who need assistance.
- Press only the button you need.
- Give people space, and avoid loud conversations, especially confidential ones.
- If you are blocking someone's exit, step out briefly to let them pass.

## Interview etiquette

Whether you are the candidate or the interviewer, courtesy matters:

**For candidates**:

- Arrive early, about ten to fifteen minutes, and allow enough time for the interview without rushing.
- Know your resume and the role thoroughly.
- Dress appropriately for the organisation and role.
- Be well groomed.
- Greet everyone politely, including reception staff, and thank the interviewers when you leave.

**For interviewers**:

- Start on time, welcome the candidate, offer water and explain the process.
- Give the candidate your full attention.
- Follow up promptly with an outcome. Candidates remember how they were treated, and they talk to others.

## Dressing for the occasion

Appearance is the first thing others see, and it shapes first impressions before you say a word. Dressing well is not about expensive brands or the latest trends. It is about wearing clothes that suit you, the occasion and the setting.

### Consider your body shape and comfort

Choose clothes that fit well and suit your build. Clothes that are too tight, too short or uncomfortable make you self-conscious, and that shows in your behaviour. Comfortable, well-fitting clothes support confident behaviour.

### Consider colour

Some colours suit people better than others. Notice which colours make you look and feel your best.

### Consider the nature of your work

Follow your organisation's dress code and the norms of your industry. A client-facing role in a corporate office, a site role in construction or manufacturing, and a creative role in a design studio each have different expectations. On industrial sites, safety requirements such as protective clothing and footwear come first.

### Consider climate

Dress for the weather. In hot climates, there are ways to look professional without heavy suits, such as lighter fabrics and colours. In cooler weather, warmer fabrics and darker colours are practical.

### Consider the occasion

- **Interviews**: dress at or slightly above the standard for the role and organisation.
- **Client meetings**: match or slightly exceed the client's typical level of formality.
- **Business dinners and functions**: follow the stated dress code.
- **Cultural and religious occasions**: respect the customs and traditions of the event.

Before leaving, check yourself in the mirror and ask whether the outfit suits you and the occasion. Being noticeably overdressed or underdressed can be uncomfortable, and it is easy to adjust.

### The basics of grooming

Whatever you wear, the basics matter more than style:

- Clean, pressed clothes.
- Good fit.
- Clean, polished shoes.
- Neat, tidy hair.

## Image and self-perception

How you look affects not only how others see you, but how you think and feel about yourself. People who are dissatisfied with their appearance may feel self-conscious, distracted or less confident. People who feel appropriately and comfortably dressed tend to feel more secure and perform better. Image management means periodically reviewing your appearance and its effect on yourself and others, and making adjustments that help you feel and appear professional.

At the same time, appearance is not everything. A well-dressed person without integrity, kindness or competence will not earn lasting respect, and people should never be judged on looks alone. Everyone is unique, and character, capability and how we treat others matter most. Appearance simply helps ensure that nothing distracts from those qualities.

## Video call etiquette

Video calls have their own etiquette:

- **Join on time**, with audio and video tested.
- **Use a tidy, appropriate background**, or a simple blurred background.
- **Mute when not speaking** in larger meetings to avoid background noise.
- **Look at the camera** when speaking, and give visible attention when listening.
- **Avoid multitasking.** People can tell when you are reading emails.
- **Use the chat appropriately**, for relevant links and questions, not side conversations.
- **Dress as you would for an in-person meeting** with the same people, at least from the waist up.

## Cultural awareness

Australia is a multicultural society, and many businesses work with international customers and suppliers. Etiquette varies between cultures in greetings, use of titles and first names, punctuality, gift-giving, dining customs, physical contact and directness in communication. Before meeting people from another culture, learn about their customs, and when in doubt, follow their lead and ask politely. Respect for difference is itself a sign of professionalism.

## Inclusive courtesy

Modern professional etiquette is inclusive. Rather than following old conventions based on gender or age alone, offer courtesy to everyone: hold doors, offer seats or assistance to people who need them, use people's preferred names and pronouns, and avoid assumptions about roles. Treat everyone, from the receptionist to the chief executive, with the same respect.

## Messaging and social media etiquette

Instant messaging and social media blur the line between personal and professional communication:

- **Respect working hours** when messaging colleagues and customers, and avoid expecting instant replies outside them.
- **Keep work messages professional**: clear, polite and free of slang that could be misunderstood.
- **Use group chats carefully**: avoid side conversations, excessive emojis and replying to everyone when only one person needs to know.
- **Think before posting publicly.** Comments on social media about customers, colleagues or employers can damage reputations and relationships, and may breach workplace policies.
- **Keep confidential information confidential.** Never share customer details, pricing or internal matters in public forums.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is etiquette old-fashioned?** The specific customs evolve, but the underlying principle, respect for others, never goes out of date. Modern workplaces still notice punctuality, courtesy, attention and care.

**What if my workplace is very casual?** Match the culture, but keep the basics: be on time, listen, respond promptly and treat everyone respectfully. Casual does not mean careless.

**How do I handle someone else's poor etiquette?** Model good behaviour, and if it affects work, raise it privately and constructively. Many people are simply unaware of how their habits come across, and most appreciate a discreet word more than continued embarrassment.

## A worked example

A small engineering consultancy notices that it often impresses prospective clients technically but loses some jobs to competitors. Client feedback reveals small but telling issues: late arrivals to meetings, emails with errors and missing attachments, and staff checking phones during presentations.

The consultancy introduces simple standards: arrive five minutes early, phones away in client meetings, every client email proofread and checked for attachments, and a brief dress guide for client meetings and site visits. Senior staff model the standards. Within months, clients comment on the team's professionalism, and the firm's proposal win rate improves.

## Summary

Professional etiquette is respect made visible. Be patient and empathetic with customers. Use courteous, clear phone and email habits, respect shared spaces and arrive at meetings on time, prepared and attentive. At business meals, follow the host's lead and focus on the relationship. Treat interview candidates and interviewers with courtesy. Dress to suit yourself, your work, the climate and the occasion, with grooming that is clean and neat. Good manners and presence cost little, build trust and make sure your skills and character get the attention they deserve.
