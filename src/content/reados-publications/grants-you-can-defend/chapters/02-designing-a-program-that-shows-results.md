In 2029–30 someone will ask whether the RCFP worked: the Minister deciding whether it should continue, an auditor, or a council that missed out. What the evaluation can say then depends on decisions made in 2026, before a single application arrives. A program that never recorded where it started cannot show how far it moved.

This is an illustration. In August 2026 Sam has a policy objective, A$20.0 million of administered funding for grants, A$1.2 million of departmental funding to run the program, and a deadline. Ivy starts drafting the guidelines in October, so the design must be settled by the end of September. Everything the guidelines say has to trace back to it.

The CGRPs cover this ground in two key principles: robust planning and design (section 7) and an outcomes orientation (section 10). Both sit in Part 2, so most of what they ask is better practice. Paragraph 7.5 still gives the task a clear shape: officials should "establish a rationale for grant opportunities, particularly what outcomes are expected and how these will be measured".

## Start from a clear objective

Paragraph 10.2 of the [CGRPs](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) says officials should determine what change a grant is expected to bring, and then measure the actual outcome. The operational objective, a statement of what the grant is intended to achieve, should be concise, unambiguous and realistic. Paragraph 10.3 adds that objectives should link to the outcomes set by government, include quantitative, qualitative and milestone information, be endorsed at the right level and be shared with applicants.

This is an illustration. The RCFP's policy objective is to help small towns in NRIO's region keep community facilities that serve them. That is a purpose, not yet something anyone can measure. Sam's operational objective reads: "By 2029–30, community facilities funded by the RCFP in towns of fewer than 15,000 people are safe, accessible and used by more local groups for more hours than before their upgrade."

Each phrase does a job. "Funded by the RCFP" limits the claim to what the program can influence. "Safe, accessible and used" names three things that can be observed. "Than before their upgrade" commits the program to a baseline. The Director endorses the objective, and it goes into the guidelines.

A common alternative, "to support regional communities through investment in community infrastructure", cannot fail. That is the problem: no result could show it was not met.

## Build the program logic

A program logic is a chain from what a program puts in to the change it expects, with each link written down so it can be tested. Five levels suit most grant programs:

- **Inputs:** money, people and other resources.
- **Activities:** what the agency and the grantees do with them.
- **Outputs:** the direct products, counted when the work is done.
- **Short-term outcomes:** changes that follow within about a year.
- **Medium-term outcomes:** the changes the program exists for, two to three years on.

The CGRPs draw the same distinctions for measures (10.4). Outcome measures assess changes in the community, and the examples include "the level of usage of facilities built with the grant". Output measures count things such as completed projects, and input measures things such as the cost of administering a grant. Figure 1 follows one thread of the RCFP logic.

```figure
type: steps
title: One thread of the RCFP program logic
caption: This is an illustration. Each step rests on an assumption. New toilets lead to more use only if their absence was keeping people away, so the application asks for evidence of that.
- Inputs | grant money, any council contribution, volunteers' time and NRIO's grants team
- Activities | a hall committee contracts a builder to replace a leaking roof and build accessible toilets
- Outputs | the works are finished, inspected and paid for within 18 months of the agreement
- Short-term outcomes | the hall is no longer closed by leaks, and people who could not use it before now book it
- *Medium-term outcomes | more local groups meet in the hall regularly, two to three years after the works
```

The links are where programs fail. Paragraph 10.5 lists common mistakes, starting with "assuming that a grant being awarded automatically secures the desired outcome". A new roof is an output. It becomes an outcome only if leaks were closing the hall and the committee can keep it open. Write each assumption beside its link, with the evidence that would show it holds.

The logic also needs a counterfactual: what would happen without the grant. Paragraph 12.2 calls it a fundamental appraisal criterion that a grant should add value "by achieving something worthwhile that would not occur without the grant".

## Eligibility that follows the objective

Paragraph 7.5 says officials should ensure that eligibility criteria reflect the operational objectives and policy intent. Every rule should exclude something on purpose, and the design file should say why.

This is an illustration.

| Eligibility rule | Why it is there |
| --- | --- |
| An incorporated not-for-profit organisation or a local council in NRIO's region | A legal entity that can sign an agreement and be held to it |
| A community facility in a town of fewer than 15,000 people at the most recent Census | The policy is about small towns, where one hall may be the only place to meet |
| A grant of A$50,000 to A$500,000, excluding GST | Below A$50,000 a competitive application costs more effort than the grant justifies; the cap spreads each round across many towns |
| Work finished within 18 months of the grant agreement | Results can be seen, and final reports received by 31 March 2029, inside the funding period |

The cap is easy to check: A$10.0 million ÷ A$500,000 = 20, so a round funds at least 20 facilities.

Eligibility is a gate, not a measure of quality. Whether a project is needed, deliverable and good value belongs to the weighted assessment criteria (Chapter 3).

## Co-contribution: who else pays

This is an illustration. In the RCFP, councils must contribute at least 25 per cent of eligible project costs; incorporated not-for-profit organisations need not contribute. The cost-shifting warning in 12.4 supports a council contribution. Officials should be alert to "cost shifting", where the Commonwealth funds an activity that a state, territory or local government should pay for. A council owns many of its facilities and raises its own revenue, so a contribution shows the project is a council priority, not a cost moved to the Commonwealth.

A volunteer hall committee has no rates base and often small reserves. Requiring a contribution would screen out the smallest towns, the ones the program exists to serve. Paragraph 9.2 makes the general point: administration should reflect the capabilities of potential grantees. Paragraph 12.4 also warns of "double-dipping", funding for the same activity from more than one source, so every applicant lists other funding for the work.

For a council, the two limits interact: the largest grant is the lesser of 75 per cent of the eligible cost and A$500,000 (Figure 2).

## Proportionality and risk

Proportionality, in paragraph 9.1, means balancing complexity, risks, outcomes and transparency. Paragraph 9.4 says officials should use it to inform the choice of selection process, grant agreement and reporting and acquittal requirements. Paragraph 9.5 corrects a common belief: "There are no mandatory acquittal or reporting requirements for grantees in the CGRPs." Reporting is a design choice, matched to the risks and the outcomes sought.

The CGRPs sort risks into three groups (7.9): the grant opportunity itself, the grantee, and the funded activity. Each risk should be assigned to the party best placed to manage it (7.14).

This is an illustration.

| Risk group | RCFP example | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Grant opportunity | Far more demand than A$10.0 million can meet, and a grants team of four | Clear eligibility, so groups that cannot succeed do not apply; an assessment plan sized to the volume (Chapter 5) |
| Grantee | Volunteer committees managing a building contract for the first time | A capacity-to-deliver criterion worth 30 per cent; payments in three stages |
| Grant activity | Rising building costs, few builders in small towns, an 18-month limit | A budget and risk plan in every application; the final 20 per cent held until acquittal |

The payment schedule is the main proportionality tool, and it is NRIO's choice, not a Commonwealth rule. NRIO pays 40 per cent when the agreement is executed, so work can start; 40 per cent on an approved mid-point progress report; and 20 per cent on an approved final report and acquittal. Holding back the last 20 per cent keeps a reason to report after the works finish, which is when outcome data starts to exist.

This work feeds the guidelines: footnote 36 says officials developing guidelines are required to complete a risk assessment, and Finance, in consultation with the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, agrees the final risk rating. Proportionality decisions should also be recorded and periodically reviewed (9.7).

## Profile the money across four years

This is an illustration. The RCFP's A$20.0 million is administered funding, available by financial year. A profile says how much will be paid in each year, and it should follow the payment schedule. Dividing by four, A$5.0 million a year, is almost always wrong. Sam's design-stage profile rests on three written assumptions:

- Round 1 agreements are executed from 15 June 2027, and about a quarter of the round by value is executed and paid before 30 June. Its first instalments, 0.40 × A$10.0 million = A$4.0 million, fall A$1.0 million in 2026–27 and A$3.0 million in 2027–28.
- Mid-point reports come about nine months into the projects, in early 2028, so the second A$4.0 million falls in 2027–28. The final 0.20 × A$10.0 million = A$2.0 million follows final reports due by 31 March 2029, in 2028–29.
- Round 2 runs a year behind Round 1, with the same split.

The funding ends with 2029–30, which forces the third assumption. Round 2 agreements executed by mid-June 2028 mean projects finished within 18 months, by mid-December 2029, with time for final reports and payments before 30 June 2030. A later round would not fit.

The total checks: A$1.0 million + A$8.0 million + A$9.0 million + A$2.0 million = A$20.0 million. Against an even split, the plan is A$4.0 million lower in 2026–27, A$3.0 million higher in 2027–28, A$4.0 million higher in 2028–29 and A$3.0 million lower in 2029–30. Each difference would otherwise appear at year end as an unplanned underspend or overspend. Delays move money later, so Ivy updates the forecast from the agreements and reports, and tells the CFO area early (Chapter 7).

The departmental A$1.2 million is A$1.2 million ÷ A$20.0 million = 6 per cent of the grant funding. Sam asks for part of it to be reserved for evaluation.

## Plan the evaluation and the baseline now

Evaluation is part of the grants lifecycle (2.8(e)). Paragraph 10.8 says officials should establish performance measures and specify them in the guidelines and agreements, and "should undertake an evaluation of a grant opportunity before initiating further grant opportunities or extending existing grant agreements". Paragraph 7.6 says programs should be "designed to enable closure on successful completion".

A baseline is the value of a measure before the grant changes anything, so it must be collected before the work starts.

This is an illustration. For the RCFP the cheapest moment is the application, when applicants are already gathering evidence of need. Three points of better practice shape what Sam asks for:

- **Use records grantees already keep.** Paragraph 9.5 says officials should not ask for information available from other sources, and should consider aligning reporting with grantees' own reporting. Most halls keep a booking diary, even a paper one.
- **Check the grantee can produce the data.** Paragraph 10.5 warns against indicators "reliant on data provided by the grantee" without checking the grantee's capacity to provide it. Applicants say how they record use; those with no records count use for four weeks in February 2027, while the round is open.
- **Measure outcomes as well as outputs.** Paragraph 9.6 warns that "poorly formulated reporting requirements, which focus on outputs rather than outcomes, can be overly burdensome".

Round 2 opens in 2028, before Round 1's projects finish, so Sam plans two pieces of work. A short review of Round 1's process runs in August and September 2027, after its agreements are executed and before Round 2's guidelines are drafted. It informs Round 2 and is NRIO's recorded answer to 10.8. The full evaluation in 2029–30 tests Round 1's outcomes and informs whether the program continues, and on what settings.

## When it goes wrong: halls painted, use unknown

This is an illustration. In 2023–24 the Regional Programs Branch ran a smaller, one-round program for hall refurbishment. It paid 48 grants of up to A$30,000 each, A$1.15 million in total, for painting, floors, curtains and furniture. It measured halls refurbished, projects on time and acquittals received. Final reports carried photos, invoices and a "project complete" tick.

By those measures it succeeded: 48 halls refurbished, 44 of them on time (44 ÷ 48 = 91.7 per cent), and every grant acquitted. In late 2025 the branch tried to use it as evidence for the RCFP and could not. Nobody had recorded how the halls were used before the works, or asked afterwards. When Ivy rang 12 of the hall committees in early 2026, only 3 had booking records from before the works.

Worse, two of the halls closed within a year because their roofs leaked onto the new floors. Together they had received A$54,000. Nothing in the design asked whether a hall's condition would let it stay open.

```figure

title: What the earlier program's reports showed, and what they could not

rowhead: Question
caption: This is an illustration. Every number in the first column was accurate. None of them answered the question the branch needed answered.
- Was the work done | 48 halls refurbished, 44 of them on time | whether the works fixed what kept people away
- Was the money spent as agreed | A$1.15 million acquitted against invoices | what changed for the money
- Are the halls used more | photos of finished halls | any change in bookings, because almost nobody recorded them before the works
- Are the halls in good condition | a "project complete" tick on every report | that two roofs were leaking above the new floors
```

The cost was A$1.15 million with no evidence of what changed, A$54,000 in two halls that then closed, and a new program whose case had to rest on other evidence.

The control is the design in this chapter: an objective that names a change, a logic that tests the link from works to use, a baseline collected with the application, and outcome measures written into the guidelines and agreements. It is also why the RCFP's community need and benefit criterion, weighted 40 per cent, asks about each facility's condition and current use.

## Exercise: the RCFP program logic and three measures

This is an illustration. It is September 2026. The Director asks Sam for one page that settles the design before Ivy starts the guidelines: the RCFP program logic at five levels, and three outcome measures. For each measure, give a definition, the data source, how and when the baseline is collected, when the result is measured and how it is calculated.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

| Level | RCFP program logic |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | A$20.0 million administered over four years, A$10.0 million a round; A$1.2 million departmental; council contributions of at least 25 per cent; volunteers' time |
| Activities | NRIO runs two open competitive rounds, then agrees, pays and monitors grants; grantees plan, contract and manage the works |
| Outputs | Upgrades finished within 18 months and within budget: roofs, accessible toilets, change rooms, kitchens, library extensions |
| Short-term outcomes, within 12 months of completion | Facilities open and safe; fewer days lost to their condition; more hours of use; new groups using them |
| Medium-term outcomes, two to three years on | More local groups meet regularly in funded facilities, and owners keep them in use and in repair |

Three assumptions are tested: condition or access limits use (measure 3); local groups would use a better facility (measure 2); and owners can afford upkeep afterwards (the capacity criterion).

All three measures share one baseline and one result period. The baseline is the 12 months from 1 February 2026 to 31 January 2027, or 365 days, ending with the last full month before the round opens. It comes from the applicant's records, attached to the application. Applicants without records attach the February count and, for measures 2 and 3, a signed account of the year's user groups and closures. The result is the first 12 months after the works finish, in a short return the agreement requires. NRIO checks a sample of baselines against source records at site visits (Chapter 8).

**Measure 1: booked hours a week.** Average hours a week the facility is booked or open for community use, from booking records. Calculation: hours in the period ÷ 52. For example, 312 hours ÷ 52 = 6.0 hours a week at baseline, and 494 hours ÷ 52 = 9.5 hours afterwards. The change is 9.5 − 6.0 = 3.5 hours a week, or 3.5 ÷ 6.0 = 58.3 per cent. Report the change in hours as the main result, because a facility closed for the whole baseline has zero hours, and a percentage change from zero cannot be calculated. A four-week count gives hours ÷ 4.

**Measure 2: regular user groups.** The number of different community groups that used the facility in at least 6 of the 12 months, from the same records. For example, 4 groups at baseline and 7 afterwards is a change of 7 − 4 = 3.

**Measure 3: days lost to condition.** Days when the facility, or the part being upgraded, could not be used because of its condition, such as leaks, unsafe floors or no working toilet. The source is the grantee's closure records, with photos attached to the application. For example, 41 days lost in the baseline year is 41 ÷ 365 = 11.2 per cent of days; none afterwards is a fall of 41 days.

The timing works for Round 1's short-term outcomes. The last projects finish in December 2028, so their result period ends in December 2029 and the return can be due by the end of February 2030, inside the 2029–30 evaluation. Medium-term outcomes fall after it. Round 2's projects finish by about December 2029, too late for a full year of use, so the evaluation will report Round 2 as partial.

For the program, report each measure as the number of facilities that improved and the median change, so one large library cannot swamp many small halls.

Chapter 3 turns these design decisions into grant opportunity guidelines that applicants can rely on.
