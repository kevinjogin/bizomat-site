A recurring work habit can survive a clear intention to change it. You decide to stop checking a project dashboard unnecessarily, yet open it whenever a difficult task stalls. Another reminder to concentrate may have little effect because it does not address what the checking provides in that moment.

James Clear's *Atomic Habits* describes habits through four connected stages: cue, craving, response and reward. The framework is useful as a way to investigate a pattern before choosing a remedy. Applied carefully, it helps distinguish the event that prompts an action, the benefit someone expects, the behaviour itself and the consequence that may encourage repetition.

## Use the model as a set of questions

A **habit** is a learned pattern of behaviour associated with recurring circumstances, often performed with reduced conscious deliberation. Not every repeated task is automatic, and not every failure to act is a habit problem. The model offers questions to investigate rather than a complete explanation of every decision.

In Clear's framework, a **cue** is information that signals a possible opportunity or need. A **craving** is the anticipated change or benefit that makes an action appealing. The word need not mean an overwhelming urge; it can refer to wanting reassurance, progress or an answer.

The **response** is the action or thought that follows. The **reward** is the experienced consequence that makes the response seem useful or satisfying. What a person expects before acting and what they experience afterwards may differ, so examine both.

Together these stages form a **feedback loop**: the consequences of an action can influence what happens when a similar situation returns. Treat any proposed loop as a working explanation to test. Observing behaviour does not give you direct access to another person's motives.

## Start with one clearly defined behaviour

Choose an action specific enough to recognise. Being distracted is broad; opening the order dashboard while drafting a proposal is observable. Improving communication is broad; sending a clarification request immediately after an incomplete handover is more precise.

Define the context and boundary. The same action may be useful in one situation and unnecessary in another. Checking an urgent delivery status can be part of the job, while repeatedly checking an unchanged status may interrupt work without adding information.

Explain what makes the pattern worth examining. It may consume time, create errors or displace an important task. Alternatively, you may want to understand why a helpful action, such as recording a decision, does not happen consistently.

Avoid attaching a character judgement to the description. The purpose is to understand how the behaviour functions in its setting. A label such as lazy or careless supplies little information about the cue, the obstacle or the consequence.

## Observe before selecting an explanation

Clear introduces a habit scorecard as a way to notice routines that normally pass without examination. A business adaptation is a brief record of the relevant action and its surrounding circumstances. Keep the observation narrow enough to use without disrupting the work.

Record when the action happened, what occurred immediately beforehand and what followed. Include whether the action produced new information or resolved the issue it appeared to address. This helps distinguish a useful recurring practice from one that mainly offers temporary relief.

Collect examples when the behaviour does not occur as well. A demanding task completed without repeated checking may reveal a useful difference in information, environment or expectations. Exceptions can challenge an explanation that initially seems obvious.

Keep observation proportionate and transparent when others are involved. A shared investigation of a process does not require intrusive monitoring of personal activity. Agree on the work-related question and the information needed to answer it.

## Identify the cue without assuming it is a notification

Cues can include a time, place, preceding action or a change in the task. A notification is easy to notice, but a moment of uncertainty can also precede a familiar response. Ask what was happening immediately before the behaviour began.

Look for patterns across several observations. If checking happens after a difficult paragraph rather than after a message, removing notifications may address only part of the problem. If it happens when a delivery is due, the issue may be an unclear update arrangement.

Distinguish the cue from a background condition. A busy day may make the behaviour more likely without being the immediate prompt. Recording both can help you design a response that fits the actual situation.

Do not force every example into the same explanation. One behaviour can occur for several reasons. Separate the main patterns when they require different changes, rather than building an increasingly vague account that explains everything after the fact.

## Examine the expected benefit

The anticipated benefit often explains why an unhelpful pattern persists. Rechecking a task may promise reassurance; opening a message may promise a quick answer; tidying a workspace may provide a visible accomplishment when another job is ambiguous.

Ask what the person hoped would change in that moment. If you are examining your own behaviour, answer concretely. If you are discussing someone else's work, ask rather than assign a motive based on appearance.

Recognise when the desired benefit is legitimate. A person who repeatedly asks for updates may need information to make a decision. The improvement may be a dependable update process, not an instruction to stop asking.

Separating the need from the current response creates alternatives. You may still need clarity, progress or a pause, but there may be a more useful way to obtain it. A replacement is more plausible when it addresses the same underlying situation.

## Check whether the intended response is possible

A proposed good habit may fail because the action is difficult, unclear or unavailable. The person may lack access, knowledge, time or authority. More motivation cannot by itself supply a missing input.

**Friction** is the effort or inconvenience involved in performing an action. Some friction is unnecessary, such as searching several locations for the current form. Other friction protects quality, such as reviewing a consequential calculation. Examine what the effort achieves before removing it.

Observe the response as a sequence. A task described as one action may involve finding a record, interpreting it, obtaining approval and entering information elsewhere. A breakdown at any step can prevent completion even when the person remembers the intention.

Test the simplest relevant obstacle first. If access is the problem, improve access and observe what changes. Changing reminders, rewards and tools simultaneously may produce a result while leaving you unable to tell which change mattered.

## Examine what happens immediately afterwards

The consequence of an action can explain why it remains attractive. A check may provide new information, remove uncertainty or simply make a stalled task temporarily disappear from attention. Record the observed result without assuming that the long-term effect is beneficial.

Compare immediate and later consequences. Skipping a handover note may make it easier to finish the current job, while leaving the next person to reconstruct information. The person performing the action may not see that later cost unless the process makes it visible.

Look for feedback that unintentionally supports the wrong behaviour. If urgent rescues receive recognition while routine prevention is ignored, the team may learn more from that pattern than from the stated preference for planning. This is a question about the work arrangement as well as individual habits.

Avoid reducing every consequence to a prize or punishment. A useful result, a clear sense of completion or an easier next step can be enough to make an action worth repeating. Start by understanding the natural consequence already present.

## Worked example: repeated status checking

This is an illustration. A founder drafting product instructions repeatedly checks the status of a sample shipment. The shipment matters, but the carrier updates only occasionally. During an observed work period, the founder opens the dashboard 12 times, with each check taking about two minutes.

Those checks occupy 24 minutes directly: 12 multiplied by two. This calculation excludes any additional time required to resume the writing task. The observation therefore describes measured checking time rather than a claim about the full cost of interruption.

The founder records the context and notices two patterns. Some checks occur at an agreed update time; others happen when the wording of the instructions becomes difficult. The second pattern rarely produces new shipment information.

| Stage | Working explanation for the unnecessary checks |
| --- | --- |
| Cue | Reaching an unclear section of the instructions |
| Expected benefit | A quick sense of progress or certainty |
| Response | Opening the shipment dashboard |
| Immediate consequence | Temporary relief from the writing difficulty |
| Later effect | The unclear section remains unresolved |

The founder keeps the necessary shipment checks and changes the response to a writing difficulty. Before switching tasks, the founder records the unresolved question and chooses one small action, such as testing an instruction against the prototype or marking it for review. This gives the stalled work a concrete next step.

In a comparable trial period, dashboard checks fall to four. At two minutes each, the direct checking time is eight minutes, a reduction of 16 minutes from the original 24. The founder also checks whether important shipment updates were missed and whether the writing actually progressed.

The result would remain provisional. A quieter shipment day or an easier writing section could explain part of the difference. The trial supports further observation of a plausible change; it does not prove that every dashboard check had the same motive or that the new routine will always help.

## Select a change that matches the diagnosis

Clear's four laws of behaviour change connect the stages to practical design choices. In your own work, these can be treated as ways to improve visibility, appeal, ease and the experience after completion. Choose the relevant lever from the evidence rather than applying every technique at once.

| Observed difficulty | Change to consider | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The intended action is not noticed | Put a relevant prompt at the decision point | Is the prompt seen when action is possible? |
| The action has no clear value to the person | Explain or expose its useful consequence | Does the action solve a recognised problem? |
| The action is cumbersome | Remove an unnecessary step or missing input | Does completion become practical? |
| The result is invisible or disappointing | Provide meaningful completion feedback | Is the feedback linked to worthwhile work? |

For an unwanted habit, examine whether the cue can be reduced, the apparent benefit reconsidered or an alternative response made easier. Avoid obstructing legitimate work simply to suppress a visible behaviour. The replacement should preserve the useful need the old response was serving.

## Distinguish a habit issue from a wider process issue

A repeated problem may involve several people, conflicting instructions or a missing decision. An individual habit change can help only within those conditions. If a task requires approval that rarely arrives, the central issue may be the approval process.

Ask who controls the relevant part of the system. The person experiencing the problem may be able to change a personal prompt but unable to alter workload or customer commitments. Bring the wider constraint to the appropriate decision-maker instead of assigning the entire solution to that person.

Check whether the desired behaviour is reasonable under the actual conditions. Asking for immediate updates while scheduling every available minute creates a conflict. Resolve the expectations rather than treating inconsistent performance as a mystery of motivation.

The approach in [Turning workplace initiative into useful improvements](/reados/collections/work-habits-that-stick/turning-workplace-initiative-into-useful-improvements/) can help turn a diagnosis into a bounded process trial. The habit framework is most useful when it sharpens the question the trial is intended to answer.

## Review the explanation as well as the result

Before the trial, state what you expect to observe and what would challenge the explanation. If the problem is access, making the information available should change completion under comparable conditions. If it does not, inspect the next obstacle rather than defending the original diagnosis.

Review both helpful and unhelpful effects. A reduction in unnecessary checking is only useful if required information still reaches the person. A faster handover is only useful if the recipient can continue without additional reconstruction.

Keep the observation period suitable for the frequency of the habit. A behaviour that occurs several times daily provides different evidence from one that occurs once each month. There is no fixed number of days that makes every conclusion reliable.

Record whether to retain, revise or stop the change. Keep the reasoning brief and specific so that the same problem can be understood later if conditions change or the habit returns.

## Apply the method in a small Australian business

Begin with one repeated event that has a clear work consequence. A founder might examine how decisions are captured after a call; a workshop might examine how shared tools are returned. A narrow investigation is easier to carry through than a general campaign to improve discipline.

Where people work across locations or schedules, check whether the cue occurs when action is possible. A reminder sent while someone is away may be irrelevant by the time they return. A prompt attached to the actual handover may fit the work more closely.

Use ordinary records and conversations before introducing new monitoring tools. The information required for a useful diagnosis may be modest. Keep the effort proportional to the problem and avoid collecting detail that does not inform the decision.

Maintain a clear boundary around the method. It is intended for ordinary work patterns, not for diagnosing health conditions or treating serious behavioural difficulties. Those situations require appropriate expertise beyond a business habit exercise.

## Common mistakes in habit diagnosis

One mistake is naming a motive without asking or observing. The same action can serve different purposes. Keep explanations provisional until the evidence supports a useful distinction.

Another is removing the visible behaviour while leaving the need unresolved. If repeated questions reflect missing information, suppressing them may simply conceal the problem. Improve the information arrangement as part of the response.

A third is changing several features simultaneously and attributing the result to a favourite theory. A limited trial makes the reasoning easier to assess. Where several changes are necessary, acknowledge that the effect of each remains uncertain.

Finally, avoid treating Clear's model as a complete account of human behaviour. Use it to organise observations, then include the practical constraints and alternative explanations that the situation presents.

## Questions to ask

- What exact behaviour am I trying to understand?
- What happens immediately before and after it?
- What useful change does the person expect from the action?
- Is the intended alternative actually possible under current conditions?
- Which small change would test the most plausible explanation?
- What evidence would make me revise that explanation?

## Bringing it together

Changing a habit is easier to reason about when the pattern is visible. Clear's cue, craving, response and reward framework provides a way to examine what prompts an action and what may keep it recurring.

Observe the work, identify a plausible explanation and test a change that fits it. Keep the person's legitimate needs and the wider process in view so that the improvement resolves a problem rather than merely hiding a behaviour.
