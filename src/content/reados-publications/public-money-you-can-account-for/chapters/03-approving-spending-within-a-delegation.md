A spending approval is a small act with a long tail. It commits public money before any invoice arrives, it fixes who answers for the decision, and it becomes the record an auditor reads a year later. Most approvals are routine. The ones that go wrong usually looked routine too.

Lena Moss signed purchase orders for years in private-sector operations. There, an approval meant that the manager agreed and the budget could take it. In an Australian Government agency it means more. The approver must hold the power, use it within its limits, and be able to show why the decision was a proper use of public money. This chapter follows Lena through her first large approval and through a request that should never have reached her in the form it did.

## Where the power to commit money comes from

Section 23 of the PGPA Act gives the accountable authority of a non-corporate Commonwealth entity the power, on behalf of the Commonwealth, to enter into, vary and administer arrangements, and to approve a commitment of relevant money. An arrangement includes a contract, agreement, deed or understanding. Relevant money is, in short, money the Commonwealth holds or has in its bank accounts.

The accountable authority cannot sign every order, so section 110 lets it delegate powers to officials by written instrument. That instrument, read with your agency's Accountable Authority Instructions, is the source of your authority. A position title, a team habit or an email from a senior officer is not a delegation (Chapter 1).

Approving a commitment and entering into the contract are separate powers, even when one person uses both on the same day. Approving the commitment is the decision that the spending should go ahead, for this purpose, at this value. Check your instrument for which powers you hold and on what conditions.

Two questions sit behind every approval. The first is about money: is the whole value within my delegation, and is there budget for it? The second is about method: was the supplier chosen in a way that the Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) and my agency's instructions allow? A delegate who answers only the first question has not finished the job.

## What the law asks of the person approving

Section 18 of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) says the official approving a commitment of relevant money must record the approval in writing as soon as practicable after giving it. The approval and the record must also follow any written requirements, including requirements about proper use. In practice that means your delegation and your agency's Accountable Authority Instructions.

The guide to that section makes three further points for an official acting under a delegation. Use the power consistently with the delegation. Comply with the accountable authority's instructions. Comply with sections 25 to 29 of the PGPA Act. It singles out two of those duties. Section 25 asks for the care and diligence a reasonable person would exercise in your position and circumstances. Section 26 asks you to act honestly, in good faith and for a proper purpose.

"Proper" has a defined meaning. Section 8 of the Act says proper use of public resources is efficient, effective, economical and ethical. Those four words become four questions a delegate can ask of any request:

- **Efficient:** does this get the result with sensible use of money, time and effort?
- **Effective:** will it achieve what the program needs?
- **Economical:** is the cost reasonable for what we get, and is there budget for it?
- **Ethical:** was the process honest and fair, and is anyone conflicted?

Care and diligence does not mean redoing everyone's work. It means reading what is in front of you, checking the points that matter, and asking when something does not add up.

## The case: a facility condition survey

This is an illustration. The Facilities Director wants a condition survey of all 46 facilities before NRIO goes to market for a new maintenance contract (Chapter 4). Without it, the branch would price five years of maintenance on old site reports. The survey serves NRIO's purpose directly: communities cannot have safe, working facilities if nobody knows their condition.

Grace ran the procurement. Its estimated value was above the A$125,000 procurement threshold, so Division 2 of the CPRs applied, and NRIO's Accountable Authority Instructions required central procurement's sign-off on the method before release. Central procurement cleared an open approach to market. The evaluation report recommends one supplier at a fixed price of A$180,000 including GST: 46 site inspections at A$3,500 each (A$161,000) plus A$19,000 for the condition report and asset data. The contract runs four months, has no options, and uses the Commonwealth Contracting Suite, which the CPRs require for contracts under A$200,000.

The CPRs state thresholds and values inclusive of GST, and NRIO's delegation limits follow the same rule. The branch budget is kept exclusive of GST (Chapter 2), so Arun records the commitment against the suppliers line the way NRIO's finance system requires. Never compare a GST-inclusive approval with a GST-exclusive budget line without converting one of them.

Lena works through the request before she signs.

| Check | What Lena finds |
| --- | --- |
| Whole value | A$180,000 including GST, the full four months, no options |
| Delegation | Within her A$250,000 limit for commitments of relevant money |
| Method | Above A$125,000, and central procurement's sign-off is on file |
| Funds | Arun confirms the survey is already in the suppliers line forecast, so approving it turns forecast spend into a commitment rather than adding to the forecast overspend |
| Proper use | The evaluation report compares the offers and explains the choice |
| Interests | One to declare (below) |
| Roles | The Facilities Director asked for it; Lena approves; others receipt and pay |

Lena declares her interest, records her approval in NRIO's finance system the same day, and attaches the evaluation report and the sign-off.

## What the approval record should show

A good record lets someone who was not in the room understand the decision a year later. An email that says "approved" shows that someone agreed to something. It does not show what, under which authority, or why.

```figure
type: steps
title: What an approval record shows
caption: Record the approval in writing as soon as practicable after giving it, in the form your agency's written requirements set.
- What and why | The goods or services, the supplier and the program need they meet
- Whole value | The maximum over the entire duration, including options and GST
- Authority | Your name and position, the delegation instrument and the item you relied on
- Method | How the supplier was chosen and who cleared the method
- Funds | The budget line and finance's confirmation
- Interests | Declarations made, or a statement that you have none
- *Decision | Approved, the date it was given, and any conditions
```

Two habits protect the record. First, never date an approval earlier than the day you gave it. If your instructions allow an urgent approval by phone, write it down as soon as practicable afterwards and say when and how you gave it. Second, attach the evidence you relied on rather than describing it. A link to an evaluation report that is later moved or edited proves little. Ask your finance area where your system keeps attachments and for how long.

## Keep the four roles apart

Separation of duties means no single person controls a purchase from start to finish. Four roles matter most:

- Requester: states the need and the budget it will come from.
- Approver: the delegate who approves the commitment.
- Receipter: confirms that the goods or services arrived and meet the contract.
- Payer: releases payment against a correctly rendered invoice.

For the survey, the Facilities Director is the requester and Lena the approver. The site coordinators confirm each inspection took place, and Tom accepts the final report against the contract. Arun matches the order, the receipt and the invoice before payment is released.

Small teams cannot always keep every role in different hands. When one person must hold two roles, add a compensating check, such as a monthly review of orders and payments by someone outside the transaction. Your Accountable Authority Instructions may say which roles must be separate. Read them; do not assume.

## Declare interests before you approve

Section 29 of the PGPA Act is short: "An official of a Commonwealth entity who has a material personal interest that relates to the affairs of the entity must disclose details of the interest." Section 16 of the PGPA Rule says officials other than the accountable authority make that disclosure in line with the accountable authority's instructions. The Public Service Act has a similar but separate requirement in subsection 13(7) of the APS Code of Conduct: take reasonable steps to avoid any conflict of interest, real or apparent, and disclose material personal interests.

In the survey case, the recommended supplier's project lead worked with Lena at her previous employer for two years. They have no financial link and have not been in contact since. It may not be a material personal interest. It could still look like a conflict to a losing tenderer. NRIO's instructions ask officials to declare in writing whenever they are unsure, and the Branch Manager decides how the matter is managed. Lena declares before she signs. The Branch Manager records that she may approve, because she had no part in the evaluation and has no tie to the supplier. Had the answer been to step aside, the approval would have gone to the Branch Manager, not to the Facilities Director who asked for the survey.

Declare first. A declaration made after the decision explains what happened. It does not protect the decision.

## Value the whole need, not the order

The CPRs are direct on this: "A procurement must not be divided into separate parts solely for the purpose of avoiding a relevant procurement threshold." They also say how to value a procurement. Use the maximum value over its entire duration. If that cannot be estimated, treat the procurement as valued above the threshold. If a procurement is in several parts, its value includes the maximum value of all the contracts.

NRIO's Accountable Authority Instructions apply the same logic to delegations. A delegate approves the whole commitment, not a slice of it. Approving a first stage that you know will lead to more is not approving the commitment. It is approving part of it and leaving the rest to chance.

## When it goes wrong: three orders for one cleaning service

This is an illustration. Over one fortnight, a regional site coordinator raises three purchase orders for the same cleaning service from the same supplier. Each order covers two years of cleaning at one of three sites and is worth A$95,000 including GST. Each looks unremarkable on its own: below the A$125,000 threshold and below Lena's A$250,000 delegation. In a busy week, Lena approves the first two.

When the third arrives, Arun's weekly commitments report groups open orders by supplier. It shows A$190,000 already committed with this supplier for cleaning and another A$95,000 waiting. Lena stops and does the sum.

- Whole value: 3 × A$95,000 = A$285,000 including GST.
- Against the threshold: A$285,000 is above A$125,000, so Division 2 of the CPRs applies to the whole procurement.
- Against her delegation: A$285,000 is above A$250,000 by A$35,000, so the commitment is not hers to approve.

Whether the coordinator meant to avoid the threshold is a question of fact, and Lena does not assume either way. The value does not depend on intent. A procurement in several parts includes the maximum value of all of them.

What should have happened is simple. The coordinator takes one need across three sites to Grace. Grace values it at A$285,000 for the two years. Central procurement clears the method, as NRIO's instructions require above A$125,000. A delegate whose limit covers A$285,000 approves it: the Facilities Director, whose limit is A$500,000, or the Branch Manager. Grace also asks whether other sites need the same service, because the whole need sets the value.

What Lena does now matters as much. She holds the third order. She tells the Branch Manager the same day, in writing. With central procurement, she works out how to deal with the two orders already issued and whether to approach the market for the whole need. She reports the matter through NRIO's process for possible non-compliance with the finance law. She talks to the coordinator to establish what happened, not to assign blame. She does not cancel the orders quietly and raise them again under a different description. Then she fixes the control. Arun's report now flags any supplier with more than one order for a similar service within 90 days, and the coordinators get a short briefing on valuing a whole need.

## Exercise: check five draft approvals

This is an illustration. Five draft approvals are waiting for Lena. For each, decide whether she should approve it as drafted, approve it after a fix, or send it elsewhere. State the value you would use and the reason.

| Draft | Request | Raised by | Notes on the draft |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Roof repair at one depot, A$62,000 including GST | A site coordinator | Three written quotes, all from Australian businesses; funds confirmed; no interests |
| B | Inspection app subscription, A$4,000 a month for 36 months | The Facilities Director | Value shown as A$48,000 (year one); no method sign-off attached |
| C | Security patrols, two years at A$115,000 a year, plus a one-year option at A$120,000 | A site coordinator | Value shown as A$230,000; central procurement cleared the method at A$230,000 |
| D | Venue hire for a branch planning day, A$6,600 | Lena | Lena shown as approver |
| E | Office furniture, A$18,700 | Grace | The supplier is part-owned by Grace's partner; no declaration on file |

### Worked answer

**A: approve as drafted.** A$62,000 is below the A$125,000 threshold and within Lena's A$250,000 limit. It is at or above A$10,000 and below the threshold, so the CPRs required NRIO to invite only Australian businesses unless an official documented why that was not appropriate. All three quotes came from Australian businesses. Lena records the approval. Because the contract is at or above A$10,000, it must be reported on AusTender within 42 days of entering into it.

**B: fix, then approve.** The whole value is A$4,000 × 36 = A$144,000, not A$48,000. The draft shows one third of the commitment (A$48,000 ÷ A$144,000). At A$144,000 the purchase is above the threshold, so Division 2 applies and central procurement must clear the method. The value is within Lena's limit, so once the draft shows A$144,000 and the sign-off is attached, she can approve it.

**C: send to another delegate.** The whole value is 2 × A$115,000 + A$120,000 = A$350,000. That exceeds Lena's limit by A$100,000. The Facilities Director (limit A$500,000) can approve it, as a site coordinator raised it. Central procurement cleared the method at A$230,000, which left out the A$120,000 option, so its sign-off must be confirmed at A$350,000 first.

**D: send to another delegate.** The value is within Lena's limit, but she is the requester. NRIO's instructions keep requester and approver apart, so the Branch Manager approves it.

**E: pause, then decide.** A partner's share in the supplier is the kind of interest that section 29 of the PGPA Act and subsection 13(7) of the Public Service Act are about, so Grace declares it in writing under NRIO's instructions. Lena moves the purchase to Tom. He checks that the quotes were genuinely competitive and that only Australian businesses were invited, since A$18,700 is at or above A$10,000 and below the threshold. A declared interest does not automatically rule the supplier out. Lena approves only when the declaration and Tom's check are on file and the purchase is still value for money.

Only draft A is ready as written. B and E need fixes first; C and D belong to other delegates.
