Chapter 1 set out the problem: two welders, eight working days, 120 welding hours, and three customers who want 150. Somebody will get less than they asked for. The question this chapter answers is who, and on what basis.

This is distributive justice in Aquinas's sense, the fair sharing of something common among several people, measured by proportion rather than by equal amounts (*Summa Theologiae* II-II q.61 a.2). Its characteristic vice he calls respect of persons: giving something to someone for a reason that has nothing to do with what makes them deserving of it (II-II q.63 a.1). His example is appointing a man to a professorship because of who he is, rather than because he has the knowledge the post requires. The workshop faces a modern version of the same temptation twice in one week: once in sharing its welding hours, and once in choosing a subcontractor. This chapter works through both.

## State the basis before sharing

Proportion needs a basis. Equal shares of 40 hours each were shown in Chapter 1 to treat the three customers unequally. Shares proportional to hours wanted, 80% each, treated them equally in one sense but ignored real differences between them. Before any hours are allocated, the owner has to say what entitles a customer to welding time.

This is an illustration. The three jobs differ in ways that matter.

| Customer | Welding hours wanted | Commitment | Due | Effect of delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quarry: conveyor guard repairs | 50 | Signed order | Day 5 | The conveyor cannot run without its guard |
| Food manufacturer: tank | 40 of 62 | Signed order, accepted after Chapter 3 | Requested in three weeks; date confirmed in Chapter 8 | Production at risk if the old tank fails |
| Equipment dealer: 12 trailer frames | 60 | Informal enquiry, no order | Not stated | Dealer's stock arrives later |

Three bases suggest themselves, and each produces a different allocation.

```figure
- Proportional to need: quarry | 40
- Proportional to need: tank | 32

```

**Proportional to need** gives every customer 80% of what it asked for: 40 hours to the quarry, 32 to the tank and 48 to the frames. It sounds neutral, but it leaves both signed orders short and gives the largest share to the one customer with no order at all.

**Entitlement first** gives the signed orders what they need, in the order of their due dates, and shares whatever remains among unconfirmed work. The quarry receives 50 hours, the tank 40, and the frames the remaining 30. The dealer is told honestly that 30 hours are available now and the rest the following week.

**Friend first** gives the dealer, a long-standing friend of the owner, all 60 hours he asked for, leaving 60 hours for 90 hours of signed work. Either the quarry's guard or the tank would be late.

## Why entitlement is the right basis here

The entitlement basis is not the only defensible one in every business. It is the right one here because of what the workshop has already given the customers.

A signed order is a promise. The workshop has, in exchange for the quarry's and the food manufacturer's commitments, undertaken to supply by stated dates. Those undertakings are what entitle the two customers to capacity ahead of the dealer, who has made no commitment and received none. The quarry's job also carries the most serious consequence of delay: a conveyor cannot be run without its guard, so every day of delay is a day of lost production for the quarry. That consequence would justify giving the quarry priority even if both orders had the same date.

Other businesses can fairly use other bases. A service desk may serve requests in the order received. A maintenance contractor may give priority to customers on a paid response agreement, because that priority is what those customers bought. A hospital triages by clinical need. Each of these is a legitimate basis because it is connected to what is being shared and is announced in advance. What they have in common is that the basis is a feature of the claim, not of the claimant. The test Aquinas's analysis supplies is simple to apply: if two customers with identical jobs, commitments and consequences would be treated differently, the difference must come from something other than the jobs, and that something is usually who they are.

The dealer is not being treated unfairly. He is being treated according to his actual position, which is that of a valued customer who has enquired but not ordered. He is told the truth about what can be done and when, and he can decide whether to order on that basis.

### A failure: respect of persons

The owner's first allocation was the friend-first one. The reasoning was understandable: the dealer had sent the workshop a great deal of work over the years, always paid promptly, and had asked as a personal favour. The owner did not think of it as favouritism. It felt like loyalty.

The error was detected when the production planner tried to schedule the week and found that the quarry's repairs and the tank could not both be met. The planner asked the question that exposes respect of persons: what is it about the dealer's job, as distinct from the dealer, that entitles it to these hours? The honest answer was nothing. The frames had no order, no date and no consequence of delay beyond inconvenience. The entitlement belonged to the person, not the job.

The correction was to adopt the entitlement allocation and to tell the dealer plainly why. The owner's loyalty to a good customer is real and appropriate, and it can be expressed in ways that do not take capacity from others: a firm date for the frames the following week, priority for his next confirmed order, or a call before the next quiet period. What it cannot fairly do is turn another customer's signed order into the casualty of a personal relationship.

## Choosing a subcontractor

The tank must be pickled and passivated after fabrication, which the workshop subcontracts. Two local firms can do it. This is an exchange with whichever firm wins, but the choice between them is a distribution of opportunity: each firm has a claim to be judged on the same criteria.

The owner and the production manager agree the weights before requesting quotes: price 30%, lead time 20%, quality 25% and delivery reliability 25%. Quality and reliability are rated from 1 to 5 using the workshop's records of previous jobs. This is an illustration.

| | Firm A | Firm B |
|---|---|---|
| Price | A$1,200 | A$950 |
| Lead time | 5 days | 9 days |
| Quality rating | 4 | 3 |
| Reliability rating | 4 | 3 |

The BIZOMAT [supplier comparison](/tools/supplier-compare/) scores each criterion from 0 to 100: the lowest price scores 100 and a dearer price scores in proportion, the shortest lead time scores 100 and a longer one in proportion, and each rating out of 5 is converted to a percentage. It combines the four with the stated weights, normalising them if they do not add to 100, and reports a weighted total for each supplier. It excludes any supplier rated 1 or 2 on quality or reliability, whatever its price, and it calls the result too close to call if the totals differ by less than 3 points.

On the agreed weights, firm A scores 79.2 for price, 100 for lead time, 80 for quality and 80 for reliability, a weighted total of 83.75. Firm B scores 100 for price, 55.6 for lead time, 60 for quality and 60 for reliability, a weighted total of 71.11. Firm A wins by more than 12 points.

## A failure: weights changed after the quotes

When the quotes arrived, the owner noticed that firm B was A$250 cheaper and suggested that, since the tank job was tight on margin, price should count for more. He proposed weights of 60% price, 10% lead time, 15% quality and 15% reliability.

```figure
type: compare
title: The same quotes under two sets of weights
cols: Firm A | Firm B | Result
caption: Changing the weights after the quotes are known turns a clear result into one the tool cannot separate.
- *Weights agreed before quotes: 30, 20, 25, 25 | 83.75 | 71.11 | Firm A, by 12.6 points
- Weights proposed after quotes: 60, 10, 15, 15 | 81.50 | 83.56 | Too close to call, 2.1 points
```

Under the new weights, firm B scores 83.56 and firm A 81.50. The difference is 2.1 points, which the tool reports as too close to call. The change of weights has not produced a clear win for firm B, but it has turned a decisive result into a toss-up, and it was chosen with the result in view.

This is respect of persons in a different form. The criteria are meant to express what the workshop needs from a passivation subcontractor, decided before anyone knew which firm would do well on them. Changing them once the quotes are open is choosing the winner first and the reasons afterwards. It is unfair to firm A, which priced its work against the criteria it was told about, and it is a poor decision for the workshop, because the original weights reflected a judgement made without the distraction of a particular price.

The error was caught because the production manager had recorded the weights, with the date, in the request for quotation. The correction was to keep them. If the workshop genuinely believes price matters more on future jobs, it can change the weights for the next request, before the quotes arrive. The Library chapter on [running a fair tender process](/reados/collections/project-delivery-procurement-and-governance/running-a-fair-tender-process/) discusses the same principle at a larger scale.

## Burdens are distributed too

Distributive justice is not only about sharing good things. Burdens have to be shared as well, and the same rules apply. The rush request in Chapter 2 meant about 20 hours of overtime for the welders over the following fortnight. Who works it?

The workshop's practice, written into its employment arrangements and known to everyone, is that overtime is offered first to those who want it, then shared in rotation, with a stated maximum for any one person in a fortnight. This is an illustration. One welder volunteers for 12 hours and the other for 8, which covers the 20 hours without anyone being directed to work. Had nobody volunteered, the rotation would have split the hours 10 and 10.

The basis matters for the same reason as with capacity. If overtime always went to the owner's favourite, or was always pushed onto the most junior person, the distribution would be unfair even though every individual instruction looked reasonable. And if one welder had a known reason for limited availability, such as caring responsibilities, a fair basis would take it into account openly, in the written arrangement, rather than through quiet exceptions that others resent because they cannot see the reason. Australian employment law and any applicable award or agreement also govern overtime, including when it may reasonably be refused, and the workshop's arrangement has to sit within them. The point here is narrower: whatever the rules, applying them on a stated basis is what makes the sharing fair.

## Telling the customer who receives less

Fair distribution includes how the decision is communicated. The dealer receives 30 hours instead of 60, and he will notice. The owner's message to him says three things: that the workshop has two signed orders with dates this week, one of them a safety guard, that it can give his frames 30 hours now and the rest at the start of the following week, and that if he places an order today the second half will be scheduled with a firm date. It does not apologise for having other customers, and it does not hint that he has been treated as a special case, because he has not.

The dealer orders the frames that afternoon. Being told the basis, rather than being told the workshop was busy, gave him something he could act on.

## A checklist for distributions

The two decisions in this chapter share a structure, and the structure generalises to any distribution in a small business: overtime, training places, a bonus pool, the order in which jobs are started.

```figure
type: checklist
title: Before sharing anything scarce
caption: Each line guards against respect of persons, whether in capacity, opportunity or reward.
- State the basis before the shares are known | entitlement, need, contribution or a mix
- Apply the same basis to everyone it covers | no exceptions for who someone is
- Record the basis and the date | so that it cannot drift towards a preferred result
- Tell those who receive less the reason | in terms of the basis, not the person
- x Do not change the basis after seeing the outcome | change it for next time instead
- ? Is any share explained only by who received it? | If so, it is a favour, not a distribution
```

## Exercise: a tighter week

In a later week the workshop has 100 welding hours. Two customers have signed orders: one needing 30 hours and one needing 45. Two others have unconfirmed enquiries needing 40 and 20 hours. Allocate the hours on the entitlement-first basis, sharing any remainder in proportion to hours wanted, and say what each unconfirmed customer should be told.

**Worked answer.** The signed orders receive what they need first: 30 + 45 = 75 hours, leaving 25. The two unconfirmed enquiries want 60 hours between them, so each receives the same proportion, 25 ÷ 60, about 41.7% of what it asked for: about 16.7 hours for the first and 8.3 hours for the second. In practice the workshop would round to whole hours or half-days, perhaps 17 and 8, and tell each customer how many hours can be given this week, how many remain, and when the rest can be scheduled. Each should also be told the basis: confirmed orders are scheduled first, and remaining capacity is shared in proportion. A customer told the basis can decide whether confirming an order is worth doing; a customer simply told "we're busy" cannot.
