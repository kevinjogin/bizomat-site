## About this book

Thermit welding makes its own steel. A mixture of aluminium powder and iron oxide is ignited in a crucible; the aluminium takes the oxygen from the iron in a violent reaction that leaves white-hot liquid iron under a layer of molten aluminium oxide slag; and the iron is run into a mould around the joint, where it melts into the parts and freezes into a weld. No electricity, no gas supply and no welding machine are needed, which is why the process has joined rails in the field for more than a century and why it was once used for repairing ship stern frames, mill housings and crankshafts too large to move.

This book is for maintenance and reliability engineers, welding supervisors, rail and heavy-plant engineers and students who need to understand aluminothermic welding well enough to specify, assess or supervise it, and to judge when it is the right repair. It assumes basic chemistry, physics and algebra in SI units. It does not teach anyone to carry out a thermit weld. That requires the supplier's training and accreditation, approved procedures and the specific safety controls the process demands.

## Two early books as a starting point

Hans Goldschmidt of Essen discovered how to ignite a cold mixture of aluminium and iron oxide around 1895 or 1896, patented the reduction of metals by aluminium in 1897 and used it to weld iron about 1898. By 1910, when Richard N. Hart published *Welding: Theory, Practice, Apparatus and Tests*, the Thermit process was established for rail joints and heavy repairs. Ethan Viall, editor of *American Machinist*, gave it a large section of his *Gas Torch and Thermit Welding* in 1921.

Both books are full of numbers: the proportions of the mixture, the calculated and measured temperatures, the rule for how much thermit a mould needs, the size of the collar, the additions that strengthen the steel and the results of tests. Most of those numbers can be checked with modern chemistry and physics, and doing so is a good way to understand the process. Each chapter takes one of their themes, checks it and works it through a modern case.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A quarry's primary jaw crusher has a cast steel side frame with a crack right through one of its tension members, a solid section 200 × 150 mm. The crusher is large, the frame is expensive and slow to replace, and the quarry's maintenance engineer must choose a repair. One option, put forward by a specialist contractor, is a thermit fusion weld across the cracked member. The quarry also has a rail siding whose rails were joined by aluminothermic welds, and one of those welds has failed.

Through the book, the engineer works out the chemistry, the charge, the mould, the preheat, the shrinkage allowance, the weld metal, the testing and the cost, and compares them with arc welding and replacement. The quarry, the crusher, the dimensions, quantities and costs are invented for teaching. They are not KEVOS® work, and nothing in the case is a procedure for carrying out a thermit weld.

## How to use the book

Chapters 1 to 3 cover the reaction: its chemistry, its heat and temperature, and the ignition, moisture and other hazards that come with it. Chapters 4 to 7 cover the weld: sizing the charge, moulds and risers, preheating, and shrinkage. Chapter 8 deals with the weld metal and its additions, Chapter 9 with rail welding today, and Chapter 10 with testing, cost and the choice of repair. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Thermit welding involves molten metal at well over 2,000 °C, violent reactions with moisture, fire, fumes and heavy lifting. It must be carried out only by trained and accredited people, using the supplier's approved materials and procedures, under the controls the work health and safety regulator, the asset owner and, for rail, the rail safety framework require. Rail welds in Australia are qualified under AS 1085.20 and the rail infrastructure manager's standards. Repairs to load-bearing machinery must be designed, approved and inspected by competent engineers. The 1910 and 1921 methods are described as history, not practice.
