Suspected misconduct rarely arrives with a label. A team leader closes the door and says, "I don't know if this is anything, but…". What the manager does next matters more than most of what follows. A short note and a prompt report keep the matter fair. A quiet word or a little checking can make a fair process impossible.

The manager's role is narrower than most new managers expect. You listen, write down what you were told, report it through your agency's procedures and keep the team working. You do not investigate, decide whether the Code of Conduct was breached or choose a sanction. Those tasks belong to people the procedures appoint, who must be independent.

This is an illustration. Late in November 2026, Leila Farah tells Hannah Lee that Aaron Liu, a non-ongoing APS 4, appears to have opened the client record of his neighbour's community group without a work reason. This chapter follows what Hannah does, and what she leaves alone.

## The Code of Conduct and who decides a breach

The APS Code of Conduct is in [section 13 of the Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text). It binds every APS employee, and the Act's definition of an APS employee includes any person engaged under section 22, so non-ongoing staff are covered just as ongoing staff are. An employee "must behave honestly and with integrity" (s 13(1)) and "must act with care and diligence" (s 13(2)) in connection with their employment. They must take reasonable steps to avoid conflicts of interest, real or apparent (s 13(7)), "use Commonwealth resources in a proper manner and for a proper purpose" (s 13(8)), and not improperly use inside information or their duties, status, power or authority to benefit themselves or anyone else (s 13(10)).

Several of those could be relevant when someone opens a record without a work reason. Which applies, if any, is for the decision-maker. A manager's report describes what was seen, not which subsection was breached.

Section 15(3) says "An Agency Head must establish written procedures" for deciding whether an employee has breached the Code and what sanction, if any, to impose. The Agency Head, or a person to whom the power has been delegated in writing (s 78(7)), makes those decisions under the procedures. Section 15(1) lists the sanctions, from "termination of employment" to "a reprimand".

Reporting, by contrast, is everyone's job. The Commissioner's Directions 2022 say that upholding the Ethical value requires "reporting and addressing misconduct and other unacceptable behaviour by public servants in a fair, timely and effective way" (s 14(f)). For a manager, that means reporting promptly to the people who run the procedures, then letting them run.

## What the procedures must contain

Each agency's procedures differ in detail, but they share a legal floor. Section 15(4) of the Act says they "must comply with basic procedural requirements set out in Commissioner's Directions" and "must have due regard to procedural fairness". Section 15(7) requires them to be "made publicly available". Read your agency's procedures now, not on the day you need them.

The basic requirements are in [Part 7 of the Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text). Before a determination, reasonable steps must be taken to tell the employee "the details of the suspected breach" and the possible sanctions, and to give them "a reasonable opportunity to make a statement" (s 59). The same applies again before a sanction (s 60). The process must run "with as little formality and as much expedition as a proper consideration of the matter allows" (s 62), and a determination needs a written record (s 63).

Section 61 is about appearances as well as facts. The Agency Head must take reasonable steps to ensure that whoever decides the breach, and whoever decides any sanction, "is, and appears to be, independent and unbiased". A manager is rarely that person for their own staff. This is an illustration. NRIO's fictional procedures separate three roles. An investigator gathers the facts. A delegate, an EL2 in another branch with no link to the section, decides. Hannah and Leila may be asked for statements as witnesses.

Where review is available, an application to review a determination or a sanction goes to the Merit Protection Commissioner, not the agency (Regulations s 38(2)). Any reviewer reads the record, and the record starts with the manager's first note.

## The first hour: listen, write it down, report

This is an illustration. On Wednesday 25 November 2026, Leila was drafting a reply to a community group's complaint about a facility booking. NRIO's client record system shows who has opened a record and when. Leila saw that Aaron had opened the group's record on three days in November, with no call, email or task for the group assigned to him. She remembered him mentioning that his neighbour runs the group. At 2.30 pm she asked Hannah for ten minutes with the door closed.

Hannah did four things that afternoon.

First, she listened, and asked only what a report would need: what Leila saw, where, when, and who else knew. She did not ask what Leila thought Aaron was up to.

Second, she wrote a note while it was fresh: the date and time, what Leila said, the record and dates shown, and that nobody else had been told. "Aaron opened the record on three days with no assigned work" is a fact. "Aaron was snooping for his neighbour" is a conclusion, and possibly a wrong one.

Third, she reported it. NRIO's fictional procedures ask managers to report a suspected breach to the People Branch within one working day. Hannah rang the conduct adviser at 3.40 pm and emailed her note at 4.05 pm, marked sensitive. She told her Director only that she had referred a conduct matter, as the procedures ask.

Fourth, she thought about Leila, because a report about a colleague can be a public interest disclosure.

What Hannah did not do matters as much. She did not open the group's record or search Aaron's other activity. She did not speak to Aaron or tell anyone else in the team. And she did not tell Leila that "nothing will happen" or that "he'll be dealt with": both are promises she has no power to keep.

## Reporting, not investigating

It is tempting to find out a little more first, in case there is an innocent explanation. Resist it. Once a manager starts asking questions, three things go wrong.

The first is fairness. Section 59 of the Directions requires the employee to be told the details of the suspected breach and the possible sanctions, and to have a reasonable opportunity to make a statement, before any determination. A corridor conversation gives none of that.

The second is the evidence. Logs, emails and documents can change, and memories merge when people talk. The People Branch can have system records preserved. A manager who opens records to look for more adds entries to the very logs that matter.

The third is independence. A manager who has asked the questions and formed a view no longer "appears to be" independent and unbiased (s 61), and may have become a witness.

The manager's role after the report is limited: answer the People Branch's questions accurately, free staff for interviews, pass on new information without going looking for it, and manage the employee's work as you would anyone's.

## Public interest disclosures

Older guidance points to section 16 of the Public Service Act for whistleblower protection, but that section was repealed with effect from 15 January 2014. The protections are now in the [Public Interest Disclosure Act 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00133/latest/text) (the PID Act). A person who makes a public interest disclosure "is not subject to any civil, criminal or administrative liability (including disciplinary action) for making" it (s 10(1)(a)). Section 13 explains what taking a reprisal means, and section 19 makes it an offence.

Supervisors have duties of their own. Section 60A applies when a public official tells their supervisor something the supervisor has reasonable grounds to believe "concerns, or could concern" disclosable conduct, and the supervisor is not an authorised officer. The supervisor must tell the person the disclosure could be treated as an internal disclosure, explain how it would be handled and the protections the Act gives, and "as soon as reasonably practicable" give the information to an authorised officer of the agency (s 60A(2)–(3)). Disclosable conduct includes conduct by a public official that involves "abusing his or her position as a public official" (s 29(2)(a)).

This is an illustration. Leila's report could concern disclosable conduct, so Hannah treated section 60A as applying rather than guessing. Before Leila left, Hannah told her the report could be treated as an internal disclosure, explained what that would mean and the protections that come with it, and said she would pass it to an authorised officer that afternoon. NRIO's fictional procedures name the People Branch's conduct adviser as an authorised officer, so one call met both duties. How the matter proceeds is for the authorised officer and the People Branch to decide.

People rarely say "public interest disclosure", and the Act does not require them to. If you are unsure whether a report is one, ask an authorised officer rather than settle it alone.

## Confidentiality and the team

A suspected breach is not a finding. Until the procedures run, nobody has decided that the employee did anything wrong, and they are entitled to be treated that way.

This is an illustration. The People Branch, the authorised officer and the people they appoint know the details. Hannah and Leila know because they were there. The Director knows a matter was referred. Nobody else in the team is told anything.

The team will still notice things. If asked, Hannah gives the same answer every time: "I can't talk about anyone's individual employment matters, and I'd do the same for you."

This is an illustration. Supporting the team means supporting each person in it.

- **Leila** reported in good faith. Nothing about how she is treated or rostered should change. Hannah checks in with her privately a week later.
- **Aaron** still has a manager. Hannah keeps their weekly one-to-one about his work and makes sure he knows about NRIO's employee assistance service.
- **The rest of the team** needs the work to go on. If interviews take people off the phones, Hannah adjusts the roster without saying why.

The note to s 63 of the Directions says the Archives Act 1983 and the Privacy Act 1988 apply to the written record of a determination. Treat your own notes with the same care, and never keep them in a shared folder.

## Suspension and other interim steps

The power to suspend is in section 14 of the Public Service Regulations 2023. An Agency Head may suspend an employee from duties if the Agency Head believes on reasonable grounds that the employee "has, or may have, breached the Code of Conduct" and that the suspension "is in the public, or the Agency's, interest" (s 14(2)).

The section then sets limits. Suspension "may be with remuneration" (s 14(3)). If it is without remuneration, that period "must not be more than 30 days unless exceptional circumstances apply" (s 14(4)). It must be reviewed "at reasonable intervals" (s 14(5)) and ended at once if the grounds no longer exist or a sanction has been imposed (s 14(6)–(7)). Procedural fairness applies unless the Agency Head is satisfied on reasonable grounds that it would not be appropriate (s 14(8)).

Suspension is a decision for the Agency Head or a person given the power in writing (Regulations s 105(3)), not the manager, and it is not a sanction. The manager's contribution is facts about the work: what the employee does, what access they hold and what other duties exist.

This is an illustration. Asked whether Aaron could work without the group's record, Hannah said he could, because another officer could take the group's enquiries. The delegate decided suspension was not needed. Instead, the system owner removed Aaron's access to that one record, and he kept working on the phones. That step protects the record. It decides nothing about what happened.

## When it goes wrong: the quiet word

This is an illustration of what Hannah did not do. Suppose that on 25 November she had decided to save everyone the fuss. She asks Aaron for a coffee and mentions, kindly, that someone noticed he had been in the group's record. Aaron is embarrassed. He says his neighbour asked whether their complaint had arrived, so he looked, and he apologises. Hannah says, "Fine, let's leave it there." She makes no note, and tells Leila it is sorted.

In January a routine access audit flags the same entries and the People Branch opens a matter. The Director, keen to keep it quick, suggests a two-person decision panel with Hannah on it "because she knows the facts". Hannah agrees.

Now everything is harder. Aaron has explained himself without being told what was alleged or what could follow, the reverse of what s 59 is there to secure, and he was told the matter was closed. Nobody wrote down when the concern was raised, what was seen or what was said over coffee. Leila's report never reached an authorised officer, so the steps s 60A of the PID Act requires were missed. And the panel includes someone who has questioned the employee, formed a view, given an assurance and become a witness. She is not, and does not appear to be, independent and unbiased, so the panel cannot meet s 61.

The People Branch has to rebuild the panel and start again, months after the events. Aaron faces a process he was told would not happen. Leila learns that raising a concern leads nowhere.

The control is the one this chapter began with: write it down, report it the same day and stay out of the decision. A manager who has already had a quiet word should tell the People Branch at once, hand over a note of what was said and step back.

## Exercise: five situations

This is an illustration. Each situation happens while Aaron's matter is with the People Branch. Say what Hannah does, which rule applies and what record should exist.

1. On Tuesday 1 December, Aaron stops Hannah in the corridor. "Has someone said something about me? I'd rather just sort it out with you."
2. At the team meeting on Monday 7 December, Sophie asks why Aaron can no longer open a record and whether he is "in trouble".
3. The Director suggests that Hannah decide the matter herself, "since you know the team and it's only a small thing".
4. Marcus tells Hannah that a caller from the community group said "someone at NRIO" had told them how their complaint was going.
5. Ruby asks to speak privately. She saw an officer in another section give a contractor details of a tender before it was released, and asks whether she will get into trouble for saying so.

### Worked answer

1. Hannah does not discuss it or let Aaron explain. Any explanation belongs in the process s 59 of the Directions sets up, after he has been told the details and the possible sanctions. She says the People Branch can answer questions about his access, and reminds him of the employee assistance service. Record: a dated note of the conversation, sent to the People Branch.
2. Hannah says she does not discuss anyone's individual employment matters, then moves on. Afterwards she tells Sophie privately that speculation is unfair to a colleague. The Code's requirement to treat everyone "with respect and courtesy" (s 13(3)) covers how the team talks about Aaron. Record: a line in Hannah's own notes, and a note to the People Branch if speculation continues.
3. Hannah declines. The decision-maker must be, and appear to be, independent and unbiased (Directions s 61), and she heard the first report, manages Aaron and may be a witness. Handling it informally would also bypass the procedures s 15(3) of the Act requires. Record: a short email to the Director giving her answer and the reason, copied to the People Branch.
4. This is new information that may be relevant. Hannah writes down what Marcus told her, with the date and the caller's words as closely as he recalls them, and sends it to the People Branch that day. She does not ask Aaron, call the group or ask Marcus to find out more. The rule is the same as on 25 November: report facts and leave the investigation to others.
5. Giving a contractor tender details early could involve an official "abusing his or her position as a public official" (PID Act s 29(2)(a)), so Ruby's report could concern disclosable conduct, and Hannah is not an authorised officer. Under s 60A she tells Ruby the report could be treated as an internal disclosure, and explains how it would be handled and the Act's protections, including that a discloser is not subject to disciplinary action for making a public interest disclosure (s 10(1)(a)). She gives the information to an authorised officer as soon as reasonably practicable, that day if she can, and asks Ruby to tell her if anyone treats her badly for reporting. Record: a dated note of what Ruby said and what Hannah explained, sent to the authorised officer.
