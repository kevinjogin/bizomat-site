Policy advice is written for one reader who has the power to decide. A minister reads it between many other briefs, often with an adviser's view beside it, and then agrees, asks for more or goes another way. Advice that buries the decision, softens the bad news or bends to what the office wants to hear fails that reader, however good the analysis behind it.

This is an illustration. Grace Mitchell's advice to the Minister on bridge load limits took three written forms in 2027. There was the brief of Fri 13 Aug, the options paper of Fri 29 Oct, and a one-page note a week later, after the Minister's senior adviser pressed for the strengthening fund, Option D. On Thu 11 Nov 2027 the Minister agreed to develop Option C, the central assessment service, as a new policy proposal for the 2028–29 Budget. This chapter follows how that advice was written, kept frank and recorded.

## Who decides and who advises

Ministers decide, and they answer to Parliament for what they decide. The APS advises and implements, and it is accountable "within the framework of Ministerial responsibility" (s 10(4) of the [Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text)). The APS Value of Impartial, in s 10(5), sets the standard for advice: "The APS is apolitical and provides the Government with advice that is frank, honest, timely and based on the best available evidence." The word "comprehensive" is not in that list. It comes from the Commissioner's Directions, below.

Section 57(1)(a) makes the Secretary of a Department the "principal official policy adviser to the Agency Minister". Where a portfolio also has agencies that are not Departments, their advice reaches the minister beside the department's. This is an illustration. NRIO is not a Department, so in its portfolio that role belongs to the portfolio department's Secretary. Grace's team told the portfolio department's policy area about each major piece of advice when it went, so the Minister never received two unexplained views.

The [Australian Public Service Commissioner's Directions 2022](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2022L00088/latest/text) (the Directions) say what upholding the Impartial value requires, having regard to each person's duties. Four of its elements govern advice:

- (a) "serving the Government of the day with high quality professional support, irrespective of which political party is in power";
- (d) providing "the best objective, non-partisan advice based on the best evidence available";
- (e) "providing advice that is relevant and comprehensive, is not affected by fear of consequences, and does not withhold important facts or bad news";
- (f) advice that "takes account of the context in which policy needs to be implemented".

A minister's advisers are part of the picture too. They are employed under the Members of Parliament (Staff) Act 1984. Even an APS employee on leave without pay to work in a minister's office under that Act is not bound by the Values of Committed to Service and Impartial while there (Directions s 17B); one placed there without that leave remains bound. An adviser's job includes the political side of a decision, and a good one tells you what the Minister needs. But the Code of Conduct requires you to follow lawful and reasonable directions given by "someone in the employee's Agency who has authority to give the direction" (s 13(5)). A request from the office to change NRIO's advice is not one of those. Take it to your manager, who decides how NRIO responds.

## Briefs that lead with the decision

A brief exists to get a decision or to tell the minister something they need to know. Its first lines should say which, and what is asked. A minister who reads only the top third should know what you recommend, what it costs and the one thing that could go wrong.

```figure
type: steps
title: What a decision brief puts first
caption: Agencies have their own templates. Whatever the headings, the order of importance is the same.
- Purpose | One sentence: the decision sought, and by when it is needed.
- Recommendation | Each one a decision the minister can agree or not agree, as written.
- Key points | The reason, the cost and the main risk or bad news, in three to five points.
- Options and views | What else was considered, and who disagrees.
- Background | Only what the minister needs to judge the points above.
- *Attachments | The analysis, for anyone who wants to check it.
```

This is an illustration. Grace's first brief went on Fri 13 Aug 2027, ten days after the commission. Its purpose and recommendation came first: agree that NRIO define the problem and test the evidence before advising on funding options, and note that an options paper would follow by the end of October. Two pages were enough. Sending a short brief in ten days, instead of a long one in six weeks, was part of being timely in the sense of s 10(5). The office needed to know at once what NRIO proposed to do.

The options paper of Fri 29 Oct 2027 had a covering brief that led the same way. It recommended that the Minister agree to develop C as a new policy proposal. Its key points gave C's cost (A$14.5 million over four years for 1,200 priority bridges) and its value (benefits worth 1.63 times its costs over 10 years). They also named the main alternative (grants to councils, B, with benefits 1.34 times its costs) and C's main risk: NRIO had never run such a service. One point dealt with D: a strengthening fund now would cost A$120 million, its benefits over 10 years were worth less than its costs, and without assessments it could not be targeted. The five options, at their strongest, were in the attachment (Chapter 3).

## Frank advice when the office prefers D

This is an illustration. On Tue 2 Nov 2027 the senior adviser rang Grace. The Minister liked the idea of a fund, D was a bigger announcement, and could NRIO recommend D, or at least put it first?

Grace listened and asked what the Minister wanted to achieve. The answer was visible action on regional bridges that councils and operators would notice. She said NRIO's advice would change if the evidence did, and that she would take the request to the Director, Strategy and Policy. She also offered a one-page note on D against C within the week. She made a file note of the call that afternoon. The Director cleared the note, and it went on Fri 5 Nov 2027. The exercise at the end of this chapter asks you to write it.

Frank advice is not the same as repeating the recommendation more firmly. This is an illustration. Grace's note did five things:

1. **It said the bad news plainly.** Over 10 years, D's benefits were worth less than its costs: a ratio of 0.80.
2. **It gave D its due.** Over 30 years, because strengthened bridges last, D's ratio was 1.38. Element (e) of the Directions requires advice that does not withhold important facts, and that includes facts that favour the option you do not recommend.
3. **It named the real weakness: sequencing.** Without assessments, nobody knows which bridges need strengthening. D's benefits assume the money reaches the right bridges, and a competitive fund picks the best bids, not the greatest needs.
4. **It compared like with like.** It did not set D's 30-year ratio beside C's 10-year ratio as if they measured the same thing.
5. **It offered a path to the Minister's objective.** C now, then a strengthening fund targeted on C's results. That is a larger story than C alone, and a sounder one than D alone.

```figure
type: balance
title: D now, or C first and D later
left: D now
right: C first, then a targeted D
tilt: right
- A$120m before the need is known | A$14.5m over four years, about one eighth of D's cost
- Benefits worth 0.80 of costs over 10 years | Benefits worth 1.63 times costs over 10 years
- 1.38 over 30 years, if it reaches the right bridges | A data register that shows which bridges need work
- Grants go to the strongest bids | Bridges chosen on assessed load and condition
caption: This is an illustration. The comparison behind Grace's note of Fri 5 Nov 2027. Each ratio is present-value benefits divided by present-value costs at NRIO's 7 per cent real discount rate.
```

Element (e) also says advice "is not affected by fear of consequences". That standard is easier to meet when it does not rest on one EL1 alone. Grace's advice went through her Director, and the Director could take it higher. Escalating pressure on advice through your own line is how the system is meant to work. It is not disloyalty to the office.

## Oral advice and records

This is an illustration. On Tue 9 Nov 2027 the Minister met the Director and Grace for 30 minutes, with the senior adviser present. The Minister asked two questions. "If I fund D now, what goes wrong?" Grace answered that the money would go to the strongest bids, not the bridges in most need. Some limited bridges might need only an assessment to have their limits eased, and some unsafe ones would stay unknown. "How soon would C show results?" The Director answered that, with funding from July 2028, the first assessments could start within months, once the panel was in place.

Grace wrote a file note that afternoon, the Director checked it, and it went into NRIO's records system the same day. Upholding the Stewardship value requires "complete, accurate, and appropriately accessible recordkeeping of key actions and decisions" (Directions s 17A(e)). Upholding the Accountable value includes "being able to demonstrate that actions and decisions have been made with appropriate consideration" (Directions s 16(c)). Oral advice leaves no trace unless someone writes one.

```figure
type: checklist
title: A file note of oral advice
caption: Write it the same day, keep it short and file it where colleagues can find it. Write it as if a stranger with a right to see it will read it years from now.
- Date, place and who was there | Including the adviser, by role
- What was asked | In the minister's words where they matter
- What advice was given | Including any figure quoted, and its source
- What was decided or requested | And who will act, by when
- Checked by a second person who was present | Then filed in the records system
- x Opinions about people | Record what was said and decided, not impressions
- x Leaving it in a notebook or inbox | A record nobody can find is no record
```

This is an illustration. On Thu 11 Nov 2027 the Minister signed the options paper's covering brief, agreeing to develop C as a new policy proposal. The signed brief is the record of that decision. Grace's file notes record the advice that came before it.

## Responsiveness without partisanship

Upholding the Committed to Service value requires "being responsive to Ministers", including understanding the Government's objectives and the environment it works in (Directions s 13(j)). Responsiveness is not a softer kind of advice. It means answering quickly, understanding what the minister is trying to achieve and helping to get there. This is an illustration. Grace did this when she asked what the Minister wanted and built a staged path that gave it.

Partisanship is different. It is shaping advice for party advantage: choosing the option with the better headline against the evidence, or comparing a proposal with another party's policy for political use. It also includes delaying advice until the timing suits a party. Advice that is "apolitical" (s 10(5)) and "non-partisan" (Directions s 17(d)) cannot do these things. If you are asked, say what you can provide and take the rest to your manager.

Once a decision is made, implement it well even if you advised otherwise. Element (g) of the Impartial value requires "implementing Government policies in a way that is free from bias, and in accordance with the law". This is an illustration. Had the Minister chosen D, NRIO's next advice would have been how to make D work: for example, a rule that a bridge needs a current load assessment before it can receive strengthening money.

## When a minister approves spending

Section 71(1) of the [Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text) (PGPA Act) says a Minister "must not approve a proposed expenditure of relevant money unless the Minister is satisfied, after making reasonable inquiries", that it would be a proper use of that money. In s 8, "proper" means "efficient, effective, economical and ethical". A Minister who approves must "record the terms of the approval in writing as soon as practicable after giving the approval" (s 71(2)(a)).

This is an illustration. Option C would not involve the Minister in approving individual payments. NRIO would buy assessments under its own authority, because its accountable authority may approve commitments of relevant money for the entity (s 23(3)), usually through delegations. Nor was the Minister's agreement of 11 Nov 2027 an approval of expenditure: it was an agreement to develop a proposal.

But if a later strengthening fund had the Minister choosing which bridges to fund, s 71 would apply to each approval. Then NRIO's brief should give the Minister what reasonable inquiries need: how each proposal was assessed, its cost and value, its risks and the alternatives, and a place to record the terms of approval. A brief that asks a minister to approve spending without that material leaves the minister unable to show the inquiries s 71 requires. If the spending is a grant, the Commonwealth's grants rules also apply; the companion book *Grants You Can Defend* covers them.

## When it goes wrong: the overrun in paragraph 11

This is an illustration. In early 2026, before the bridge work began, another NRIO branch was delivering a A$18.0 million program to rebuild 12 flood-damaged causeways on roads to remote communities. Tenders closed in February 2026. The lowest compliant prices came to A$24.3 million: A$6.3 million, or 35 per cent, above the estimate (A$6.3 million ÷ A$18.0 million = 0.35).

The branch's brief of Thu 12 Mar 2026 was headed "Causeway program: progress update". It asked the Minister to note it. Its first key point said the program remained on schedule. The tender result appeared in paragraph 11 of the background as "tendered prices exceed the estimate; options are being considered", with no figure and no options. The Minister noted the brief. Two weeks later, on a regional visit, the Minister said all 12 causeways would be rebuilt by the end of 2027.

In June 2026 the branch asked for A$6.3 million more. The Minister now had to choose between finding money elsewhere and cutting causeways that had been promised in public. The office asked why it had not been told in March. Three months in which the work could have been re-scoped, staged or funded were gone, and the office began asking for the figures behind every NRIO progress brief.

Every sentence in the brief was true. But it withheld the one fact the Minister needed, in a form the Minister could act on, and element (e) requires advice that "does not withhold important facts or bad news". Briefs are also one way an agency's accountable authority meets its duty to "keep the responsible Minister informed of the activities of the entity" (PGPA Act s 19(1)(a)).

Two controls would have caught it. First, the structure: bad news is the first key point, with a number and a decision. "Tendered prices are A$6.3 million (35 per cent) above the A$18.0 million budget. Before contracts are signed, you will need to decide whether to find more funds, stage the work or rebuild fewer causeways." Second, a clearance question for every brief: what is the worst thing in this brief, and is it on the first page?

## Exercise: one page on D against C

This is an illustration. It is Wed 3 Nov 2027. Write the note Grace sent on Fri 5 Nov: a purpose, a recommendation and no more than six key points, in plain words, on one page. Use these inputs from Chapter 4: NRIO's 7 per cent real discount rate; P/A(7%, 10) = 7.0236 and P/A(7%, 30) = 12.4090; D has an initial investment of A$120 million, an operating cost of A$0.5 million a year and benefits of A$14.0 million a year; C's results are PV benefits A$25.28 million and PV costs A$15.55 million. Show the working for D at 10 and 30 years.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. Check the numbers first, with the formula NRIO used (salvage is nil in both cases).

```figure
type: formula
title: Option D at 10 and 30 years, and C at 10 years
caption: This is an illustration. All amounts are A$ million in present values at NRIO's 7 per cent real discount rate.
formula: B/C = PV benefits ÷ PV costs
formula: PV benefits = B × P/A(i, n); PV costs = P + C × P/A(i, n)
- P | initial investment: 120 for Option D, 14.5 for Option C
- B | annual benefits: 14.0 for Option D, 3.6 for Option C
- C | annual operating cost: 0.5 for Option D, 0.15 for Option C
step: Option D, 10 years: 14.0 × 7.0236 = 98.33; 120 + 0.5 × 7.0236 = 123.51; 98.33 ÷ 123.51 = 0.80
step: Option D, 30 years: 14.0 × 12.4090 = 173.73; 120 + 0.5 × 12.4090 = 126.20; 173.73 ÷ 126.20 = 1.38
step: Option C, 10 years: 25.28 ÷ 15.55 = 1.63; D's initial cost is 120 ÷ 14.5 = 8.3 times C's
```

A note that meets the brief:

**Purpose.** To compare a strengthening fund (Option D) with a central assessment service (Option C), as your office asked, before you decide on the options paper of 29 October.

**Recommendation.** That you agree to develop Option C as a new policy proposal for the 2028–29 Budget, and note that a strengthening fund targeted on C's results could follow. Agreed / Not agreed / Please discuss.

**Key points.**

1. A strengthening fund now would commit A$120 million before anyone knows which bridges need strengthening. Of the 4,410 structures in councils' survey responses, 1,540 have had no load assessment in the last 10 years.
2. Over 10 years, D's benefits are worth less than its costs: A$98.3 million against A$123.5 million, a ratio of 0.80.
3. Over 30 years, because strengthened bridges last, D's ratio rises to 1.38. That result depends on the money reaching the bridges that need it, which only assessments can show.
4. C assesses 1,200 priority bridges for A$14.5 million over four years, about one eighth of D's A$120 million. Its benefits are worth 1.63 times its costs over 10 years, and 1.14 times if benefits are 30 per cent lower.
5. C's results go into a shared bridge data register: the list a later strengthening fund would need to choose bridges on evidence.
6. Assessment first, then strengthening targeted on the results, can be described as a single staged approach now.

Why it works:

- The first lines carry the decision, and the bad news on D fills the first two key points, with numbers.
- The 30-year ratio is there, because it is true and favours D, and point 3 explains why it does not change the advice.
- The ratios compared directly, 0.80 and 1.63, are both over 10 years.
- Point 6 answers what the Minister wants, visible action, without bending the analysis.
- Every figure matches the options paper, so the office cannot find two versions of the truth.

Once the Minister had agreed and the Budget had decided, the work became delivery. Chapter 9 turns to planning implementation.
