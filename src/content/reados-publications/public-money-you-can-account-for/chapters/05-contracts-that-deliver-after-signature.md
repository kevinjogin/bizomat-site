An evaluation predicts value for money. A contract delivers it only if someone manages the work, checks it, pays for it on time and changes it properly when the need changes. In a services contract the money is spent after the signature, through small decisions made by people who never sat on the evaluation panel.

Those decisions are where public money leaks. A site visit turns into extra work that nobody approved. An invoice is paid because it looks familiar. An option is taken because the date arrived, not because anyone tested its value.

## The contract in front of Lena

This is an illustration. NRIO has signed with Ridgeway Maintenance for regional facilities maintenance at its 46 sites. The term is three years plus two one-year options. Ridgeway is paid a fixed fee of A$4,000 per site per year and A$375 per call-out, and the total contract value is A$1,240,000 including GST if both options are taken. The Commonwealth Procurement Rules state their thresholds and values inclusive of GST, and every amount below follows that convention. The Branch Manager, the only official in the branch whose delegation covers that value, approved the commitment. Tom, the contracts officer, is the contract manager. Lena is accountable for how the branch runs the contract.

Section 23 of the PGPA Act gives the accountable authority of a non-corporate Commonwealth entity the power to enter into, vary and administer arrangements, and to approve commitments of relevant money. Those powers usually reach officials through a written delegation under section 110. Entering into the contract took a day; varying and administering it takes five years.

## Write a contract management plan

A contract management plan turns the contract into a working routine. The contract says what Ridgeway must do. The plan says how NRIO will know whether it has, who does what, and what happens when something changes. Check whether your agency's instructions require a plan and in what form.

The CPRs require officials to maintain for each procurement "a level of documentation commensurate with the scale, scope and risk of the procurement". Apply the same sense to the plan. A five-year contract like Ridgeway's needs more than a page, but not a binder that nobody opens. Tom's plan for Ridgeway covers:

- the obligations on both sides, with clause numbers;
- each service level and how it is measured;
- how work is ordered, and who may instruct the contractor, by name;
- how delivery is accepted and invoices are checked;
- the meeting rhythm and what each meeting records;
- the contract's risks, linked to the branch risk register;
- how variations are priced, approved and reported;
- the option dates and the evidence each decision needs;
- the records to keep, and how the contract will end.

Review the plan whenever something material changes: a variation, a new site or a new contract manager.

## Name who does what

Contracts go wrong at the edges between roles, so write down what each person does and does not do.

| Role | Who at NRIO | Does | Does not |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contract manager | Tom | Single point of contact; issues written work orders; keeps records; prepares variations | Approve variations |
| Site coordinators | One in each region | Report faults; confirm work done at their sites | Ask the contractor directly for work |
| Business manager | Lena | Oversees the contract, budget and commitments; reports and escalates | Approve beyond her delegation |
| Program owner | The Facilities Director | Sets the service the program needs; chairs the quarterly review | Manage the contract day to day |
| Delegate | The Branch Manager | Approves the contract and changes to its value | Approve without the cost and the reason |
| Finance | Arun | Pays correctly rendered invoices after acceptance | Pay before delivery is acknowledged |

The table keeps the separation of duties from Chapter 3. The person who orders work is not the person who confirms it was done, and neither of them pays. Short staffing puts pressure on that separation, so name a backup for each role.

## Accept the work, then pay on time

The CPRs require non-corporate Commonwealth entities to "make all payments to suppliers within the maximum payment terms", after acknowledging satisfactory delivery and receiving a correctly rendered invoice. Both halves matter. NRIO does not pay for work it has not accepted. Once it has accepted the work and holds a correct invoice, it must not sit on the payment. Ask your finance area for the current maximum payment terms and how the period is counted, and read what your contract says a correctly rendered invoice must contain.

Acceptance is a decision based on evidence. For Ridgeway, the evidence is the work order or scheduled task, the job sheet with its date, times and photos, and the site coordinator's confirmation that the work was done to standard. Tom checks the invoice against the contract's rates, and Arun pays. When one line is wrong, tell Ridgeway promptly and in writing, and ask your finance area how your agency handles a partly disputed invoice.

## Run performance meetings that leave a record

Tom meets Ridgeway's account manager every month to go through open work orders, late jobs, safety incidents and invoice problems. Every quarter the Facilities Director chairs a review with Ridgeway's regional manager to look at the trend against the service levels and agree actions.

Each meeting produces a short record: who attended, which numbers were examined, what was agreed, who will act and by when. Keep it in NRIO's records system, not in an inbox. It lets a later option decision or dispute rest on facts rather than memory.

This is an illustration. Ridgeway's contract sets a fictional service level: at least 95% of urgent work orders attended within the contract's response time in each quarter. Year one looked like this.

The review at the end of month 9 heard why: technicians could not reach remote sites when roads flooded, and Ridgeway had no local subcontractors arranged. When performance slips, raise it early and in writing, and work from what the contract itself provides, with advice from central procurement or legal before any formal step.

## Change the contract before the work changes

A variation is how the contract follows a changing need. It is also where a well-run procurement can be quietly undone, so it deserves the same care as the original decision.

This is an illustration. In month 10, two community buildings are transferred into NRIO's care. The Facilities Director wants them under the Ridgeway contract from month 13 for the four years that remain if both options are taken. The change is priced from the contract's own rates, with a planning figure of 16 extra call-outs over the four years.

- Fixed fee: 2 sites × A$4,000 × 4 years = A$32,000
- Call-outs: 16 × A$375 = A$6,000
- Variation: A$32,000 + A$6,000 = **A$38,000**
- Revised contract value if both options are taken: A$1,240,000 + A$38,000 = **A$1,278,000**
- Increase: A$38,000 ÷ A$1,240,000 = 3.1% (rounded to one decimal place)

Before anything is signed, Tom works through four questions.

**Is it within what was bought?** Adding two similar sites may or may not be within what the approach to market and the contract allowed. Grace checks the tender documents and the variation clause with central procurement. The answer depends on the documents, not on the size of the change.

**Who can approve it?** NRIO's Accountable Authority Instructions, like everything about NRIO, are fictional. They say a variation that changes value is approved by an official whose delegation covers the revised total contract value, not just the increase. Lena's A$250,000 covers A$38,000 but not A$1,278,000, and neither does the Facilities Director's A$500,000. The Branch Manager approves. Other agencies write this rule differently.

**Is the approval recorded properly?** Section 18 of the PGPA Rule requires the official approving a commitment of relevant money to "record the approval in writing as soon as practicable after giving it". The guide to that section reminds officials that sections 25 and 26 of the PGPA Act require care and diligence, and a proper purpose, when approving. Tom's brief gives the Branch Manager what that care needs: the reason, the checked price build-up, procurement's advice on scope, the effect on the budget forecast and the cumulative change in value.

**What must be reported?** Under the [Commonwealth Procurement Rules](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2025L01263/latest/text), an amendment must be reported on AusTender within 42 days when it changes the reported value by A$10,000 or more. This one changes it by A$38,000, so Tom, who reported the original contract, prepares the amendment report with central procurement as soon as the variation is signed. Small changes are caught too: once accumulated unreported amendments reach A$10,000, they must be reported.

Tom adds one line to the brief. Half of the variation sits in the option years. At 4 call-outs a year, years 2 and 3 cost (2 × A$4,000 × 2) + (8 × A$375) = A$19,000, and years 4 and 5 cost the same again, but only if NRIO takes the options. The Branch Manager should see both halves now, not at the option decision.

## When it goes wrong: the verbal request

This is an illustration. A Ridgeway technician is at a remote depot for a scheduled service. The site coordinator asks whether, while they are there, they could repaint the amenities block and replace the rusted gutters. Ridgeway's office agrees by phone. Three weeks later an invoice for A$14,600 arrives with no work order behind it.

Everyone acted in good faith, and the work was useful. It was still wrong in four ways. Nobody with a delegation approved a commitment, so there is no approval to record under section 18 of the PGPA Rule. The work may sit outside what NRIO procured. The A$14,600 was in no forecast, so the branch's commitments were understated for three weeks. And Ridgeway holds a claim built on an instruction from someone the contract never authorised. Section 23 of the PGPA Act says an arrangement includes an "understanding", and a friendly request on site can look very like one to a contractor who acts on it.

What should have happened is simple. The coordinator reports the defects to Tom, who checks whether the work is within the contract. If it is, he prices it from the contract's rates and issues a written work order. If it is not, it becomes a proposed variation, approved by the right delegate before any work starts and, at A$14,600, reported on AusTender within 42 days.

Now that it has happened, do not fix it quietly. Establish the facts in writing: who asked, what was done and what it is worth. Tell Lena and the delegate. Ask your finance and legal areas how your agency deals with a commitment made without approval, and record the outcome. Then fix the control: Tom writes to Ridgeway confirming that only written work orders from named NRIO officers are instructions under the contract, and briefs every site coordinator. ## Decide options on evidence

An option gives NRIO a right to extend, not a duty. Treat each one as a fresh value-for-money judgement. Is the service still needed in this form? Has Ridgeway performed, on the record, across every site and season? Are its prices still good value? Does the budget carry the option year?

This is an illustration. The contract's fictional terms require notice of the first option at least three months before the initial term ends, so by the end of month 33. Tom's plan works backwards: evidence gathered from month 27, a decision brief to the Branch Manager by month 30, and a decision in month 31. Check whether the original approval covered the option years or only the initial term; if only the initial term, the option needs its own approval, recorded in writing. If taking the option changes the value reported on AusTender by A$10,000 or more, the 42-day rule applies, so ask your procurement team how the contract was first reported.

## Disputes and close-out

Disputes are easier to prevent than to win. Many start as a difference about scope or quality that nobody wrote down. When a disagreement forms, follow the contract's dispute clause step by step, record every exchange and involve your legal area before positions harden.

Close-out starts months before the last day. The CPRs list transition-out costs among whole-of-life costs, and they are real: keys, site information, asset records and open work orders all have to move to the next contractor or back to NRIO. Tom's close-out list covers final acceptance and invoice, any security handled as the contract says, records handed over, the asset register updated, the final value reconciled with AusTender, and a lessons note for the next procurement.

## Exercise: four changes to the Ridgeway contract

This is an illustration. It is the start of month 13. After the two-site variation, which has been approved and reported, the contract value is A$1,278,000 if both options are taken. Use NRIO's fictional rules: a variation that changes value is approved by an official whose delegation covers the revised total; a change that alters no price, scope or risk may be approved by Lena. Assume Grace has confirmed that each item is within scope.

Take the changes in order. For each, decide who approves it and whether it must be reported on AusTender.

1. Add quarterly gutter cleaning at 10 remote sites for the 24 months left in the initial term, at A$180 per site per visit.
2. Move Ridgeway's monthly invoice date from the 1st to the 15th.
3. Add a one-off storm-damage allowance of A$6,000.
4. A month later, add a one-off switchboard inspection at the two new sites, at A$2,500 each.

### Worked answer

| Change | Value change | Revised value | Approver | AusTender |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| 1. Gutter cleaning | 10 × 8 × A$180 = A$14,400 | A$1,292,400 | Branch Manager | Report within 42 days |
| 2. Invoice date | A$0 | A$1,292,400 | Lena | Not reportable |
| 3. Storm allowance | A$6,000 | A$1,298,400 | Branch Manager | Not yet |
| 4. Switchboard inspections | 2 × A$2,500 = A$5,000 | A$1,303,400 | Branch Manager | Report items 3 and 4 |

Change 1: 24 months is eight quarters, so 10 sites × 8 visits × A$180 = A$14,400, which is A$10,000 or more. The revised total, A$1,278,000 + A$14,400 = A$1,292,400, is above both Lena's and the Facilities Director's limits, so the Branch Manager approves. Under NRIO's rule the size of the contract, not the size of the change, decides the approver.

Change 2 alters no price, scope or risk, so Lena may approve it and nothing goes to AusTender. It still needs a written record, and Arun needs to know so that payment due dates are right.

Changes 3 and 4 show why Tom keeps a running total of unreported amendments. Neither reaches A$10,000 alone, but together they make A$6,000 + A$5,000 = A$11,000, so both are reported within 42 days; ask your procurement team how the period is dated. Each approval is recorded in writing as soon as practicable.

The cumulative change is A$1,303,400 − A$1,240,000 = A$63,400, and A$63,400 ÷ A$1,240,000 = 5.1% of the original value (rounded to one decimal place). Tom shows that figure on every variation brief, so the Branch Manager approves each change knowing where the whole contract stands.
