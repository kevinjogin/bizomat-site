Every business owner faces setbacks: a lost customer, a failed product, a cash crisis, a key employee resigning, a bad year. What separates owners who recover from those who stall is often not the size of the setback but how they respond to it. Their attitude, meaning their habitual way of interpreting and reacting to events, shapes the decisions they make next.

Attitude matters more as responsibility grows. A skilled tradesperson succeeds largely through technical skill. A business owner or senior leader relies far more on judgement, persistence, how they treat people and how they handle uncertainty. Technical skill still matters, but attitude increasingly determines results.

This article explains how attitudes form as patterns, offers a three-step method for replacing unhelpful patterns, draws lessons on bouncing back from setbacks and suggests how to sustain energy over a long career. It also notes when stress needs more than self-help.

## Attitudes are patterns

Attitudes grow from repeated patterns of thought and desire. Faced with a particular kind of situation, such as criticism, a difficult customer or a missed target, most people react in a consistent way, and repeat that reaction whenever a similar situation arises. Over time, the pattern feels automatic and becomes part of how they see themselves.

Some patterns help, such as treating problems as puzzles to solve. Others hold people back:

- Assuming the worst: "this customer will never pay".
- Blaming others: "my staff are useless".
- Catastrophising: "if this order is late, the business is finished".
- Giving up early: "I tried marketing once and it didn't work".
- Avoiding discomfort: putting off difficult conversations indefinitely.

Because these patterns are learned, they can be unlearned. People are not stuck with the attitudes they have. They can choose and cultivate new ones.

## Develop a "higher taste"

Indian philosophy offers a helpful idea. The Bhagavad Gita observes that people turn away from lower pleasures most reliably when they experience a higher one. Simply suppressing an unhelpful habit rarely works for long. Replacing it with something more rewarding does.

Patanjali's Yoga Sutras offer a related technique: when negative thoughts obstruct you, deliberately cultivate their opposite. Modern psychology describes similar approaches. Cognitive behavioural methods, for example, teach people to notice automatic thoughts, test them against evidence and replace distorted ones with more balanced alternatives.

The practical message: rather than staying trapped in a circle of difficulty, deliberately build a circle of possibility. When a negative situation arises, look for new options, new choices and new alternatives.

## Three steps to replace an unhelpful pattern

### Step 1: See the existing pattern

Notice how you react in particular situations. Keep a simple log for a few weeks:

| Situation | What I thought | What I felt | What I did | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer complained about a delay | "They're always difficult" | Irritated | Replied defensively by email | Customer escalated to their manager |

Patterns become visible quickly. You may find that you always respond to complaints defensively, avoid chasing overdue invoices or assume new staff will fail.

### Step 2: Decide to dissolve it

Change begins with a decision. Accept that the pattern exists, that it is not serving you and that you are willing to change it. This sounds simple, but many people resist acknowledging their own patterns because doing so feels like admitting fault. It is not a fault. It is a habit, and habits can change.

### Step 3: Replace and reinforce a new pattern

Choose a specific new response and practise it deliberately:

- Old pattern: replying defensively to complaints. New pattern: thank the customer, acknowledge the problem and ask a question before proposing a solution.
- Old pattern: avoiding overdue invoices. New pattern: call every overdue account on Monday morning.
- Old pattern: assuming new staff will fail. New pattern: set clear expectations and check progress after one week.

Then reinforce it. Repeat the new response consciously until it becomes natural, and notice the better results it brings. Those results become the "higher taste" that makes the old pattern less attractive.

### Useful reframing questions

When an unhelpful thought appears, ask:

- What evidence supports this thought, and what evidence contradicts it?
- What would I tell a friend who was thinking this?
- What is the most likely outcome, rather than the worst?
- What is one thing I can do about it now?
- What could I learn from this?

## Lessons on bouncing back

An Indian actor who became well known in his forties illustrates resilience. His father died before he was born, and as a child he struggled at school with dyslexia, while excelling in theatre, debating and music with his mother's encouragement.

As a young man, he worked as a waiter in a luxury hotel. When he asked about becoming a manager, he was advised to start in room service, because reaching the top requires learning the basics first. He then spent around fourteen years working in his family's small snack shop. Wanting to take his family on holiday, he used his savings from tips to start photographing school sports events, and earned enough for a trip. The hotel they could afford turned out to be dismal, and the experience convinced him that he needed to aim higher.

He developed his photography until he was photographing international sporting events, where the quality of his work earned him several times the fee he had expected. He later trained in theatre, and his first major film role came when a director saw his stage work and approached him, rather than the other way round.

### Lessons from his journey

- **Identify your signature strength**, the thing you do unusually well and enjoy, even if it is not what others expect of you.
- **Learn the basics first.** Expertise at the top is built on fundamentals.
- **Think bigger.** Open your mind to what the next level could be.
- **Be happily dissatisfied with your last success.** Enjoy it, then aim higher.
- **Do honest, high-quality work.** Quality is noticed and rewarded, often unexpectedly.
- **Let opportunities find you.** People who are ethical, dedicated and genuinely good at what they do are sought out.

## Think like the person you want to become

If you want to be an exceptional business owner, study how exceptional business owners think:

- How do they approach problems?
- How do they build their businesses and teams?
- How do they win and keep customers?
- How do they develop products?
- How do they handle setbacks?

Read their accounts, listen to their interviews and, where possible, talk to them. Then ask, when facing a decision, "How would an excellent owner approach this?" and act accordingly.

## Sustaining energy over a long career

Owners need energy for decades, not months. Practices that help:

- **Remain a student.** Keep learning every day, absorb what others teach you and learn from the people around you.
- **Apply what you learn.** Application creates energy, because you are always looking forward to trying something new.
- **Choose work you enjoy.** Turning a hobby or passion into work that others pay for sustains motivation.
- **Make small, steady improvements.** The Japanese principle of kaizen, continuous improvement in small steps, makes change manageable. Aim for 1 per cent improvements rather than dramatic overhauls that are hard to sustain.
- **Look after your health.** Sleep, exercise and time away from the business are not luxuries. They are the foundation of good judgement.
- **Keep supportive people around you.** Mentors, peers and family who encourage you and tell you the truth.

## Resilience after business setbacks

When a significant setback hits the business, a structured response helps:

1. **Pause before reacting.** Take time to understand what happened before making big decisions.
2. **Separate facts from interpretations.** "We lost our largest customer" is a fact. "The business is finished" is an interpretation.
3. **Look for what you can control.** Focus energy on actions within your influence.
4. **Talk to someone.** A mentor, adviser, accountant or peer can offer perspective and practical ideas.
5. **Act on one thing.** Momentum restores confidence. Take one constructive step today.
6. **Extract the lesson.** Once the immediate crisis has passed, ask what you would do differently and what you will change.

## Leaders' attitudes spread

An owner's attitude spreads through the business. Teams notice whether the owner responds to problems with blame or curiosity, panic or calm, avoidance or action. A leader who models constructive responses to setbacks gives the team permission to do the same, and makes it safer for people to report problems early.

## When to seek support

Resilience does not mean coping alone. Running a business can be isolating and stressful, and financial pressure, long hours and uncertainty take a real toll. If low mood, anxiety, sleeplessness or exhaustion persist for weeks, or begin to affect your health, relationships or ability to work, speak to your doctor. In Australia, services such as Beyond Blue and Lifeline offer support, and some programs are designed specifically for small business owners. Seeking help is a sign of good judgement, not weakness.

## Common mistakes

- **Forcing positivity.** Pretending everything is fine ignores real problems. Constructive attitudes acknowledge difficulties honestly and then look for options.
- **Trying to change every pattern at once.** Work on one pattern at a time until the new response becomes natural.
- **Treating setbacks as verdicts on your worth.** A failed product or a lost customer is an event, not a judgement on you as a person.
- **Isolating yourself.** Many owners withdraw under pressure, exactly when perspective from others would help most.
- **Neglecting recovery.** Working longer hours through every crisis erodes the judgement you need to solve it.
- **Making permanent decisions in temporary emotions.** Selling, closing or firing in the heat of a bad week often leads to regret.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is attitude something people are born with?** Temperament varies between people, but attitudes are strongly shaped by habit and experience. With deliberate practice, most people can change how they typically respond to difficulties.

**How long does it take to change a pattern?** It varies with the person and the pattern. Expect weeks to months of conscious practice before a new response feels automatic. Tracking it in a log helps you see progress that might otherwise go unnoticed.

**How can I help a team member with a negative attitude?** Start with curiosity rather than criticism: ask what is behind it. Sometimes negativity reflects real problems that need fixing, such as workload, unclear expectations or poor tools. Where it is a habit, give specific feedback on behaviour, model the response you want and recognise improvement.

## A worked example

The owner of a small joinery business loses a contract with a builder who supplied 40 per cent of the business's work. His first reaction is despair and anger: he blames the builder, snaps at staff and stops returning calls.

After a week, a mentor helps him reflect. Using a simple log, he notices a pattern: when things go wrong, he withdraws and assumes the worst. He decides to change. He separates facts (revenue will fall by 40 per cent for at least six months) from interpretations (the business is finished). He lists what he can control: costs, marketing, relationships with other builders and the quality of work in progress.

He takes one action per day: calling past customers, meeting two new builders, reviewing costs with his accountant and asking his team for ideas. One team member suggests offering kitchen refurbishment directly to homeowners, a market the business had never pursued.

Six months later, revenue has recovered to 85 per cent of its previous level, with a more diverse customer base. More importantly, he has a new pattern for responding to setbacks, which he discusses openly with his team.

## Summary

Attitudes are learned patterns, and they can be changed. Notice your existing patterns, decide to dissolve the unhelpful ones and replace them with specific new responses, reinforced until they become natural. Develop a "higher taste" by experiencing the better results of new patterns. Bounce back from setbacks by learning the basics, thinking bigger, doing honest, high-quality work and staying happily dissatisfied with past success. Study how excellent owners think, keep learning, improve in small steps and look after your health. When setbacks strike, pause, separate facts from interpretations and act on what you can control. And when stress persists, seek support.
