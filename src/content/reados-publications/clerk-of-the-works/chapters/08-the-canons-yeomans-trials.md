Late in the Canterbury Tales, two riders catch up with the pilgrims: a canon and his yeoman, his servant. The canon soon rides off in embarrassment, and the yeoman, freed to talk, explains what they do. For seven years he has served the canon in alchemy, trying to turn base metal into silver and gold. He describes the equipment, the materials and the endless work at the fire. Every attempt has failed. Once the pot cracks and the metal is lost; the assistants argue about whether the fire was too hot, the bellows wrongly worked or the mixture badly made; they sweep up what they can and resolve to try again. The yeoman has lost his money and his complexion to the work, and still, he admits, he hopes the next attempt will succeed.

His account is the best description in English of a project that will not converge: each failure has a new explanation, each explanation justifies another attempt, and nobody asks whether the attempts are worth what they cost. This chapter applies it to a trial on the grandstand, then closes the project: completion, delay, retention and the records the society keeps.

## The heritage trial

This is an illustration. The old grandstand's seating was made of hardwood boards that many members had sat on for sixty years. The committee wanted to reuse them as cladding on the new stand's rear wall, about 120 m², rather than use new fibre-cement panels. New cladding was in the contract at A$152 per m², or A$18,240 excluding GST. The committee's estimate for reuse was A$88 per m², A$10,560, to de-nail, mill, treat and coat the boards: a saving of A$7,680, as well as the heritage value. The committee approved a trial budget of A$6,000, separate from the project contingency, to prove that the boards could be made fit for exterior use.

The trial was three batches, each a sample panel of reclaimed boards put up on a test frame and left in the weather.

```figure
caption: This is an illustration. Each failure had a different explanation, and each explanation justified another trial. The approved trial budget was exceeded during the second batch.
- Week 6 | batch 1, A$3,200 | boards split along old nail holes within two weeks; put down to milling too thin
- Week 10 | batch 2, A$2,900 | thicker boards cupped in the sun; put down to drying too fast after treatment
- * Week 15 | batch 3, A$3,400 | coating peeled at the end grain; put down to the wrong primer
- Week 18 | batch 4 proposed, A$3,300 | a different primer and a longer drying time
```

Three batches had cost A$9,500 against an approved A$6,000. A fourth was proposed at A$3,300, with a new primer and a longer drying period. The trial's champion on the committee was confident: each problem had been understood and fixed, and only the coating remained.

## The yeoman's pattern

The Canon's Yeoman describes the same rhythm. A failure; an argument about its cause; a confident fix; a new attempt; a new failure with a different cause. What never happens is a step back to ask whether the method can work at all, and whether it is worth what it is costing. His canon, he says, always believed they were nearly there.

```figure
type: cycle
title: The trial that does not converge
caption: Each turn of the cycle feels like progress because a cause is found and fixed. What the cycle never contains is a decision about whether to keep turning it.
- Attempt | new batch on the test frame
- Failure | a defect appears
- Explanation | a cause is found, different from last time
- * Fix | a confident change to the method
- Renewed hope | the next attempt should work
```

There are honest reasons for trials to fail more than once, and persistence is often what makes engineering work. The difference between persistence and the yeoman's trap lies in the questions asked before the next attempt: is each failure a new cause, or the same weakness showing up in different places; and is the expected value of the next attempt positive on its own terms?

Applied to the boards, the first question gave a clear answer. The joiner looked at the three failed panels together rather than one at a time. Splitting at nail holes, cupping in the sun and coating failure at the end grain are three symptoms of one condition: sixty years of weathering had opened the grain and left the boards unstable in moisture. Each fix had treated one symptom. None could treat the cause, because the cause was the material itself. Three different explanations for three failures had looked like learning. Read together, they were one explanation that nobody had wanted to accept.

## Deciding on future cost alone

The A$9,500 already spent is gone whatever the committee decides. It cannot be recovered by trying again, and it should play no part in the decision. The only question is whether the next A$3,300 is likely to return more than it costs.

The clerk of works asked the trial's supervisor, a joiner experienced with reclaimed timber, for an honest estimate of the chance that the fourth batch would succeed and hold up. The answer was about one in four: the boards had already shown three different weaknesses, and old hardwood with nail holes and decades of weathering was proving unreliable outside.

```figure
type: formula
title: Expected value of a fourth trial
formula: EV = p × saving − cost of trial
caption: This is an illustration. The sunk A$9,500 does not appear: it is spent whatever the committee decides. The fourth trial would need better than a 43% chance of success to be worth funding.
- p | chance the fourth batch succeeds and holds up
- saving | A$7,680, the cost of new cladding less the cost of reuse
- cost of trial | A$3,300
step: EV = 0.25 × 7,680 − 3,300 = 1,920 − 3,300 = −A$1,380
step: break-even p = 3,300 ÷ 7,680 = 0.43
step: even with certain success, the whole idea returns 7,680 − 9,500 − 3,300 = −A$5,120 on the money spent
```

The fourth trial was worth −A$1,380 on the best available estimate. It would have needed a better than 43% chance of success to be worth funding, and nobody close to the boards believed it had one. The third step in the formula is a separate point, useful for the lessons-learned record but not for the decision: even a successful fourth batch would leave the idea as a whole A$5,120 behind, because so much had already gone on trials. That sunk cost was a reason to set stop rules next time, not a reason to continue or to stop now.

## Failure: no rule for stopping

The trial's real failure was not the boards. It was that the committee approved a budget but no stop rule. The A$6,000 budget was passed during batch 2, but nothing required anyone to come back to the committee, so batch 3 went ahead on the champion's say-so. The yeoman's canon had no stop rule either.

Detection was the clerk of works noticing, while preparing the week 15 report, that the trial had spent 158% of its approval. The correction, adopted for any future trial on a society project, set the rules before the first attempt:

1. A trial has a budget and a maximum number of attempts, both approved before it starts.
2. Before each attempt after the first, the person running it states the chance of success and what the attempt will learn that the last one did not.
3. If the budget or the number of attempts is reached, the trial stops and returns to the committee with a recommendation.
4. The decision to continue is made on the cost and value of the next attempt only.

The committee declined the fourth batch. New fibre-cement cladding went on as contracted. Twelve of the best reclaimed boards were made into a memorial panel in the stand's entrance, carrying the names of the society's past presidents, for A$1,200. The heritage value was kept at a fraction of the cost.

## Closing the project

Chaucer's term as clerk of the works ended in June 1391, and what remained of it was an enrolled account at the Exchequer: what he had received, what he had spent and what he had been forgiven. A modern clerk of works also finishes with an account, and with the records that will answer questions about the building for years.

This is an illustration. The builder reached practical completion at the end of week 28, two weeks after the contract date of week 26, as the week 14 forecast had predicted. The superintendent granted an extension of time of four working days for the unrecorded water main found in week 9, a latent condition the contract put at the society's risk. The remaining six working days of delay, mostly from the steel rework, were the builder's, and liquidated damages of 6 × A$1,500 = A$9,000 were deducted from the final payment.

The contingency register from Chapter 2 grew to five entries: the water main (A$14,600), the deeper footing at grid C (A$9,800), the certifier's fire engineering review (A$6,200), weather protection for roofing during a wet fortnight (A$8,400) and a new circuit to keep the pavilion supplied after the old switchboard was replaced (A$14,300). Every entry had a cause, a date and two approvals. The unused A$115,833 went back to the society's reserves, and the grant body received a final report that matched the register line by line.

## What practical completion meant

The contract's definition of practical completion was one of the clauses the clerk of works had tightened in Chapter 2. As drafted, it said the works were complete when "ready for use". As signed, it listed the deliverables: the occupation certificate issued by the certifier, the water tests of the roof witnessed and passed, the seats installed and every fixing recorded, the kiosk services commissioned, and the as-built drawings and certificates handed over. Minor defects that did not stop the stand being used could be listed and fixed afterwards.

In week 27 the builder asked for practical completion on the grounds that the stand was physically finished. The clerk of works walked the list with the superintendent. The occupation certificate had not yet been issued, because the certifier was waiting for the final fire certificate for the kiosk, and the roof water test had not been witnessed on the two front bays. Under the drafted clause, those gaps would have been an argument. Under the signed clause, they were two items on a list. Both were closed in the following week, and the superintendent certified practical completion at the end of week 28.

The difference between the two dates mattered. Each working day between the contract date and practical completion was a day of possible liquidated damages, and the builder's claim for week 27 would have stopped the clock a week earlier. A definition written as a list of evidence decided the question in an afternoon.

## Writing the lessons down

Chaucer's yeoman tells his story so that others will not repeat his mistakes. The clerk of works' final report to the committee did the same in a page, one line per chapter of the job:

| Event | What it cost or saved | What the society will do next time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payment redirection email, week 5 | A$141,075 not lost | keep the call-back and two-signature rules |
| Constant risers on the concept drawings | rows 9 to 12 failed the sightline; fixed before construction | appoint the clerk of works before tender |
| Revision C not sent to the fabricator | A$11,100 of rework, borne by the builder | keep the hold point before galvanising; check revisions first |
| Steel progress claimed at 90% | about A$62,000 paid ahead of value, recovered | agree milestone rules at start-up and use them for every certificate |
| Heritage trial without a stop rule | A$3,500 over its approval | set budget, attempts and a stop rule before any trial |

The table is short because each line rests on the registers and records behind it. A lessons-learned report that cannot point to its evidence is a set of opinions; this one can be checked against the diary, the contingency register and the inspection records, line by line.

## Retention, defects and records

Retention was held at 5% of the head contract's final value. The head contract finished at A$1,052,800 excluding GST: the original A$1,020,000 plus three variations, for the water main, the footing and the roofing protection. Retention totalled A$52,640. Under the contract, half, A$26,320, was released at practical completion and the other half is held until the end of the 12-month defects liability period, when the clerk of works inspects the stand again with the builder and lists anything that has failed.

The clerk of works' last task was the handover of records to the society:

The building will stand for decades. The records are what will tell the society, in five or ten years, what is behind the cladding, which revision the steel was built to, why a footing is deeper at one grid line and what was promised about the seats. Like Chaucer's enrolled accounts, they will outlast everyone who made them.

## Exercise: decide a second trial

A year later the society proposes reusing the same reclaimed boards inside the kiosk, as a counter front of 8 m². New laminate would cost A$210 per m²; reuse would cost A$140 per m². The joiner estimates the chance of a first trial panel succeeding indoors at 70%, and the trial would cost A$450. Using the stop rules, decide whether to fund the trial, state the break-even chance, and say what the trial approval should record.

**Worked answer.** The saving if reuse works is 8 × (210 − 140) = A$560. The expected value of the trial is 0.70 × 560 − 450 = 392 − 450 = −A$58, slightly negative. The break-even chance is 450 ÷ 560 = 0.80, so the trial would need an 80% chance of success to pay on money alone. On these figures it should not be funded as a cost-saving measure. The committee may still choose it for heritage value, which is a legitimate reason, but then it should say so: approve it as a heritage feature with a fixed budget of A$450 and one attempt, record that the expected saving does not justify it, and agree in advance that if the panel fails the counter will be laminate. The stop rules exist to make that kind of decision explicit, not to forbid it.
