## About this book

Many welders dream of their own shop, and many small fabrication businesses outgrow the corner of a shed where they started. Setting up a shop is a chain of decisions that are easy to get wrong and expensive to change: how much floor to rent, where the bays go, whether the power supply will run the machines, how the gas will be supplied, how the fume will be removed, what equipment to buy first, how to keep the place from burning down, and what to charge so that the business survives its first year.

This book is for welders and fabricators setting up their own shop, and for owners and managers planning a new welding bay or reorganising an existing one. It assumes a working knowledge of welding and simple arithmetic. It puts numbers on each decision, so that the shop is sized for its work and its prices cover its costs.

## Early manuals as a starting point

In the years after 1910, oxy-acetylene welding opened a new kind of small business: the custom repair welding shop, mending broken castings, cracked cylinders and worn parts for garages, farmers and factories. The makers of welding equipment and their writers knew their customers were often new to business. The Imperial Brass Manufacturing Company's handbook of 1916 warned new owners not to solicit work before they had learned to weld. P. F. Willis's *Practical Manual* of 1917 listed the tools a repair shop needed and told owners that few custom welding shops properly accounted for overhead. M. Keith Dunham's *Automobile Welding* of 1917 advised on where to put the welding outfit, how to build a welding table and how to keep a cost card for every job.

Their advice about overhead, cost cards and practice before taking customers' money is as sound now as then. Each chapter takes up one of their themes and works it through for a modern shop.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A qualified welder with fifteen years' experience in a fabrication shop plans to set up her own business with one employee, doing jobbing fabrication and repair: trailers, gates, brackets, machinery repairs, site work for farms and local industry, and small structural jobs. She has found a rented industrial unit and savings to equip it, and wants to plan the shop, its services, its equipment and its prices before signing the lease.

The business, its building, costs and prices are invented for teaching. They are not KEVOS® work. Real shops must meet planning, building, electrical, gas, fire and work health and safety requirements, and the advice of licensed electricians, gas suppliers, accountants and insurers governs.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 decides what work the shop will do. Chapter 2 sizes the floor and lays out the bays. Chapters 3 and 4 size the power and gas supplies, and Chapter 5 the fume extraction. Chapter 6 chooses the first equipment, and Chapter 7 deals with fire and safety. Chapters 8 and 9 build the hourly cost and price jobs, and Chapter 10 forecasts the first year's cash. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Limits and professional responsibility

This book is educational. Electrical installation work must be done by licensed electricians to AS/NZS 3000 and the network's requirements; gas storage and supply must follow AS 4332, the supplier's instructions and the regulator's requirements; hot work must follow fire precautions such as those in AS 1674.1; and welding must follow the work health and safety regulator's requirements, including Safe Work Australia's model code of practice for welding processes as adopted in each jurisdiction. Leases, planning permits, insurance, tax and business structure need professional advice. The prices and costs in this book are illustrations.
