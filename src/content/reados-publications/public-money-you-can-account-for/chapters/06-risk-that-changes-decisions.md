A risk register can be a list of worries kept for the audit committee, or a working tool that changes what people approve, buy, schedule and report. The difference is easy to test. Pick any entry and ask which decision it changed in the last three months. If nobody can name one, the entry is decoration.

The Asset Programs Branch has an entry that started as decoration and became a tool: "Maintenance contractor cannot staff remote sites in the wet season".

## Where the branch register sits

Section 16 of the PGPA Act requires the accountable authority to establish and maintain "an appropriate system of risk oversight and management" and "an appropriate system of internal control" for the entity. A branch register is one small part of that system. It is not a private list. It should use the agency's risk framework, rating scales and reporting lines, and the risk team can tell you what those are.

The Department of Finance's [Commonwealth Risk Management Policy](https://www.finance.gov.au/government/comcover/risk-services/management) sets the wider frame. The policy was first introduced on 1 July 2014, and the revised policy took effect on 1 January 2023. Non-corporate Commonwealth entities must comply with its elements, and corporate entities are encouraged to align with it. Finance also publishes RMG 211, "Implementing the Commonwealth Risk Management Policy", as non-mandatory guidance. Your agency's risk framework is how the policy reaches your desk, so read it before you write an entry.

For a business manager the job is practical. Lena keeps the branch register current, makes sure every entry has an owner who acts, brings risks to the monthly branch meeting with evidence, and escalates when a risk moves outside what NRIO is willing to carry.

## Appetite and tolerance in plain words

Risk appetite is how much risk an organisation is willing to take to achieve its purposes. Risk tolerance turns that into limits you can measure, so you know when you have gone too far. Appetite is the sentence in the plan; tolerance is the line on the report.

This is an illustration. NRIO's fictional risk framework says it has a low appetite for risks to the safety of people using its facilities, and a moderate appetite for delay to non-urgent work. For maintenance it sets a tolerance: at least 95% of urgent work orders attended within the contract's response time in each quarter, at every group of sites. That last phrase was added after Ridgeway's first wet season, for reasons the failure below explains.

Appetite and tolerance should decide things. A low appetite for safety risk means NRIO will pay for prevention it might skip elsewhere. A tolerance breach is not a line for the next quarterly report; it is a trigger to act and to tell someone.

## A register entry that changes decisions

A useful entry is specific. It names the cause, the event and the consequence separately, because each points to a different kind of action. Prevention works on causes. Preparation works on consequences. An entry that says only "contractor performance" points to nothing.

This is an illustration. After Ridgeway's first year, the branch entry reads as follows.

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Risk | Maintenance contractor cannot staff remote sites in the wet season |
| Cause | Crews are based in two regional towns; floods cut the roads from both to remote sites; no local subcontractors arranged |
| Event | Urgent work at one of the 14 remote sites is not attended within the response time |
| Consequence | Unsafe conditions stay unrepaired; a facility closes or someone is hurt; the purpose "Regional communities have safe, working public facilities" is not met |
| Existing controls | Contract response times and service levels; monthly contract meetings; site coordinators' make-safe procedure |
| Owners | The Facilities Director (risk owner); Tom (contract actions); Lena (register and reporting) |
| Rating with existing controls | Likelihood 3, consequence 4: score 12, Medium |
| Treatment | Ridgeway's wet-season staffing plan, with a technician stationed for the season near the most isolated sites; local subcontractors approved before the season; pre-season inspections of roofs, gutters and drains at the 14 remote sites; weekly report of open urgent jobs during the season |
| Rating after treatment | Likelihood 2, consequence 4: score 8, Medium |
| Review | Monthly, weekly during the wet season, and at once if a trigger fires |
| Decisions changed | Inspections in the suppliers forecast; wet-season results in the option evidence; an escalation trigger |

The last row is the test, and this entry passes it three times. Tom orders the pre-season inspections as call-outs at the contract's A$375 rate: 14 × A$375 = A$5,250, which includes GST, as CPR values do. They draw on the contract's planning figure of 160 call-outs a year, so Tom tracks call-out use against it, and Lena checks that the suppliers forecast carries the cost before the season rather than finding it afterwards (Chapter 2). Tom makes wet-season performance part of the evidence for the first option decision (Chapter 5). And the Facilities Director sets a trigger, described below, that brings any breach to the risk owner the next working day.

## Rating on NRIO's 5 × 5 matrix

This is an illustration. NRIO rates likelihood and consequence from 1 to 5 against its own descriptors, then multiplies them. The fictional descriptors for the levels used here are:

- likelihood 1, rare: not expected during the life of the contract;
- likelihood 2, unlikely: could happen, but not expected in a given year;
- likelihood 3, possible: has happened in the past two years, or could happen in any season;
- consequence 3, moderate: repairs delayed and service reduced for some days;
- consequence 4, major: a facility closed for more than a week, or a person seriously hurt.

Scores from 1 to 6 are Low, 7 to 14 Medium and 15 to 25 High.

The [Risk Rating (5 × 5 matrix)](/tools/risk-score/) tool does the same arithmetic. It multiplies likelihood (1 to 5) by consequence (1 to 5) and bands the score Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14 and High 15 to 25, with a note to use your organisation's own descriptors. Use it to check a rating or to show a colleague how one factor moves the score, but rate against your agency's scales.

```figure

title: Rating the wet-season staffing risk on NRIO's matrix
caption: This is an illustration. The score sorts risks for attention. It is not a measurement, and 8 is not two-thirds of the risk of 12.
formula: Risk score = Likelihood × Consequence
- Likelihood | 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain), on NRIO's descriptors
- Consequence | 1 (minimal) to 5 (severe), on NRIO's descriptors
- Bands | Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14, High 15 to 25
step: At procurement, assuming the contract clause controlled it: 2 × 3 = 6, Low
step: With existing controls, after the first wet season: 3 × 4 = 12, Medium
step: After treatment: 2 × 4 = 8, Medium
```

Two cautions. First, the score is a ranking aid. The numbers are labels on ordered categories, and multiplying them only sorts risks for attention. Second, the score hides its parts. A likelihood of 4 with a consequence of 2 and a likelihood of 2 with a consequence of 4 both make 8, but one is a frequent nuisance and the other a rare serious harm. They need different responses, so always record both factors, not just the product.

## Residual risk and the decision to accept

Treatment works on the cause, the consequence or both. This is an illustration. At NRIO, the stationed technician, the local subcontractors and the inspections all act on the cause: they make it less likely that urgent work at a remote site waits. None of them changes what happens to a community if it does wait, so the consequence stays at 4. The residual rating is 2 × 4 = 8, still Medium.

The band did not change, but the decision did. NRIO's fictional framework lets a risk owner accept a Medium risk with documented treatments and monitoring; a High risk needs the Branch Manager. The Facilities Director accepts the residual 8 on two recorded conditions: weekly reporting during the wet season, and an immediate re-rating if any treatment fails. If the stationed technician is not in place when the season starts, the likelihood returns to 3, the score to 12, and the Facilities Director takes options to the Branch Manager.

Accepting a risk is a decision, so record who accepted it, on what basis and until when. An acceptance with no name and no date is not acceptance. It is drift.

## Shared risks with contractors and other agencies

The contract gives Ridgeway the obligation to staff the work. It does not move NRIO's accountability for safe facilities. You can allocate a task; you cannot allocate away the consequence for the communities who use the buildings.

So the risk is shared, and it is managed as one risk with two sets of actions. Tom asks Ridgeway to bring its own wet-season plan to a pre-season meeting, and the two plans are checked against each other: the same sites, the same triggers, the same contacts. During the season Ridgeway reports road closures and technician locations weekly, and NRIO tells Ridgeway at once when a site coordinator closes part of a facility.

Other agencies can share a risk too. One of NRIO's depots is also used by another Commonwealth entity's regional staff, and the roads are managed by the authorities that close them in a flood. The objects of the PGPA Act include requiring entities "to work cooperatively with others to achieve common objectives, where practicable". In practice that means agreeing, before the season and in writing, who reports what to whom.

## Procurement risk starts before the contract

The CPRs say entities "must establish processes to identify, analyse, allocate and treat risk when conducting a procurement", with effort commensurate with the scale, scope and risk of the procurement.

This is an illustration. When NRIO went to market, Grace's procurement risk assessment recorded remote-site staffing and allocated it to the contractor through the response times. That was a sound first step. The mistake came next: the branch treated allocation as treatment, and rated the risk on the assumption that the clause would control it, at 2 × 3 = 6, Low. A clause creates an obligation. It does not put a technician on a flooded road. Carry procurement risks into the contract management plan and the branch register on the day the contract is signed, each with an owner and a review rhythm that suits the risk.

## Escalation that arrives in time

Escalation is not a complaint. It gives a decision to the person who can make it while there is still time to choose. This is an illustration. NRIO's fictional rules are short:

- a tolerance breach on a safety measure goes to the risk owner the next working day;
- a risk rated High, 15 to 25, goes to the Branch Manager and the risk team within two working days;
- a risk the branch cannot treat within its own budget or authority goes up with options, costs and a recommendation;
- a shared risk is raised with the other party through the contact agreed in advance.

Check your agency's framework for its own rules, then escalate with options, not just bad news. "Remote urgent jobs are late" is a problem. "Remote urgent jobs are late; here are three ways to fix it, what each costs and which I recommend" is a decision the Branch Manager can make.

```figure
type: cycle
title: The rhythm of a register entry
caption: Each turn should end in a decision or a record. During the wet season NRIO runs the cycle weekly, and a trigger starts it at once.
- Identify | cause, event and consequence, written separately
- Rate | likelihood and consequence with existing controls, on the agency's scales
- Decide | accept within appetite or treat; record who decided and until when
- Treat | actions with owners, dates and costs in the forecast
- Monitor | the measures and triggers that would change the rating
- Review and escalate | on schedule and on triggers, with options
```

## When it goes wrong: a register reviewed once a year

This is an illustration. Through Ridgeway's first year, the remote-site entry sat in the register at its procurement rating of 6, Low. NRIO's framework at the time reviewed Low risks once a year at the planning workshop, so nobody looked at the entry during the wet season.

The evidence was there. In months 7 to 9, 45 of 60 urgent jobs across all sites were attended on time, 75%. Split by site group, the other sites managed 37 of 40, or 92.5%, while the remote sites managed 8 of 20, or 40%. Twelve of the 15 late jobs were at remote sites. One was a roof leak at a remote community hall. Floodwater cut the road, the technician arrived after six days, the ceiling was damaged and the hall closed for three weeks. Nobody was hurt.

The monthly contract meetings recorded the late jobs as a performance issue. The register still said Low. So no money moved, no subcontractors were arranged and nobody escalated, because the link between contract evidence and risk decisions was waiting for its annual review.

What should have happened is that the entry carried triggers as well as a date: the first missed urgent job at a remote site in the wet season, any breach of the service level, any facility closure. The first late remote job in month 7 should have prompted the owner to re-rate. Once the wet season showed the event could happen, the likelihood was 3 on NRIO's descriptors, and the hall closure met the descriptor for consequence 4. That gives 3 × 4 = 12, Medium, and a treatment decision in month 7 instead of at the annual workshop. Reports should also have split results by site group, because an average across every site hid a remote-site failure. That is why the tolerance now says "at every group of sites".

Annual review suits stable risks. A risk that depends on a season, a contract or a person needs review on that rhythm, and triggers that fire when the evidence changes.

## Exercise: the option notice date

This is an illustration. Ridgeway's contract requires notice of the first option by the end of month 33 (Chapter 5). Today the only controls are the date in the contract and one reminder in Tom's calendar. If the date passes without notice, the contract ends at month 36 and every site loses its maintenance contract while NRIO buys again.

Write the register entry: cause, event, consequence, existing controls, owners, rating with existing controls, treatment, rating after treatment, review, and the decisions it changes. Use NRIO's descriptors and bands above.

### Worked answer

| Field | Entry |
|---|---|
| Risk | The first option notice date passes without a decision |
| Cause | One person holds the date; staff change over three years; the decision needs evidence and the delegate's time |
| Event | Notice is not given by the end of month 33 |
| Consequence | The contract ends at month 36; sites go without planned maintenance and urgent repairs while NRIO buys again in a hurry |
| Existing controls | The date in the contract; one calendar reminder |
| Owners | The Facilities Director (risk owner); Tom (actions); Lena (reporting) |
| Rating with existing controls | 3 × 4 = 12, Medium |
| Treatment | Option timeline in the contract management plan; system reminders at months 24, 27 and 30; Grace named as backup; option status in Lena's monthly report; decision brief to the Branch Manager by month 30 |
| Rating after treatment | 1 × 4 = 4, Low |
| Review | Monthly until notice is given, then close the entry |
| Decisions changed | Evidence gathering starts at month 27; the Branch Manager's time is booked for month 31 |

Likelihood is 3 with existing controls: a single reminder, held by one person over almost three years, could well be missed. Consequence is 4: a gap in maintenance at every site could close facilities for more than a week. So 3 × 4 = 12, inside the Medium band of 7 to 14. The reminders fall 9, 6 and 3 months before the deadline: 33 − 9 = 24, 33 − 6 = 27 and 33 − 3 = 30. The treatments act on the cause, with independent reminders, a backup and monthly visibility, so the likelihood falls to 1, rare. The consequence does not change, and 1 × 4 = 4, inside the Low band of 1 to 6.

A Low score with a consequence of 4 still needs a named owner, a review on its own rhythm and a check that the reminders fire. Under NRIO's old annual rule it would have waited for the workshop, which is exactly the failure described above.
