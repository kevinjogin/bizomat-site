Payments made, reports approved and grants acquitted show that public money was handled properly. They do not show that anything changed for the people the program was meant to serve, or whether the same money could have done more. That is the evaluation's job, and it is the step most often squeezed when the next round is under way.

This is an illustration. Round 1 projects of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) ran from July 2027 to December 2028, and final reports and acquittals were due on 31 March 2029. NRIO's evaluation runs in 2029–30. Sam Reid leads it with Ivy Chen and Ben Walker, using the measures and baselines set at design (Chapter 2) and the files described in Chapter 9. It has two audiences: the branch, which is managing Round 2, and the Minister, who will need advice on whether the program should continue after its funding ends in 2029–30.

## What the CGRPs expect, and when

The [Commonwealth Grants Rules and Principles 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) (CGRPs) treat evaluation as part of the grants lifecycle (2.8(e)), and their evaluation provisions use "should": they describe better practice. Officials should work out what change a grant is expected to bring and then measure the actual outcome (10.2), and should set performance measures on which to evaluate grants (10.8). They also "should undertake an evaluation of a grant opportunity before initiating further grant opportunities or extending existing grant agreements" (10.8).

That is hard when rounds overlap. This is an illustration. Round 2 opened in 2028, while Round 1 halls were still being built, so no outcome evidence existed when its guidelines were drafted. As Chapter 2 planned, a short review of Round 1's process ran in August and September 2027, before Round 2's guidelines were drafted, and the outcome evaluation follows in 2029–30. A "should" that cannot be met in full is met as far as it can be, and the reason is recorded.

An evaluation starts with questions, not data. Each question needs a measure, a source and a comparison, agreed before anyone opens a spreadsheet.

```figure
- Was the program run properly? | Records complete and on time; variations within delegation | Grant files, GrantConnect and internal audits
- What should change? | Findings that recur across projects | All of the above, and interviews with grantees
```

## Baseline and comparison

Paragraph 12.2 states the test every evaluation faces: "a grant should add value by achieving something worthwhile that would not occur without the grant." A before-and-after figure cannot answer it. If use was rising anyway, because towns grew or a new sport arrived, a before-and-after figure credits the grant with that too. What you want is the change compared with what would have happened without the grant. Nobody can observe that directly, so a comparison group stands in for it.

This is an illustration. RCFP has a natural comparison group. Of the 61 applications that met the minimum score on every criterion, 31 were funded and 30 were not. The 30 had similar facilities and needs, applied in the same round and were judged fundable; they missed out because of the funding line and the final decisions. Every applicant's baseline, the 12 months to 31 January 2027, came with its application (Chapter 2). Funded facilities report their first 12 months after completion in the return their agreements require. The evaluation asked the other 30 for their records for 2029, which overlaps most funded result years, and 24 agreed.

| Group | Average hours a week, baseline | Result year | Average change | Median change | Facilities that improved |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Funded (31) | 11 | 22 | +11 | +10 | 27 of 31 |
| Comparison (24) | 10 | 12 | +2 | +2 | 14 of 24 |

Chapter 2's plan reports the median change and the number of facilities that improved, so that one large library cannot swamp many small halls, and both tell the same story. Cost calculations need totals, so the evaluation also uses averages: the funded facilities gained 11 hours a week, and the comparison facilities 2 without a grant. The estimated effect is the difference: 11 − 2 = 9 hours a week per facility, or 9 × 31 = 279 extra hours a week across the round. A before-and-after report would have claimed 11 × 31 = 341.

The comparison is not perfect, and the report says so. Some unfunded applicants may have found other money, six did not respond, and their result year does not match each funded facility's exactly. Each limit lowers the confidence the evaluation can claim; none makes the comparison worthless.

## Facility use you can rely on

Among its examples of outcome measures, the CGRPs list "the level of usage of facilities built with the grant" (10.4). RCFP's first measure, booked hours a week, is exactly that. Its other two, regular user groups and days lost to condition, come from the same records (Chapter 2). The evaluation also records which facilities gained accessible features, such as Kellerton Hall's accessible toilets.

Paragraph 10.5 lists common issues to consider. One is relying on indicators built from grantee data without checking that the grantee can produce accurate, reliable and complete data, and volunteers' booking records vary. This is an illustration. Chapter 2's plan checks a sample of baselines at site visits. For the result year, Ben checked six funded and four comparison facilities' records against hire receipts and a visit. Most matched within an hour or two a week. One hall had kept counting bookings that had lapsed months earlier; its figures were corrected, and the report describes the check.

Data costs grantees time. Officials should balance the information they seek against the cost to grantees of collecting it (10.7), so ask only for what answers a question. One of the ways grantees contribute to value, the CGRPs say, is "Participating in evaluation processes" (12.3). Most will, if the request is short and its purpose explained.

## Cost per outcome

A cost per outcome divides what was spent by what it achieved. It puts projects of different sizes and types on one scale, and it is where input measures, such as the cost of administering grants (10.4), meet outcome measures.

This is an illustration. The evaluation subtracts the comparison group's average gain of 2 hours a week from each project's change, a simplification it states. The three not-for-profit projects had no funding beyond their grants; Eastbrook's council added A$300,000.

| Project | Grant | Total cost | Hours a week, baseline | Result year | Attributed gain |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Kellerton Hall | A$420,000 | A$420,000 | 8 | 25 | 25 − 8 − 2 = 15 |
| Brindlemere change rooms | A$310,000 | A$310,000 | 12 | 22 | 22 − 12 − 2 = 8 |
| Shire of Eastbrook library | A$500,000 | A$800,000 | 6 | 38 | 38 − 6 − 2 = 30 |
| Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed | A$85,000 | A$85,000 | 6 | 14 | 14 − 6 − 2 = 6 |

For the round, A$9.98 million ÷ 279 hours = A$35,771 of grant per additional weekly hour. NRIO's records attribute A$600,000 of the A$1.2 million departmental funding to Round 1: A$19,355 per grant, or 6.0 per cent of the grant value. Counting it, the round's figure rises to A$10.58 million ÷ 279 = A$37,921.

Read cost per outcome with care. Lower is not automatically better: a hall in a town of 900 people may never match the use of a library in a town of 14,000, and still be the only meeting place for miles. And say whose cost you count. Eastbrook's figure is A$16,667 from the Commonwealth's side, but A$800,000 ÷ 30 = A$26,667 counting the council's money.

## The benefit–cost ratio

A benefit–cost ratio (BCR) divides the present value of a project's benefits by the present value of its costs. Present value converts future amounts into today's dollars using a discount rate, because a dollar of benefit in 15 years is worth less than a dollar now. A ratio above 1 means the benefits, as valued, exceed the costs.

This is an illustration. The evaluation states its assumptions once and uses them for every project:

- each additional hour of community use is worth A$60, the typical hire charge for a comparable regional hall, a cautious stand-in for what users would pay;
- weekly hours become yearly hours by multiplying by 52, the reverse of Chapter 2's calculation;
- benefits include costs the grantee no longer pays, such as repairs to an old roof;
- costs are the whole project cost, whoever paid it, plus any extra yearly running costs;
- the period is 20 years, with no salvage value at the end, and amounts are level yearly sums in today's dollars, excluding GST;
- the discount rate is 7 per cent a year. This is the case's assumption, not an official rate. Use the rate your agency or central guidance requires, and test others.

The [Benefit–Cost Ratio](/tools/benefit-cost-ratio/) tool does this arithmetic. From the initial investment, annual benefits, annual operating costs, salvage value, years and discount rate, it gives the present values of benefits and costs, the ratio and the net present benefit. It also gives a modified ratio, which nets running costs off benefits; RCFP reports the conventional one.

For Kellerton Hall the yearly benefit is 15 hours × 52 weeks × A$60 = A$46,800 of use, plus A$4,000 of roof repairs the committee no longer pays: A$50,800. Extra cleaning, power and insurance cost A$5,000 a year.

```figure
type: formula
title: Benefit–cost ratio for Kellerton Hall
caption: This is an illustration, using the evaluation's assumptions and a 7 per cent discount rate chosen for this case. Dollar figures use the unrounded factor, 10.594014.
formula: BCR = PV of benefits ÷ (initial cost + PV of running costs − PV of salvage)
formula: Annuity factor = (1 − 1 ÷ 1.07^20) ÷ 0.07 = 10.594
- Annual benefits | 15 × 52 × A$60 + A$4,000 = A$50,800
- Initial cost | A$420,000, the whole project
- Running costs | A$5,000 a year; no salvage after 20 years
step: PV of benefits = A$50,800 × 10.594 = A$538,176
step: PV of costs = A$420,000 + A$5,000 × 10.594 = A$420,000 + A$52,970 = A$472,970
step: BCR = A$538,176 ÷ A$472,970 = 1.14; net present benefit A$65,206
```

The annuity factor turns a level yearly amount into its present value. At the case's rate, Kellerton returns about A$1.14 of valued benefit for each dollar of cost. Now test what that depends on. At 4 per cent the ratio is 1.41; at 10 per cent it is 0.93; with benefits 20 per cent lower it is 0.91. The result rests on the hours and the value of an hour, so report the range, not a single figure.

## Attribution and its limits

The comparison group deals with the trend. An honest evaluation names what it does not deal with:

- **Selection.** Funded projects scored higher, so the estimate of 9 hours may be too high.
- **Displacement.** Some new bookings moved from another venue in the same town, a gain for the hall but not for the town. The evaluation asked new user groups where they met before, and says so beside any project where most had moved.
- **What the dollar figure leaves out.** Accessible toilets let people attend who could not before. Describe such benefits with evidence, such as the number of facilities with new accessible features, but do not invent a price for them.
- **Time.** Twelve months after completion is early. New use may fade or keep growing, and a later count would show which.

Write the finding as a contribution, not a proof: "the grants are estimated to have added about 9 hours of use a week per facility", not "the grants doubled use".

This is an illustration. The two grants approved against officials' recommendations, including Corran Bay Progress Association's hall kitchen, are measured the same way and reported in the same tables as every other grant. The evaluation reports results. It does not revisit the Minister's decisions, which were recorded and reported as Chapter 6 described.

## Lessons for Round 2 and advice on continuation

Lessons come from two sources: the outcome evidence and the round's own records. This is an illustration. The clearest outcome lesson is the one described below: facilities with nobody responsible for opening and booking them gained no use, however good the building work. From the records, the evaluation checks whether the controls added after Round 1's problems, such as the shared publication register (Chapter 9), have worked in Round 2.

Whether RCFP continues after 2029–30 is for the Minister and the government, and new funding is not officials' decision. Officials owe advice the decision-maker can rely on: the evidence, how sure the evaluation is, the options with their costs and risks, and a recommendation, including findings that are unwelcome.

Sam recommends continuing with three changes, each for future guidelines that the Minister would approve. Applicants describe who will open, book and record use of the facility, assessed under capacity to deliver. Booking records follow the evaluation's definition from the baseline year to 12 months after completion. And every applicant is asked, when it applies, to agree to be contacted for the evaluation, so the comparison group is built in from the start.

## When it goes wrong: an evaluation that counted outputs

This is an illustration. The first draft of the evaluation report, written in March 2030 from the final reports, counted facilities upgraded, milestones met, payments acquitted and grantee satisfaction with NRIO's administration, at 94 per cent. It concluded that the program had "achieved its objectives" and recommended continuing it unchanged. It did not use the booking returns the evaluation had collected, and so repeated, with the data in hand, the mistake of the 2023–24 hall program in Chapter 2.

Sam's review asked what the draft could not answer: how much more are these facilities used than before, and than the ones we did not fund? Every number in the draft was correct, and none was an outcome. Paragraph 10.5 warns against "assuming that a grant being awarded automatically secures the desired outcome". The draft made that assumption 31 times.

The booking data showed that 4 of the 31 upgraded facilities had no rise in use at all. In each, nobody was responsible for opening the building and taking bookings: one committee had wound up, a caretaker had retired, and two councils had no officer assigned. No output count could have shown it. The cost was six weeks of rework and advice that reached the Minister later than the office had asked. Had the draft gone forward, it would have recommended repeating a design with a known gap.

The control belongs in the evaluation plan, written at the design stage. Each question names its outcome measure, baseline and comparison, and every draft is checked against the plan before anyone reads its conclusions. A draft that answers an outcome question with an output count goes back.

## Exercise: a benefit–cost ratio for three projects

This is an illustration. Using the evaluation's assumptions and the 7 per cent rate, calculate the benefit–cost ratio and net present benefit for three projects, then for the three together. Explain what the results mean for the advice, and what changes if Eastbrook's cost is counted as the grant alone.

| Project | Initial cost | Attributed gain, hours a week | Avoided costs a year | Extra running costs a year |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Brindlemere change rooms | A$310,000 | 8 | A$2,000 | A$3,000 |
| Shire of Eastbrook library | A$800,000 | 30 | A$8,000 | A$10,000 |
| Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed | A$85,000 | 6 | nil | A$1,500 |

### Worked answer

The annuity factor is 10.594, as for Kellerton. Dollar figures use the unrounded factor and are rounded to the nearest dollar.

Annual benefits:

- Brindlemere: 8 × 52 × A$60 = A$24,960, plus A$2,000 = A$26,960.
- Eastbrook: 30 × 52 × A$60 = A$93,600, plus A$8,000 = A$101,600.
- Tallowood: 6 × 52 × A$60 = A$18,720.

| Project | PV of benefits | PV of costs | BCR | Net present benefit |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Brindlemere | A$26,960 × 10.594 = A$285,615 | A$310,000 + A$31,782 = A$341,782 | 0.84 | −A$56,167 |
| Eastbrook | A$101,600 × 10.594 = A$1,076,352 | A$800,000 + A$105,940 = A$905,940 | 1.19 | A$170,412 |
| Tallowood | A$18,720 × 10.594 = A$198,320 | A$85,000 + A$15,891 = A$100,891 | 1.97 | A$97,429 |
| Together | A$1,560,287 | A$1,348,613 | 1.16 | A$211,674 |

Together: A$285,615 + A$1,076,352 + A$198,320 = A$1,560,287 of benefits and A$341,782 + A$905,940 + A$100,891 = A$1,348,613 of costs, so the ratio is A$1,560,287 ÷ A$1,348,613 = 1.16 and the net present benefit A$211,674.

What the results mean:

1. **Pool the present values; do not average the ratios.** (0.84 + 1.19 + 1.97) ÷ 3 = 1.33 gives the A$85,000 shed the same weight as the A$800,000 library. The pooled 1.16 weighs each project by its size.
2. **A ratio below 1 is a finding, not a verdict.** Brindlemere's valued use does not cover its cost. Change rooms that meet current standards may matter more for safety and for who can play than for hours booked, so report the ratio and describe those benefits, with evidence, beside it.
3. **Count the whole cost.** With the grant alone, Eastbrook's costs would be A$500,000 + A$105,940 = A$605,940, and its ratio A$1,076,352 ÷ A$605,940 = 1.78. That overstates its value, because the council's A$300,000 is a real cost. The Commonwealth-only view belongs in the cost per outcome, labelled as such.
4. **Test the rate.** At 10 per cent the factor falls to 8.514 and the pooled ratio to 0.95. The advice should say that these projects' benefits exceed their costs at the case's rate, but not at a higher one.
5. **Three projects are not the program.** The advice should rest on the ratio for all 31 grants, calculated the same way.

A fair summary for the Director: at the evaluation's assumptions, these three projects return about A$1.16 of valued benefit per dollar of cost, with a wide spread between projects and a result below 1 at a 10 per cent rate. The ratio supports continuing with changes; on its own, it does not prove value for money.

This book began with a question that sounded too simple to ask: is this a grant? It ends with one that sounds just as simple: did it work? Each chapter in between added one part of a grant you can defend: rules applied to the right kind of arrangement, a program designed to show results, guidelines applicants could rely on, a selection process chosen for reasons, a fair assessment, advice and decisions on the record, controlled agreements and payments, monitoring in proportion to risk, timely publication and a complete file, and an evaluation that asks what changed. None of these steps is complicated. Each depends on doing the ordinary thing properly on the day and writing down why. A grant built that way can be explained to an auditor, to an applicant who missed out, to a minister, or to a stranger reading the file ten years later.
