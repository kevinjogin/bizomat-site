Every clause of the guidelines in Chapter 3 rested on an earlier choice: how grantees would be selected. That choice decides who may apply, whether applications are compared with each other, how long the round takes and how much work it needs. It is made early, and it is hard to defend later if nobody wrote down why.

This is an illustration. The RCFP's design record sets out why the program is open competitive, and the Minister approved that choice with the guidelines. In October 2026 it was tested, when the Shire of Eastbrook asked the Minister for a direct grant for its library extension, without waiting for the round.

## Six selection processes

Paragraph 11.3 of the [Commonwealth Grants Rules and Principles 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) (CGRPs) says officials should consider the options for selection processes, "including" six. Use the CGRPs' names, so every reader knows exactly which process you mean.

```figure
type: compare
title: The six selection processes in the CGRPs
cols: Who can apply | How applications are judged | Compared with each other
rowhead: Process
caption: Paraphrased from paragraph 11.3 and the glossary of the CGRPs. The names are the CGRPs' own. Selection criteria means eligibility and assessment criteria together.
- Open competitive | Anyone eligible, between published opening and closing dates | Against the selection criteria | Yes
- Targeted or restricted competitive | A small number, because the activity has specialised requirements | Against the selection criteria | Yes
- Non-competitive, open | Anyone eligible, at any time over the life of the opportunity | Each on its own, against the selection criteria | No
- Demand-driven or 'first-in first-served' | Anyone who meets the eligibility criteria, until the money runs out | Eligibility criteria only; assessment criteria are not used | No
- Closed non-competitive | Only those the entity invites | Each on its own, against the selection criteria | No
- One-off ad hoc grants | One or a very few, when all four elements of 2.4(c) apply | On proper use and value with relevant money, usually by a minister | No
```

Three points are easy to miss. Only the two competitive processes compare applications with each other; in the other four, each application stands alone. A demand-driven process uses only verifiable and objective eligibility criteria: "Assessment criteria are not used." Those criteria must be clearly linked to the achievement of value with relevant money (11.3), and the specific risks of the opportunity should be documented and provided to delegates (footnote 70). And better practice for a closed non-competitive process is to name the eligible applicants or explain how eligibility was determined (11.3).

## What the CGRPs say about choosing

**Factors.** Paragraph 11.1 says the choice needs to take account of value with relevant money, an outcomes orientation and proportionality, and 9.4 says officials should use proportionality to inform it. Paragraph 11.2 lists the key factors. They include the nature and needs of potential grantees, the outcomes sought, the likely number and type of applications, the nature and value of the grant, the need for timely and cost-effective decisions, and the advantages, disadvantages and risks of each option.

**The default.** Paragraph 11.4 says officials should choose methods that promote open, transparent, competitive and equitable access. Paragraph 11.5 says competitive, merit-based processes "should be used unless specifically agreed otherwise by a minister, accountable authority or delegate". Where another method is proposed, its rationale should be documented and included in the guidelines. Footnote 73 gives an example of when another process may suit: very few providers, each with a well-established record of delivering the activity.

Competitive selection is therefore a "should", not a "must", and a non-competitive process can be lawful and sensible. But several requirements still reach the choice, so it can never be invisible:

- guidelines must be developed for every new opportunity, including one-off ad hoc grants (4.4(a) and footnote 35), and published on GrantConnect (5.2);
- written advice to a minister who approves grants must outline the selection process followed, including the selection criteria (4.6(c)); and
- whoever approves must record the basis for the approval relative to the guidelines and value with relevant money (4.5 for officials, 4.10(b) for ministers).

## One-off ad hoc grants: four tests, all at once

Paragraph 2.4(c) describes one-off ad hoc grants as including a circumstance where all four of these apply:

1. there is an urgent need for payment to address an unexpected or unforeseen need;
2. there is no opportunity to establish a competitive or other non-competitive process;
3. the grant is available only to one, or a very small number of, persons or bodies; and
4. the grant is not expected to be repeated or made on an ongoing basis.

The glossary says this process may be appropriate when all the elements apply. A request that is urgent but foreseeable fails the first test. One that could wait for a short targeted process fails the second. One that others could rightly expect to receive too fails the third and fourth.

The choice lifts none of the other requirements. Guidelines are still required (footnote 35), and 9.4 says they should cover at least the purpose, objectives, selection process, any reporting and acquittal, and the proposed evaluation. Paragraph 5.2 makes no exception for one-off ad hoc grants; the only route not to publish is a minister's exemption under 5.8. Write each test as a sentence with its evidence, before anyone decides.

## Why the RCFP is open competitive

This is an illustration. The RCFP's design record works through the 11.2 factors in a page.

- **Applicants.** Many potential applicants across the region, from councils with grants teams to committees run by volunteers.
- **Outcomes.** The program logic in Chapter 2 aims at the facilities where need and benefit are greatest. Finding them means comparing applications.
- **Money and demand.** A round of A$10.0 million could fund as few as 20 projects at the A$500,000 maximum (A$10.0 million ÷ A$500,000 = 20), and demand was expected to be several times the money. Round 1 bore that out: 186 applications asked for A$41.3 million, 4.1 times what was available (41.3 ÷ 10.0 = 4.13).
- **Nature and value.** Building projects carry delivery risk, and judging capacity to deliver needs assessment, not a yes-or-no check.
- **Timeliness.** No urgency justified a faster, less open process.

The record then rejects the other five processes in a line each.

| Process | Why not for the RCFP |
| --- | --- |
| Targeted or restricted competitive | A hall roof needs no capability that only a few applicants have |
| Non-competitive, open | Each application is decided as it arrives, so early applicants would take the money |
| Demand-driven or 'first-in first-served' | With no assessment criteria, the fastest eligible applicants would be funded, not the best projects |
| Closed non-competitive | NRIO has no fair basis for choosing whom to invite |
| One-off ad hoc grants | Fails all four tests: no unforeseen need, time for a process, many bodies with the same need, and two rounds planned |

Open competitive is the 11.5 default, so the guidelines needed no rationale for a departure. The reasons are still on file, and in the brief that sought approval of the guidelines.

## Timing and capacity

Paragraph 11.2 names the tension: decisions should be timely and cost-effective while keeping rigour, equity and accountability. The answer is to plan for the time a competitive round takes, not to cut the process.


This is an illustration. The stages add to 48 + 38 + 43 + 14 + 24 = 167 days. Each carries work a competitive round cannot skip: answers published for everyone, eligibility checks, two assessors for each application, moderation, the panel, advice from Hartley Probity Services and a brief the Minister can rely on. Chapter 5 sizes the assessment workload.

The team is four officers, with the Director, Regional Programs, chairing the panel. The departmental funding of A$1.2 million over four years, separate from the grants, averages A$300,000 a year (A$1.2 million ÷ 4). It must cover two rounds, and Round 2 opens in 2028 while Round 1 projects are still running. A process needing far more effort would not fit, and one needing much less would not be fair.

The open period started in February, not straight after the December approval, so it did not fall mostly in the summer holidays. The CGRPs set no minimum open period (Chapter 3), and every day added to it comes out of the stages after the close.

## A council asks for a direct grant

This is an illustration. On Monday 12 October 2026, the Shire of Eastbrook wrote to the Minister asking for A$500,000 for its library extension as a direct grant, so that building could start early in 2027. On Friday 16 October the Minister's office asked NRIO for advice.

Paragraph 4.4(d) says officials must advise a minister considering a grant on the requirements of the PGPA Act and Rule and the CGRPs. Sam's written advice went to the Minister's office on Friday 23 October. A direct grant to one council, outside any round, would be a one-off ad hoc grant, so the advice tested it against 2.4(c).

| Test in 2.4(c) | Finding |
| --- | --- |
| (i) An urgent, unexpected or unforeseen need | Not met. The extension is planned, and the library is open and in use |
| (ii) No opportunity for another process | Not met. Round 1 was expected to open early in 2027 |
| (iii) One or a very small number of recipients | Not met. Other councils and community groups in the region have similar projects |
| (iv) Not expected to be repeated | Not met. A direct grant to one council would invite the same request from others |

The advice also showed what a grant outside the round would involve: its own guidelines on GrantConnect, with the rationale for the process in them (11.5), and written advice on its merits. Under section 71 of the [PGPA Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2013A00123/latest/text), a minister may approve a proposed expenditure only when satisfied, after reasonable inquiries, that it would be a proper use of relevant money, and must record the terms of the approval in writing as soon as practicable. And A$500,000 would leave the program's A$20.0 million before any other council could compete for it.

Sam recommended that the Minister decline the direct grant and invite the council to apply in Round 1. The Minister agreed and wrote to the council. Ivy Chen logged the request, the advice and the reply in the round's record of representations, a log of every contact about a particular applicant or project. Assessors do not see it; they judge what applicants submit.

Eastbrook applied in Round 1 for A$500,000 towards eligible costs of A$800,000, and ranked 7th on merit. Declining the direct grant was not a judgement against the library. It meant that nobody could later say Eastbrook had been offered a path that other councils were not.

## Writing the choice down

A selection process is a decision, so record it when it is made, in enough detail that someone who was not there can see what was chosen, why and who agreed.

```figure
type: steps
title: What the record of a selection process shows
caption: Make the record when the choice is made. Under paragraph 4.6(c), advice to a minister who approves grants must outline the selection process followed.
- The process, by its CGRPs name | for example, open competitive
- The factors weighed | the paragraph 11.2 factors that mattered, with evidence
- The options rejected | each with a one-line reason
- Who agreed, and when | a minister, the accountable authority or a delegate, in writing
- Where applicants see it | the guidelines, with the rationale if the process is not competitive
- *Where the decision-maker sees it | the written advice, which outlines the process and the selection criteria
```

NRIO's record of representations is the fictional agency's own practice, not a CGRPs requirement. It supports two things the CGRPs do ask for. Paragraph 11.7 says officials should ensure that decisions on grant applications are transparent and well documented. And 15.6 explains that a conflict of interest can arise from material personal associations, not only material personal interests. An official who knows the person asking should say so before giving advice.

## When it goes wrong: a grant with no reason on file

This is an illustration. In the first half of 2025, before the RCFP, NRIO ran a small competitive program for minor repairs to community buildings. Its round left A$150,000 unallocated. At a regional forum, the chair of a regional sporting association, a former mayor, raised a grandstand repair with one of NRIO's senior officers. The officer was a delegate for the program and had sat on a regional committee with him.

Three weeks later, an officer drafted a one-page approval for a A$150,000 grant to the association. Nobody else was invited to apply, and the proposal was not assessed against the program's criteria. The senior officer approved it by email: "Approved. Good project." The file held the email, the association's letter and the payment record. It did not hold:

- a reason for using a non-competitive process, or a record of who agreed to it (11.5);
- guidelines for the grant, on GrantConnect or anywhere else (4.4(a) and 5.2);
- the basis for the approval, relative to guidelines and value with relevant money (4.5); or
- a declaration of the delegate's association with the chair (15.6).

Months later, a football club whose grandstand project had ranked below the funding line saw the grant on GrantConnect and complained. NRIO could not say why this association was funded without competition when others had applied and missed out. The repair may have deserved funding, but the file could not show it. NRIO apologised, spent weeks reconstructing events, and reported the matter through its process for possible non-compliance with the finance law. The glossary defines the finance law to include instruments made under the PGPA Act, such as the CGRPs.

The control is the one the RCFP now uses. Any proposal to fund outside a published process goes to the Grants Program Manager in writing before anyone drafts an approval. It is tested against 11.3 and, for a one-off ad hoc grant, against all four elements of 2.4(c). A minister, the accountable authority or a delegate agrees to it specifically, in writing. Guidelines are published even for one grant, every contact goes in the record of representations, and officials declare any association with the people asking. None of these steps stops a sound grant. Each makes it defensible.

## Exercise: four requests, four processes

This is an illustration. Suppose these four requests reach Sam during 2027. For each, choose the selection process by its CGRPs name, and give your reasons and the records you would make.

- **A. Storm damage.** In late March 2027, a storm tears the roof off the only community hall in a town of 900 people. The not-for-profit committee's insurance covers A$90,000 of a A$160,000 repair, and the committee asks for the A$70,000 gap. Round 1 has closed, and Round 2 opens in 2028.
- **B. Accessible entrances.** The Minister asks for options for a separate opportunity: a fixed A$20,000 for each hall that lacks an accessible entrance, judged against a published checklist, with A$600,000 available. NRIO estimates that 25 halls would qualify.
- **C. Training hall committees.** NRIO proposes A$240,000 over two years to train volunteer committees across the region in managing building projects. Three region-wide not-for-profit networks have the reach and specialised skills to deliver it.
- **D. A request after the round.** In July 2027, a council asks the Minister for its Round 1 request of A$260,000 as a direct grant. Its application met the minimum on every criterion but ranked below the funding line and was not funded.

### Worked answer

**A: a one-off ad hoc grant may be appropriate, if every test holds.** (i) The storm was unforeseen and the town has no other hall. (ii) Round 1 has closed and Round 2 opens in 2028, so no process could consider the committee in time. (iii) One body. (iv) A storm repair is not expected to recur. All four hold, so the process is available, usually by the Minister's decision. A$90,000 + A$70,000 = A$160,000, so the request is exactly the uninsured gap; confirm the insurer's figure, because paying for what someone else will cover is not value with relevant money. Confirm the legal authority (3.8), and say where the A$70,000 comes from, since it reduces what is left for Round 2. Then prepare short guidelines covering at least what 9.4 lists, publish them on GrantConnect (5.2), and advise the Minister in writing (4.6).

**B: demand-driven, with the risks written down.** Eligibility can be checked objectively, the grant is a fixed amount and no comparison of merit is needed. The money covers A$600,000 ÷ A$20,000 = 30 grants, more than the 25 halls expected, so first-in first-served is unlikely to become a race. The eligibility criteria must be clearly linked to value with relevant money (11.3), so a hall that already has an accessible entrance should not qualify. Document the risks for the delegate (footnote 70). The main one is higher demand: if 40 halls qualified, 10 of them (40 − 30) would miss out on lodgement order alone, so the guidelines should say what happens when the money runs out. Each grant is below the RCFP's A$50,000 minimum, so this is a new opportunity with its own guidelines.

**C: targeted or restricted competitive.** First apply Chapter 1's test: the networks would train community committees, not NRIO, so this is a grant rather than a purchase. Specialised requirements limit the field to a few providers, which is what this process is for, and the three networks compete on merit. Paragraph 11.7 warns that care is needed so restrictive eligibility does not exclude applicants better placed to deliver, and footnote 73 names targeted processes alongside non-competitive ones. So record how the three were identified and why no one else could deliver, and put that reason in the guidelines. If only one network could deliver, a closed non-competitive process might fit, with the applicant named.

**D: no new process; Round 2 is the route.** A direct grant would be a one-off ad hoc grant, and it fails the tests. (i) Nothing unforeseen has happened. (ii) Round 2 opens in 2028. (iii) The council is not alone. Of the 61 applications that met the minimum, 31 were recommended, leaving 30 below the line (61 − 31 = 30). The Minister approved 2 of those, leaving 28 unfunded (30 − 2 = 28): this council and 27 others with the same claim. (iv) Funding one would invite the rest. Round 1's money is also committed: A$10.0 million − A$9.98 million leaves A$20,000, far short of A$260,000. Advise the Minister in writing (4.4(d)), log the request, offer the council feedback on its application, and point it to Round 2.
