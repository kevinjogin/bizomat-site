The FOI Act starts from access. The agency must give access to a requested document in accordance with the Act (s11A(3)), and every refusal is an exception that has to be shown on the facts. The exceptions come in two kinds that work differently. Some documents are exempt once a test is met. Others are only conditionally exempt and must still be released unless release would, on balance, be contrary to the public interest.

This is an illustration. By mid-June 2027 Priya Shah had the RCFP documents in a draft schedule: about 420 pages, each with a proposed decision and each needing a reason that would survive review. This chapter sets out both kinds of exemption, works through the provisions Liam O'Connor considered and ends with six of the documents to decide.

## Exempt and conditionally exempt

Part IV of the [FOI Act](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text) has two lists. Division 2, "Exemptions", covers national security, defence and international relations (s33), Cabinet documents (s34), law enforcement and public safety (s37), secrecy provisions (s38), legal professional privilege (s42), material obtained in confidence (s45), Parliamentary Budget Office documents (s45A), contempt of Parliament or a court (s46), trade secrets and commercially valuable information (s47) and electoral rolls (s47A). If a document meets one of these tests, the agency is not required to give access (s11A(4)). There is no public interest test.

Division 3, "Public interest conditional exemptions", covers Commonwealth-State relations (s47B), deliberative processes (s47C), the Commonwealth's financial or property interests (s47D), certain operations of agencies (s47E), personal privacy (s47F), business affairs (s47G), research (s47H) and the economy (s47J). Meeting one of these tests is only the first step. The agency must still give access "unless (in the circumstances) access to the document at that time would, on balance, be contrary to the public interest" (s11A(5)). Only then is the document exempt (s31B).

```figure
caption: Apply both to what a document contains, not to its title or type. Delete what meets the test and release the rest (s22).
```

"Not required" does not mean "must not". The Act does not intend to limit any power an officer has to give access outside the Act, even to an exempt document (s3A). Release outside the Act is a separate decision by whoever holds that power, and another law, such as a secrecy provision, may forbid it.

## The public interest test

Three phrases in s11A(5) do most of the work. "On balance" means weighing factors on both sides, not finding one reason against. "At that time" points to when the decision is made, not when the document was written: advice that was sensitive before a decision may not be after it. And "contrary to the public interest" means harm to the public interest, not inconvenience to the agency.

Section 11B governs the weighing. Factors favouring access include whether access would promote the objects of the Act, "inform debate on a matter of public importance", "promote effective oversight of public expenditure" or let a person see their own personal information (s11B(3)). That list is open. The Act gives no list of factors against. They come from the facts, usually from the harm the conditional exemption describes, and they must be shown, not assumed.

Four factors must not be taken into account at all (s11B(4)):

- "embarrassment to the Commonwealth Government", or a loss of confidence in it;
- the risk of a person "misinterpreting or misunderstanding" the document;
- the "high seniority" of the author;
- the risk of "confusion or unnecessary debate".

The agency must also have regard to the Information Commissioner's guidelines on the test (s11B(5)). Record every factor considered, on both sides, and how they were weighed. Reasons for refusing a conditionally exempt document must include the public interest factors taken into account (s26(1)(aa); Chapter 6).

## Legal advice: section 42

A document is exempt if it "would be privileged from production in legal proceedings on the ground of legal professional privilege" (s42(1)). Whether privilege attaches is a question of law, so ask your legal area to confirm each claim in writing and say what it relied on. The section sets two limits. A document is not exempt if the person entitled to the privilege has waived it (s42(2)). Nor is it exempt only because it contains the agency's operational information (s42(3)): the rules, guidelines and practices the agency uses in decisions that affect the public (s8A), which it must publish anyway.

Section 42 is in Division 2, so the public interest plays no part in either direction. A strong public interest in seeing the advice cannot defeat a valid claim, and a weak one cannot create a claim that is not there.

## Deliberative matter: section 47C

Section 47C(1) covers matter in the nature of "opinion, advice or recommendation ... or consultation or deliberation" prepared or recorded for the deliberative processes of an agency, a Minister or the Government. Briefs are full of it, which makes it easy to over-claim.

The section sets its own limits. Deliberative matter does not include "operational information" or "purely factual material" (s47C(2)). And s47C does not apply at all to certain expert and prescribed reports, or to "the record of, or a formal statement of the reasons for, a final decision given in the exercise of a power" (s47C(3)(c)).

So the test runs passage by passage. In a typical brief, the background, the figures and the description of the process are factual. The analysis and the recommendation are deliberative. The signed decision is the record of a final decision. Only the middle category can be conditionally exempt, and then the public interest test decides. Once a decision has been made and announced, an argument that release would harm deliberation has to be made about that document, on its facts. A general worry that officials will be less frank is not evidence.

## Personal, business and operational information

Three more conditional exemptions arose in the RCFP documents.

**Personal privacy (s47F).** A document is conditionally exempt if disclosure "would involve the unreasonable disclosure of personal information about any person (including a deceased person)" (s47F(1)). In deciding what is unreasonable, the agency must have regard to how well known the information is, whether the person is known to be associated with the matters in the document, whether the information is publicly available, and any other relevant matter (s47F(2)). The section does not keep applicants from their own information (s47F(3)).

**Business affairs (s47G).** Information about a person's business or professional affairs, or an organisation's business, commercial or financial affairs, is conditionally exempt if disclosure would, or could reasonably be expected to, "unreasonably affect that person adversely", or could reasonably be expected to "prejudice the future supply of information" to an agency (s47G(1)). Trade secrets and commercially valuable information fall under s47 instead (s47G(2)).

**Agency operations (s47E).** Paragraphs (c) and (d) cover a "substantial adverse effect" on the management or assessment of personnel, or "on the proper and efficient conduct of the operations of an agency". Substantial is a high bar, and the decision needs evidence of how release would cause the effect.

Before deciding to release information that a business or person might reasonably wish to argue is exempt under s47G or s47F, the agency must give them a reasonable opportunity to make submissions and have regard to what they say (s27(4) and s27A; Chapter 5). Their views inform the decision; they do not make it.

This is an illustration. Sam Reid's team asked Liam to withhold the assessors' comments under s47E(d), because assessors would write less frankly in Round 2 if Round 1's comments were released. Liam asked what showed that. Nobody could point to more than a worry, and the comments had been written against the published criteria, to be read by a panel and a Minister. He found no substantial adverse effect and did not apply s47E.

## Edited copies, not whole refusals

An exemption usually covers part of a document, and s22 deals with the rest. If an agency refuses access to an exempt document, or finds information irrelevant to the request, and it is reasonably practicable to prepare an edited copy without that matter, the agency must prepare the edited copy and give access to it (s22(1) and (2)). Practicability depends on the nature and extent of the deletions and the resources available (s22(1)(c)). The applicant must be told in writing that an edited copy was prepared, the grounds for the deletions and, for exempt matter, the provision relied on (s22(3)).

```figure
type: steps
title: Deciding one passage at a time
- In scope | Is the passage within the request? If not, delete it as irrelevant (s22) and say so
- Exemption | Does a Division 2 test apply on the facts? If so, it is exempt, with no public interest test
- Conditional exemption | Does a Division 3 test apply on the facts? If not, release it
- Public interest | List the factors for and against, leave out the irrelevant ones and weigh the rest (s11A(5), s11B)
- *Edited copy | Delete only exempt or irrelevant matter, release the rest and name the provision for each deletion (s22)
caption: Record each answer. The decision on a document is the sum of the decisions on its passages.
```

This is an illustration. Material the journalist agreed was outside the request, such as staff phone numbers and email addresses (Chapter 3), is deleted as irrelevant under s22(1)(a)(ii). It is not exempt, and the notice does not call it exempt.

## Sensitive is not exempt

Some documents arrive with a warning attached: the Minister will not like this, or it will be on the front page. That is a reason to plan the release, not to withhold. None of the exemption tests turns on embarrassment, and s11B(4)(a) keeps it out of the public interest test. The same goes for an author's seniority and the risk that readers will misunderstand. The remedy for a document that might be misread is to release it, with context if needed.

This is an illustration. The RCFP documents were sensitive in exactly this way. Two grants were approved against officials' recommendations, one for Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. in the Minister's electorate, and the Minister's office had rung about both on 19 May. Under NRIO's practice, Ruth Adeyemi told the Minister's office and NRIO's executive what would be released and when. The decision stayed with Liam, who holds an authorisation under s23. Others may give a decision-maker information, not instructions.

## When it goes wrong: the whole brief as deliberative

This is an illustration. Priya's first draft schedule, sent to Liam on Wednesday 16 June, marked the 7 May brief and its ranked list as conditionally exempt in full under s47C. Its public interest note said that release could cause confusion about the Minister's decisions, and that a senior officer had signed the brief.

Liam returned it the same day. The claim failed on four counts:

- Much of the brief was purely factual material, outside s47C (s47C(2)(b)): 186 applications received, 9 ineligible and 177 assessed; 61 that fully met the criteria, 104 that partially met them and 12 that met none; the funding available; how the round was run.
- Both public interest reasons were irrelevant factors: confusion (s11B(4)(d)) and seniority (s11B(4)(c)).
- No factor favouring access was weighed, though the brief shows how A$9.98 million was granted (s11B(3)(c)).
- No edited copy was considered (s22).

The cost was two days of Priya's time re-marking the brief and the ranked list, and a longer consultation list: once the grantees' entries could be released, the grantees had to be consulted. Caught after the consultation notices went out on 24 June, the error would have meant a second round of notices. Issued, it would have withheld the advice the Minister departed from, on reasons the Act forbids.

The corrected view kept s47C only for the advice and recommendations, then weighed the public interest at the decision date.

```figure
type: balance
title: The brief's advice and recommendations, at the decision date
left: For access
right: Against access
tilt: left
- Informs debate on a matter of public importance (s11B(3)(b)) | Less frank advice in future: asserted, not shown for this brief
- Promotes oversight of public expenditure: A$9.98 million (s11B(3)(c)) | Harm to a pending decision: none, the Minister decided on 31 May
- Shows the advice behind 2 approvals against recommendations | Excluded as irrelevant: embarrassment, seniority, confusion (s11B(4))
caption: This is an illustration. With the decisions made and announced, the factors against carry little weight, so the advice is released.
```

The control is now in NRIO's schedule template. Every s47C claim names the passage, the deliberative process it served, why it is not purely factual, and what harm release would do at the decision date. Liam reviews each draft schedule before any consultation notice goes out.

## Exercise: six documents from the RCFP set

This is an illustration. Decide each document: release in full, give an edited copy or refuse. Name each provision and, for a conditional exemption, the public interest factors. Assume consultation is complete; Chapter 5 deals with the third parties' submissions and the timing of release.

| Document | What it contains |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Brief of 7 May | Background, process and category counts; advice on the merits and recommendations; Attachment A, ranking 177 applications with scores, grant sought and electorate |
| 2. Decision sheet of 31 May | Every application with its recommendation and the Minister's signed decision, with a short handwritten reason beside each change |
| 3. File note of the 19 May call | Written by Sam in July: what the Minister's adviser asked and what Sam said |
| 4. Emails with the Minister's office | Sending the brief and the spreadsheet and returning the decision sheet; one chain also covers another program |
| 5. Assessment spreadsheet | 186 rows: scores, assessors' comments, grant sought, eligibility results, and each contact person's name, phone and email |
| 6. Legal advice on the program's authority | Written by NRIO's in-house lawyers for the RCFP program area; attached to the 7 May brief on the master file |

### Worked answer

**1. Edited copy.** The factual parts are outside s47C (s47C(2)(b)). The advice and recommendations are deliberative matter, but at the decision date the public interest favours their release, as the balance above shows. Officers named in their official roles are associated with the matters in the brief (s47F(2)(b)), so their names stay. The unsuccessful applicants' names are deleted under s47G (Chapter 5): disclosing them beside their results and comments on their weaknesses could reasonably be expected to unreasonably affect these mostly volunteer-run organisations adversely in their lawful financial affairs, for example with other funders (s47G(1)(a)). The rest of the ranked list, including scores, ranks and electorates, is released without the names, which serves oversight of public expenditure and debate on the round (s11B(3)(b), (c)), so on balance access to the names would be contrary to the public interest.

**2. Edited copy.** The sheet is the record of a final decision given in the exercise of a power, so s47C cannot apply (s47C(3)(c)), and the handwritten reasons are part of that record. The approvals against recommendations, and Corran Bay's place in the Minister's electorate, make it the most sensitive document in the set, but embarrassment is an irrelevant factor (s11B(4)(a)). Section 47C(3)(c) removes only s47C. The sheet lists every application, so the unsuccessful applicants' names are deleted under s47G, as in the ranked list, and the rest is released.

**3. Release.** Parts record consultation during the Minister's deliberation, but most of the note is factual: whether the two applications met the criteria and what funding both would cost. At the decision date the factors favouring access, oversight of public expenditure and debate on a matter of public importance, outweigh any harm. The adviser's name is personal information, but the adviser is known to be associated with the call (s47F(2)(b)), and it is released after consultation (Chapter 5). The note's July date is no reason to withhold it: the journalist agreed in writing to include it (Chapter 3).

**4. Edited copy.** Emails sending and returning documents are factual and administrative, and no conditional exemption fits them. The part of the chain about another program, and the staff phone numbers and email addresses, are irrelevant to the request. They are deleted under s22(1)(a)(ii), and the notice says so (s22(3)).

**5. Edited copy.** Scores, grant sought and eligibility results are the basis of a spending decision and are released (s11B(3)(c)). The assessors' comments are opinion recorded for a deliberative process, but at the decision date the public interest favours release, and s47E(d) does not apply. The three contact columns are deleted under s47F: the contact persons are mostly volunteers, and their details are not publicly available and add nothing to oversight (Chapter 6 writes these reasons in full). The unsuccessful applicants' names are deleted under s47G, as in the brief.

**6. Refuse, once privilege is confirmed.** NRIO's legal area confirms in writing that the advice is privileged and that privilege has not been waived (s42(2)): giving it to the Minister in confidence, attached to the brief, did not waive it. The advice is then exempt under s42(1), and no public interest test applies, however important the program's authority is. Releasing it anyway, outside the Act (s3A), would be a separate decision for whoever holds that power.

Five documents are released in full or in part and one is refused: partial access, which is what Liam decided on 28 July.

Chapter 5 follows the consultation behind these decisions: whom NRIO had to consult, how, and when the documents could be released.
