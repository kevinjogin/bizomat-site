A leader can read a report in two minutes and still be misled for three months. The page says green, the numbers look tidy, and nothing on it shows whether the work is on track, what it will cost or which decision is waiting. Reports like that are rarely dishonest. They are built from the data that was easiest to collect, and easy data tends to count activity and money spent rather than results.

The business manager usually runs the machinery that turns plans into reports: the branch business plan, the measures in it, the monthly cycle of closing the numbers, and the single page the Branch Manager reads. When that machinery works, the branch can show that it uses public money properly and achieves what the agency exists to do. When it does not, problems stay invisible until they are expensive.

## From the corporate plan to the branch plan

The PGPA Act builds a performance framework. One of its objects in section 5 is to require entities "to provide meaningful information to the Parliament and the public". Three duties of the accountable authority carry that object. Section 35 requires a corporate plan at least once each reporting period. Section 38 says the accountable authority "must measure and assess the performance of the entity in achieving its purposes". Section 39 requires annual performance statements, prepared after each reporting period and included in the annual report tabled in the Parliament.

Those duties sit with the accountable authority, but the evidence starts in work areas. When an agency reports how well it kept facilities safe and working, the numbers come from a contractor's job log, a finance system and a site coordinator's records. If a branch cannot produce its numbers reliably, the agency cannot report them reliably.

This is an illustration. NRIO's corporate plan purpose is "Regional communities have safe, working public facilities." The Asset Programs Branch business plan for 2026–27 turns that purpose into three objectives: facilities are safe, facilities are working, and the branch's money is well used. Each objective has an owner and one or two measures. The plan also lists the year's main pieces of work, such as the facility condition survey (Chapter 3), the maintenance tender and the new contract (Chapters 4 and 5) and the depot upgrade below, with the risks that could stop them (Chapter 6).

Keep the branch plan short enough to use, a page or two. Test every line by asking which objective it serves. A line that serves none is either missing an objective or not needed. Check your agency's corporate plan for the exact wording of its purposes and key activities, and ask the area that prepares the annual performance statements which branch data it relies on and when it needs it.

## What a good measure does

Section 16EA of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) sets six tests. Performance measures meet the requirements if, in the context of the entity's purposes or key activities, they:

- (a) relate directly to one or more of those purposes or key activities;
- (b) use sources of information and methodologies that are reliable and verifiable;
- (c) provide an unbiased basis for measurement and assessment;
- (d) where reasonably practicable, comprise a mix of qualitative and quantitative measures;
- (e) include measures of outputs, efficiency and effectiveness if appropriate;
- (f) provide a basis for assessing performance over time.

The section is written for the entity's own measures, not for a branch plan. Its tests still make good branch measures, and a branch that uses them gives the agency data it can use without rework.

In branch terms, each test becomes a practical check. A measure relates directly to the purpose when a change in it means a change in what communities get; meetings held or reports written measure effort instead. A measure is reliable and verifiable when it names its source and method, so that someone else could recalculate it from the records. It is unbiased when it cannot be improved by making things worse, and when the people being measured do not decide what counted after the event. A mix means numbers and people's own words, because a survey score without comments says little about what to fix. Outputs, efficiency and effectiveness are, in plain words, the work done, what each unit cost, and whether it achieved the aim. Performance over time needs a baseline and the same definition every year.

Write each measure on a short card: what is counted, the source, the method, who calculates it, how often, the baseline and the target. If the card cannot be filled in, the measure is not ready.

## A monthly operating rhythm

Measures are only useful if they arrive on time and in the same form each month. This is an illustration of NRIO's branch rhythm, which runs on working days after each month ends:

- Working day 3: finance closes the month. Arun pulls actuals and open commitments for each budget line, as built in Chapter 2.
- Working day 5: the Facilities Director and the site coordinators confirm progress on projects and the month's measures. Tom adds the contractor's performance and Grace adds procurements in progress.
- Working day 6: Lena drafts the one-page reports and checks every number against its source.
- Working day 8: the Branch Manager's monthly meeting takes decisions. Jas records each decision and action with an owner and a date.
- Each quarter: the branch's measures go to the area that prepares NRIO's performance reporting, with the method cards attached.

Keep the dates fixed. When data is late, report it as late rather than estimating quietly, and say when it will arrive. The same meeting is the natural place to review the risk register (Chapter 6), so that risks and results are read together.

## Earned value for the depot upgrade

Money spent tells a leader what has gone out. It does not say what was bought with it. Earned value compares three amounts at a reporting date. Planned value (PV) is the budgeted cost of the work the baseline schedule says should be done by now. Earned value (EV) is the budgeted cost of the work actually done. Actual cost (AC) is what that work has cost, including costs incurred but not yet invoiced.

This is an illustration. The depot upgrade has its own project budget, separate from the branch's operating budget. Its budget at completion (BAC) is A$800,000. Like the branch budget, it is an internal budget and excludes GST; procurement values under the CPRs include GST. The project is reported at 31 October 2026.

By that date the baseline schedule had design (A$80,000), demolition and slab (A$120,000) and the steel structure (A$200,000) complete, so PV is A$400,000. The rest of the budget, A$400,000, covers the roof, services and fit-out. Design and the slab are complete. Progress on the structure is counted by frames erected, a method agreed in the baseline: 14 of 20 frames are up, so the structure is 70% complete and has earned 0.7 × A$200,000 = A$140,000. EV is A$80,000 + A$120,000 + A$140,000 = A$340,000. Arun's ledger, with accruals, puts AC at A$380,000.

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---:|
| Cost variance (CV) | EV − AC = A$340,000 − A$380,000 | −A$40,000 |
| Schedule variance (SV) | EV − PV = A$340,000 − A$400,000 | −A$60,000 |
| Cost performance index (CPI) | EV ÷ AC = 340,000 ÷ 380,000 | 0.89 |
| Schedule performance index (SPI) | EV ÷ PV = 340,000 ÷ 400,000 | 0.85 |
| Estimate at completion (EAC) | BAC ÷ CPI = A$800,000 × 380,000 ÷ 340,000 | A$894,118 |
| Variance at completion (VAC) | BAC − EAC = A$800,000 − A$894,118 | −A$94,118 |
| To-complete performance index (TCPI) | (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC) = 460,000 ÷ 420,000 | 1.10 |

Indices are rounded to two decimal places and dollars to the nearest dollar, at the end. The estimate uses the unrounded CPI: dividing by 340,000 ÷ 380,000 is the same as multiplying by 380,000 ÷ 340,000.

Read the results in words. The work done so far cost A$40,000 more than its budget, and A$60,000 of planned work has not been done. Each dollar spent has bought about 89 cents of budgeted work. If that continues, the depot will cost about A$894,000, some A$94,000 over budget. To finish on budget, the remaining A$460,000 of budgeted work must be done for the A$420,000 left, a performance of 1.10 when the project has managed 0.89. That is not credible without a change to scope, method or budget, and the report should say so.

The [Earned Value Check](/tools/earned-value-check/) does this arithmetic. From budget at completion, planned value, earned value and actual cost, it gives the cost and schedule variances, CPI, SPI, estimates at completion and the to-complete performance index, and compares them with the limits you set. One of its estimates applies both indices to the remaining work; for the depot it gives A$984,844, a useful pessimistic case. The tool is only as good as the inputs. Earned value must come from measured progress, such as completed packages or frames erected, not from hours worked or money spent.

## A one-page report the Branch Manager can rely on

A leader needs five things from a project or program report: its status, its trend, its cost position, the main risk and the decision needed. Everything else belongs in an attachment. This is an illustration. NRIO's reporting guide sets the colours in advance from the indices: green at 0.95 or above, amber from 0.90 to below 0.95, and red below 0.90. Because the rule is fixed, nobody chooses a colour to suit the mood of the month.

Here is the depot's page for October.

| Line | Depot upgrade, 31 October 2026 |
|---|---|
| Status | Red. CPI 0.89 and SPI 0.85, both below NRIO's red line of 0.90. |
| Trend | Worse for three months: CPI 0.97, 0.93, 0.89; SPI 0.95, 0.88, 0.85. |
| Cost position | Budget A$800,000. A$380,000 spent for A$340,000 of work. Forecast about A$894,000 if cost performance continues. |
| Main risk | The roof is not closed in before the wet season, adding delay and cost. Owner: the Facilities Director. |
| Decision needed | Branch Manager, by Friday 13 November: agree that the Facilities Director obtains a costed recovery plan from the builder by 27 November, and that the forecast goes to the CFO this month. |

The page states what changed since last month and who owns each next step. It names a decision-maker and a date, because a report that needs nothing from its reader is a report the reader stops reading. Red is not a verdict on the people involved. It is the signal that brings help while help is still cheap.

## When it goes wrong: three green months

This is an illustration. Before the October report used earned value, the depot upgrade was reported green three months running. The report compared money spent with money planned: A$196,000 against A$200,000 in August, A$285,000 against A$300,000 in September and A$380,000 against A$400,000 in October. Each month spend was below plan, by A$4,000, A$15,000 and A$20,000, so the project was described as "on track and under budget". Its only performance measure was site inspections completed, which stood at 12 of 12, or 100%.

Both signals counted inputs. Spending less than planned can mean the work is efficient, or that it is behind. Inspections completed says the branch looked, not what it found. Earned value for the same three months tells a different story: CPI 0.97, 0.93 and 0.89, and SPI 0.95, 0.88 and 0.85. Under NRIO's own thresholds the project was amber on cost and red on schedule in September, a month before anyone said so. That month mattered. In September the roof had not been ordered and the wet season was further away, so a recovery plan would have had more choices and cost less.

What should have happened is simple to describe. Earned value from the first report, with the progress method agreed in the baseline. Colours set by fixed thresholds, not by the author. A measure of results, such as milestones achieved by their dates, in place of inspections counted. And a branch habit of reporting red early without blame. A leader who sees a long run of green can ask one useful question: what would have to happen for this to turn amber, and would this report show it?

## Exercise: rewrite three weak measures

The branch's draft plan for next year contains three measures:

1. Number of contractor performance meetings held each quarter.
2. Facilities are well maintained.
3. Percentage of the maintenance budget spent by 30 June.

Rewrite each so that it meets the six tests in section 16EA of the PGPA Rule. Name its source and method, say which tests it meets, and give a baseline that would let the branch assess performance over time.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. The baselines are fictional figures from the branch's 2025–26 records, rounded to one decimal place at the end.

**1. Meetings held.** This counts effort. A quarter with six meetings and poor work scores better than a quarter with two meetings and good work. Rewrite: "Share of planned maintenance work orders completed by their due date", from the contractor's job log, checked each month against the site coordinators' sign-offs. Baseline: 1,104 of the 1,200 planned work orders completed in 2025–26 were on time, and 96 were late, so 1,104 ÷ 1,200 = 92.0%. It relates directly to the "working" objective (a), uses a source someone else can check (b) and is an output measure (e). The meetings still happen; they become the place where this measure is discussed.

**2. Well maintained.** Nobody can verify a phrase. Rewrite it as two linked measures: facility days lost to unplanned closure each quarter, recorded by the site coordinators from 1 July, and the share of site users who say their facility is available when needed, from a survey twice a year, reported with a summary of their comments. Baseline for the survey: 138 of 184 responses, so 138 ÷ 184 = 75.0%. Together they give the mix of qualitative and quantitative information in (d) and an effectiveness measure in (e).

**3. Budget spent.** This measure rewards spending, and it improves with a rush of orders in June whatever they achieve, so it fails (c). Rewrite: "Average cost per completed planned work order", using the GST-exclusive expense in the finance system and completed orders in the job log, on the same basis each year. Baseline: A$1,080,000 of planned maintenance expense for 1,200 completed orders, A$1,080,000 ÷ 1,200 = A$900.00 per order. It is an efficiency measure (e) with a basis over time (f). Read it with measure 1, so that cost cannot be cut by doing the work late.

Each rewritten measure names its source and method, so Arun, an auditor or the performance area could recalculate it from the records.
