An FOI decision reaches the applicant as a letter, and the letter is all the applicant has. It says what they will not get, why not, and what they can do about it. Every reviewer starts with the same letter: the internal reviewer, the Information Commissioner and possibly the Administrative Review Tribunal. A decision may be right, but if its reasons do not show why, it is hard to defend and easy to challenge.

This is an illustration. On Wednesday 28 July 2027 Liam O'Connor gave the journalist partial access to the documents about Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP), nine days before the extended deadline (Chapter 5). Twelve days later the journalist applied for internal review, and IC review followed. This chapter covers what a statement of reasons must contain, how each kind of review works, and what one thin paragraph in Liam's notice cost the team.

## What a statement of reasons must contain

Section 26 of the [Freedom of Information Act 1982](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A02562/latest/text) applies when a decision refuses access to a document, in whole or in part, or defers access. The decision-maker must give the applicant a written notice that does four things:
- **Findings and reasons:** state "the findings on any material questions of fact, referring to the material on which those findings were based", and the reasons for the decision (s26(1)(a)).
- **Public interest factors:** for a conditionally exempt document, include "the public interest factors taken into account" (s26(1)(aa)).
- **The decision-maker:** for an agency document, the "name and designation" of the person deciding (s26(1)(b)).
- **Rights:** information about review rights and the right to complain to the Information Commissioner, and how to use each (s26(1)(c)).

The notice need not contain anything that would make a document exempt (s26(2)). That limits detail; it does not license vagueness. You can describe a deletion as "the assessors' comments on an applicant's finances" without repeating them. Section 26 also applies to internal review decisions (s54C(4)).

In deciding a request, the agency must have regard to the guidelines the Information Commissioner issues under section 93A (s15(5A), s93A(2)). They are not legislative instruments; read the current version alongside the Act.

## Findings, evidence and the test

Each deletion needs its own short chain of reasoning:
1. **The information:** which document, which part, what kind of information.
2. **The facts that matter:** what release would do, to whom, and how likely that is.
3. **The material:** the evidence for each finding, such as a submission, an officer's advice or the document itself.
4. **The test:** the words of the section, and how the findings meet them.
5. **The public interest (conditional exemptions only):** the factors for access, including any in s11B(3); the factors against; confirmation that no irrelevant factor in s11B(4) was considered; and how you weighed them.

The two kinds of exemption need different reasons. A document exempt under Division 2, such as one covered by legal professional privilege under section 42, need not be released (s11A(4)), and there is no public interest test. A conditionally exempt document must be released unless access "would, on balance, be contrary to the public interest" (s11A(5)). Reasons that weigh public interest factors for a section 42 document, or skip them for a section 47F document, show the wrong test was applied.

Write for a reader who was not there. The applicant cannot see the deleted text, so the findings are the only way to judge the decision, and a reviewer cannot test findings that were never made.

## Internal review: a fresh decision by someone else

Internal review lets the agency look at its own decision again, quickly. The applicant may seek internal review of an access refusal decision (s54), and a third party of an access grant decision (s54A; Chapter 5). It is not available for a decision made personally by the principal officer or the responsible Minister (s54(1)); those go straight to IC review.

- **Applying:** in writing, within 30 days after the day the decision is notified, or a further period the agency allows, even after the 30 days have passed (s54B(1)(a), (2)). Where the decision gave access to some documents but not all, the period is the longer of those 30 days and 15 days after access was given (s54B(1)(b)).
- **Deciding:** the agency must arrange, as soon as practicable, for a person other than the original decision-maker to review the decision (s54C(2)). That person must make a fresh decision within 30 days after the day the application was received (s54C(3)).
- **Missing the time:** the principal officer is taken to have personally affirmed the original decision on the last day (s54D(2)). The agency may apply in writing to the Information Commissioner for further time; a decision made within that time, meeting any conditions, displaces the deemed one (s54D(3) to (6)).
- **No second round:** there is no internal review of an internal review decision (s54E). The next step is IC review.

A fresh decision means deciding the request again on the material available now, not asking whether the first decision was reasonable. The reviewer may affirm, vary or reverse it. If the fresh decision would release third-party information the original withheld, check section 27 or 27A first.

The Act requires only that the reviewer is not the original decision-maker. NRIO, by its own choice, goes further: its internal reviewers had no part in the decision and hold an authorisation under section 23. For the RCFP request, the reviewer was an EL2 from another branch.

## The journalist's internal review

This is an illustration. The journalist applied on Monday 9 August 2027, well within time: the period ran at least to Friday 27 August, 30 days after the decision was notified. The application challenged two parts of Liam's decision:
- the deletion, under section 47G, of the names of the 155 unsuccessful applicants from the brief's ranked list and the assessment spreadsheet;
- the refusal, under section 42, of NRIO's legal advice on the program's authority.

On Tuesday 10 August Ruth arranged the review. The fresh decision was due by Wednesday 8 September, 30 days after 9 August. The reviewer decided on Friday 3 September, day 25 of the 30, and affirmed both parts.

On the legal advice, she found who wrote it (NRIO's in-house lawyers), for whom (the RCFP program area), for what purpose (legal advice on the program's authority), that it had been kept confidential, and that NRIO had not waived privilege (s42(2)). She explained that section 42 has no public interest test, so the journalist's public interest arguments could not make the advice available under the Act. On the names, she wrote the findings that Liam's notice had left out (see "When it goes wrong" below). Her letter set out the journalist's rights to IC review and to complain.

## Review by the Information Commissioner

An applicant may seek IC review of an access refusal decision, an internal review decision, or a refusal to allow more time for internal review (s54L(2)), "regardless of whether the decision was the subject of internal review" (s54L(2), Note 1). A third party may seek IC review of an access grant decision (s54M).

- **The application:** in writing, with an address for notices and a copy of the section 26 notice of the decision under review (s54N(1)).
- **Time:** within 60 days after the day the section 26 notice was given, or 30 days for a third party (s54S). The Commissioner may extend the time if it is "reasonable in all the circumstances" (s54T(2)).
- **Whether to review:** the Commissioner may make preliminary inquiries (s54V), and may decide not to review, for example if the application is "frivolous, vexatious, misconceived, lacking in substance or not made in good faith" (s54W(a)(i)), or if the Tribunal should consider the decision (s54W(b)).

The agency has duties throughout:
- **Onus and help:** it carries the onus of establishing that its decision is justified (s55D(1)), and it must use its best endeavours to assist the Commissioner (s55DA).
- **Reasons:** if the Commissioner believes the statement of reasons is inadequate, the Commissioner may require an adequate one by written notice. The agency must then provide it within the period set, or 28 days after the notice (s55E).
- **Third parties:** if the agency refused access to information to which a consultation requirement under section 26A, 27 or 27A applies, it must take all reasonable steps, as soon as practicable, to notify the affected third party of the application, and copy the notice to the Commissioner (s54P).
- **Outcome:** the agency may revise its decision during the review to give more access (s55G). The Commissioner's decision affirms, varies or sets aside the decision, with reasons (s55K), and the principal officer must comply (s55N).

This is an illustration. The journalist applied for IC review of the internal review decision on Monday 20 September 2027, 17 days into the 60, attaching the reviewer's notice. The 155 names are business information about organisations that might reasonably wish to argue they are exempt, so s54P applied. By Friday 24 September Priya had emailed each of the 155 applicants a short notice of the application, and Ruth had sent the Commissioner a copy. The team then prepared its submissions, knowing that NRIO carries the onus.

## The Tribunal and the Federal Court

The Administrative Review Tribunal (ART) replaced the Administrative Appeals Tribunal on 14 October 2024; material that sends FOI review to the AAT is out of date. The Tribunal reviews a decision of the Information Commissioner on an IC review, or a decision the Commissioner declined to review under s54W(b) (s57A(1)). An applicant cannot go straight from an agency to the Tribunal.

A person whose interests are affected by the decision may apply (ART Act s17(1)). Under the [Administrative Review Tribunal Rules 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L01179/latest/text), the period ends 28 days after the day the applicant is given the notice of the decision, or the statement of reasons if one is given (r5(3), (4)). The Commissioner's decisions include reasons (s55K(4)(a)). The Tribunal may extend the period if that is reasonable in all the circumstances (ART Act s19(2)). After a s54W(b) decision, the period starts on the day of that decision (s57A(2)). The Tribunal's guidance and appeals panel does not hear FOI reviews (s57A(3)).

A party to an IC review may also appeal to the Federal Court on a question of law, within 28 days after the decision is given or a further time the Court allows (s56(2)).

## Complaints are not reviews

Review asks whether a decision was right. A complaint is about how the agency acted. Under section 70, a person may complain in writing to the Information Commissioner about an action an agency takes under the Act, identifying the agency. The Commissioner may decide not to investigate where the person could reasonably have sought review and did not (s73(b)).

This is an illustration. The sporting club's complaint about the early release of its information (Chapter 5) concerns an action, and review cannot undo a release. The journalist's disagreement with the deletions concerns decisions, so it belongs in review. When a complaint arrives, give the Commissioner the facts, the records and what you changed.

## When it goes wrong: reasons that cite sections but not findings

This is an illustration. Liam's notice of 28 July dealt with the 155 names in two sentences: "The names of unsuccessful applicants have been deleted under section 47G. I am satisfied that their release would be contrary to the public interest."

That names a section and states a conclusion, but it does not meet s26(1)(a) and (aa). It does not say what the information was, which limb of s47G(1) applied, what release would do or what evidence showed it, and it lists no public interest factors. The journalist could not tell whether the deletions were sound, and the internal review application said so first.

The cost came in stages. The reviewer found no record of why Liam thought release would harm the applicants, and spent days rebuilding the facts with the RCFP team. She found that:
- the applicants were mostly volunteer-run community organisations relying on grants and donations;
- the files linked each name to scores below the funding line and to comments on its weaknesses, including its finances;
- several applications said the organisation had also applied to other funders for the same project;
- the program guidelines had told applicants that NRIO would publish details of successful grants.

She concluded that release could reasonably be expected to unreasonably affect the applicants adversely in their lawful financial affairs. In the public interest test she weighed debate on a matter of public importance and oversight of public expenditure (s11B(3)(b), (c)) against that harm. She noted that every unsuccessful application's score, rank, region and project type had been released, so the public could already see where each stood against the funded ones. She recorded that she had not considered embarrassment to the Government or the risk of misunderstanding (s11B(4)). By then the journalist no longer trusted the first reasons, and the matter went to the Commissioner, where NRIO carries the onus and the statement of reasons is read first.

The control is a check before any notice goes out. Ruth now asks a second officer to answer five questions for each deletion from the notice alone: What is the information? What would release do? What shows that? Why does that meet the test? For a conditional exemption, what was weighed, and how? If the reader cannot answer, the notice goes back.

## Exercise: write the reasons for one deletion

This is an illustration. The RCFP assessment spreadsheet has 186 rows, one for each application, and three columns give the contact person's name, phone number and email address.
- The contact persons lodged the applications for their organisations; most are volunteers.
- Most phone numbers are mobiles, and many email addresses are personal ones.
- The request asked for briefs, decision records and correspondence with the Minister's office, not for contact details.
- The grantees' names, scores and assessors' comments have been released, subject to the holds in Chapter 5.
- Liam was not minded to release the three columns, so no contact person was consulted.

Write the part of Liam's statement of reasons that deals with the three columns. Then list what the rest of the notice must contain under s26(1)(b) and (c).

### Worked answer

A model text, with the section each part meets:

**The material deleted.** "From the assessment spreadsheet I have deleted the name, phone number and email address of the contact person for each of the 186 applications, and given access to the rest of the spreadsheet." (An edited copy, s22.)

**Findings and material (s26(1)(a)).** "Each entry is information about an identified individual, so it is personal information (Privacy Act s6(1), adopted by FOI Act s4(1)). The contact persons lodged applications for community organisations, and most are volunteers; I rely on the spreadsheet, the application forms and the RCFP team's advice. Most phone numbers are mobiles and many email addresses are personal; I rely on the spreadsheet."

**The test in section 47F.** "Having regard to s47F(2): (a) the details are not well known; (b) the contact persons are associated with their organisations' applications, not with the briefs, decisions and correspondence the request concerns; (c) they are not publicly available, and an organisation's published contact is its own, not the person's; (d) release would expose private individuals to unsolicited contact and adds nothing to an understanding of the round. Disclosure would be unreasonable, so the columns are conditionally exempt under s47F(1)."

**The public interest (s26(1)(aa), s11A(5), s11B).** "Access would promote the objects of the Act, inform debate on a matter of public importance and promote oversight of public expenditure (s11B(3)(a) to (c)). These factors carry little weight here, because contact details reveal nothing about the assessment or the decisions, which have been released. Against access is the privacy of 186 individuals, most of them volunteers. I have not taken into account any embarrassment to the Government or risk of misunderstanding (s11B(4)). On balance, access to these columns at this time would be contrary to the public interest."

**The rest of the notice.**
- s26(1)(b): "Liam O'Connor, Senior FOI Officer, authorised under section 23."
- s26(1)(c): how to apply for internal review, in writing within 30 days after notification (s54B); for IC review within 60 days after the notice, with or without internal review (s54L, s54S(1)); and how to complain to the Information Commissioner (s70), with addresses for each.

Test the draft against the five questions. What is it? Three columns of contact details. What would release do? Expose volunteers to unsolicited contact. What shows that? The spreadsheet and application forms. Why does that meet the test? Each s47F(2) matter is addressed. What was weighed? Three factors for access, given little weight, against the privacy of 186 people. Each finding can be tested.
