Funding decisions go wrong when an owner counts equipment and registration fees but ignores the months before receipts arrive. Build a cash-based funding estimate before accepting an expansion commitment.

## Four funding categories
**One-off setup:** formation expenses, fit-out, initial tools, deposits and initial systems. **Operating runway:** rent, necessary software, insurance, utilities, planned labour and administration while sales ramp. **Working capital:** money tied up in stock, work in progress and invoices awaiting payment. **Contingency:** a cash buffer sized to the business's risks rather than a universal percentage.

```figure
caption: Count each expense once, and never treat tax provisions or refundable deposits as free cash.
- Funding required

  - Operating runway | costs while sales ramp up
  - Working capital | stock, work in progress, unpaid invoices
  - *Contingency | a buffer sized to the risks
```

A practical first-pass expression is:

**Additional funding required = setup outflows + forecast operating shortfalls + working-capital requirement + contingency − unrestricted cash already available.**

Do not count restricted tax provisions or customer deposits that must be refunded as free cash. Avoid double-counting the same expense in 'runway' and 'working capital'. Use dated cash movements for the final calculation.

## Illustrative example
Assume startup equipment and setup costs of $9,000, expected net operating cash shortfalls of $3,000 per month for four months, a $4,000 inventory timing gap and a $5,000 buffer. Unrestricted funds on hand are $7,000. Indicative additional funds are **$9,000 + $12,000 + $4,000 + $5,000 − $7,000 = $23,000**. These figures do not prove that debt or equity is suitable; they simply reveal the scale and assumptions.

## Model at least three cases
A **base** scenario uses supported sales and expense assumptions. A **downside** case delays payments and reduces conversion. An **upside** case increases sales but also models the stock, delivery and staffing cash required to fulfil them. Fast growth can consume cash before it creates cash.

For a quick runway indicator, divide accessible cash by average monthly net cash burn. If $18,000 of freely available cash supports monthly cash burn of $3,000, the simple runway is six months. This is an approximation: uneven bill dates and lump-sum payments may cause an earlier shortfall.

## Decision gates
Before spending, require a recorded answer to: what must be true for this commitment to pay back? When will cash leave? When can it return? Is the expenditure reversible? What happens if sales are half the forecast? Who checks the actual outcome?

## Funding sources
Owner capital, retained earnings, customer prepayments where appropriate, asset finance, loans, grants and equity have different risks, restrictions and control implications. Compare total cost, repayment conditions, security, dilution, tax treatment and downside survivability with a qualified adviser.

## Implementation exercise
List all planned commitments for the next six months, assign a payment date, probability and category, and attach evidence for major numbers. Then calculate the highest cumulative cash deficit, not only the final month-end balance.

*Illustrations exclude GST unless otherwise stated. Obtain specialist advice before taking on borrowing or investment commitments.*


## Extended study: applying the method

### Build a uses-of-funds schedule

Start with a dated procurement list, not a single round number. Each line should identify item, purpose, quote source, payment date, refundable deposit, contingency and whether the item is essential for first delivery. Separate items owned after purchase from ongoing services and consumables. Equipment purchases may involve installation, power requirements, training, insurance and maintenance that are not included in an attractive purchase price. For growth plans, include hiring or stock commitments made before additional sales arrive. The most reliable funding estimate is a sequence of cash requirements backed by documentary evidence, with uncertain amounts plainly labelled.

### Cash deficit versus total spending

Funding needed is not necessarily equal to the sum of every expense. Some spending is covered by receipts arriving before or during the period, while deposits may restrict how those receipts can be used. Build a weekly cash schedule and find the deepest negative cumulative balance before external financing. That minimum point reveals the liquidity bridge required under the assumptions. For instance, if cumulative forecast balances after paying setup costs reach minus $8,000 in week three, minus $17,000 in week seven and positive $2,000 in week thirteen, the greatest modelled deficit is $17,000 before any reserve. A funding plan should cover timing and risk, not just eventual profitability.

### Working capital in plain language

Working capital arises because production and collections take time. Materials are paid before sale, finished goods sit in stock, invoices wait for collection and suppliers may give payment terms. Track inventory days, debtor days and creditor days with care. The cash conversion cycle is often described as inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days; it is a useful indicator but not a substitute for dated forecasting. A customer paying in 60 days while suppliers require 14 days can create pressure even with a profitable order. Negotiate clear milestones or lawful deposits where appropriate and avoid assuming every invoice will arrive on its due date.

### Compare funding instruments by downside

A personal loan, equipment lease, equity investor and customer prepayment can provide cash but transfer different risks. Debt requires repayment even if the experiment disappoints and may involve security. Equity can dilute ownership and change decision rights. Customer deposits create delivery and refund obligations. Grants may restrict eligible spending or require evidence and reporting. For each source, list access conditions, all fees, effective financing cost, time to funds, repayment or performance obligations, consequences of failure and impact on control. Professional advice is especially valuable where personal guarantees or business restructuring are contemplated.

### Forecast volatility instead of a single future

Build three scenarios that vary specific drivers, not random percentages. If customer acquisition is uncertain, reduce expected conversions. If receivables are uncertain, shift actual cash collection dates. If material cost is exposed to suppliers, add the quoted range. If fulfilment is constrained, limit units sold to deliverable capacity. Show the effect on minimum weekly cash rather than comparing annual sales alone. A downside scenario is not a prophecy; it is a rehearsal for decisions. Define which expenditure can be paused, what lead time is necessary and the earliest reliable warning.

### Protect a clearly defined liquidity buffer

A meaningful buffer is determined by the obligations that cannot safely be postponed. Consider payroll cycles, tax liabilities, insurance renewals, contract penalties, refund exposure, supplier concentrations and the time needed to reduce costs. A fixed 'three months of expenses' rule can be a starting sensitivity, but it is not universally suitable. Document which balances are restricted, which borrowing facilities are available only conditionally and whether any funds belong economically to customers. Agree who can release the reserve and under what circumstances. Review the adequacy of the buffer as the business changes.

### Practical funding decision note

Before committing, prepare one page answering: What exact capability does the funding purchase? Which customers and revenue will support repayment or return? Which costs occur first? What evidence supports the estimates? What is the most negative weekly cash forecast? What obligations remain if the project fails? What approval is required? What measurable result determines the next investment? Revisit the note when assumptions materially change. Keep all quotes, forecasts and approvals together so a future adviser or purchaser can reconstruct the decision.

## Implementation laboratory: funding and expenditure gates

### Define a realistic starting point

Before changing anything, create a baseline record dated at the beginning of the exercise. State what is currently happening, who owns the process, what happens when it fails, and what evidence is available. For this topic, give special attention to **running short of cash before the first customer remittance**. A single recent incident is not necessarily a representative trend, so look for several examples where available. If the process is entirely new, say that there is no observed baseline and use a synthetic rehearsal rather than describing assumptions as history.

Write a one-sentence objective in the form: "Over the next four weeks we want to improve [measurable outcome] while maintaining [quality or safety requirement]." Avoid using a vague outcome such as working harder, being more professional or creating growth. A useful objective contains an observable difference and a control that prevents apparent efficiency from concealing new problems.

### Identify the people and decision boundaries

Consider the different responsibilities of the **owner, lender or investor, accountant and supplier**. In a microbusiness one individual may hold several roles, but those roles should still be conceptually separated. Identify who prepares the information, who checks it, who approves a change, who carries it out and who records the result. If outside advice is required, identify the point at which the process must pause for that advice. Avoid giving software or an AI assistant implicit authority to make legal, financial or employment commitments.

Prepare a short responsibility table with the columns *Input owner*, *Process owner*, *Independent check*, *Final approver* and *Backup contact*. For a sole trader, the independent check may be a periodic external professional review instead of a second employee. The objective is accountability, not artificial complexity.

### Build a small evidence package

An evidence package should contain the current procedure, one or more real or simulated examples, relevant figures, the expected output and a list of known exceptions. Remove confidential personal information from teaching materials. For a real business, store operational evidence in an approved private location with access limited to the people who need it. Link each figure to its source instead of copying it manually between documents without a reference.

Separate observed facts from forecasts and estimates. An observed payment date is a fact; a future customer's payment is an assumption, even when it is a reasonable one. A change in accounting classification or business circumstances may alter which facts are comparable. Mark missing information explicitly and assign responsibility for collecting it.

### Work through an exception rather than only the easy case

Normal scenarios are useful for learning the steps; exceptions test the quality of the process. Imagine that **running short of cash before the first customer remittance** occurs just before an important customer or statutory deadline. Which facts would you need immediately? Which decisions can be made under the existing authority? Which tasks could be paused safely? How would the customer, supplier, employee or adviser be informed where appropriate? What written record would be necessary to explain the response later?

Run the situation twice. First follow the present procedure. Then follow the improved procedure and compare clarity, elapsed time, error risk and confidence in the information. Note where the new approach merely moves work to another person. An effective improvement should not reduce one person's workload by hiding costs or risk elsewhere.

### Choose a review metric and define it precisely

The principal indicator for this exercise is **lowest weekly cash forecast and unrestricted cash after commitments**. State the numerator, denominator, measurement period and data source where applicable. Decide what level of movement would be meaningful before seeing the results. Pair a speed or output measure with at least one quality indicator. For example, a process that closes actions more quickly but increases uncorrected mistakes has not clearly improved.

Review results using a small table: *Period*, *Baseline or target*, *Actual*, *Difference*, *Reason*, *Corrective action* and *Owner*. Do not infer causation from a single good week. Demand, seasonality, staff absence or unusual orders can affect outcomes independently of the process change.

### Put an approval gate before an irreversible action

The major decision boundary is **signing leases, loans or personal guarantees**. Before crossing it, prepare a concise decision note: the available options, evidence, total cash exposure, dependencies, legal or customer obligations, reversibility, downside risk and the person authorised to approve. If information is missing, a smaller pilot may be preferable to a permanent commitment. Refusing to invent an uncertain input is a sign of a trustworthy process.

Agree a review date and an exit rule. An exit rule might limit an experiment to a fixed amount of money, a defined sample size or a certain number of weeks. The rule should be proportionate to the risk rather than copied from another business. Keep written approval evidence for consequential commitments and use specialist advice where required.

### Complete a four-week practice cycle

**First week:** Describe the present method and collect baseline evidence. Do not redesign everything at once. Check whether the records can support **lowest weekly cash forecast and unrestricted cash after commitments** and choose one narrow intervention.

**Second week:** Create the new procedure, checklist or template. Test it on a simple example and on a difficult exception. Document each correction and identify whether missing inputs or ambiguous responsibility caused the problem.

**Third week:** Conduct a limited live pilot where appropriate and authorised. An example of a suitably narrow outcome for this topic is **funding a reversible pilot instead of a full launch**. Measure time, quality and unintended consequences. Keep the ability to return to the prior process if the pilot fails.

**Fourth week:** Compare results, collect feedback from the people affected and write a decision: adopt, refine, retest or stop. Assign an owner and future review date. If no clear benefit appears, keep the evidence but do not claim success. The purpose of a pilot is to learn accurately, not to defend the original idea.

### Reflection and transfer

Explain the principle to someone who has not read the chapter, without using jargon. Ask them to reproduce the calculation, review or process from your instructions. Any point requiring your verbal rescue is an opportunity to improve the documentation. Then consider where the same logic applies in another process: setting clear inputs, acknowledging uncertainty, naming decision rights, measuring outcomes and checking the effect on both money and people.

**Completion evidence:** a dated baseline, one-page procedure, exception test, recorded results and approved next action. These five records convert general business advice into a verifiable operating improvement.
