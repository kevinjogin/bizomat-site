A signed agreement and a first payment start a grant; they do not finish the work. For the next 18 months, NRIO's money is in other people's hands, spent by volunteer committees and council project teams on roofs, kitchens and change rooms. Monitoring tells NRIO, early enough to act, whether the money is doing what the Minister approved it for. Variations let an agreement follow real events without quietly becoming a different grant. Acquittal ends the grant with evidence rather than an assumption.

This is an illustration. By September 2027 the 31 Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) agreements were executed and the first instalments paid. Four officers could not watch 31 projects equally closely, and they should not try. This chapter follows Sam Reid and Ben Walker as they set monitoring by risk, handle requests to change agreements and close grants. It also shows how an unauthorised "yes" in an email surfaced sixteen months later.

## Monitoring in proportion to risk

Paragraph 14.12 of the [CGRPs](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2024L00854/latest/text) says a well-drafted agreement is not enough on its own. Officials should support it with "ongoing communication, active grants management and performance monitoring requirements, which are proportional to the risks involved". The paragraph adds that monitoring payments and progress gives a measure of assurance that the money has been spent for its intended purposes.

Proportion works both ways. Paragraph 9.5 says "There are no mandatory acquittal or reporting requirements for grantees in the CGRPs." It asks officials to set the volume, detail and frequency of reporting in proportion to the risks and the outcomes sought. Heavy reporting from a small volunteer group wastes its time and NRIO's. Light reporting from a struggling project risks public money.

This is an illustration. The RCFP agreement fixes what every grantee must report: the mid-point progress report, and the final report and acquittal. It also lets NRIO ask for information it reasonably needs and visit the site on reasonable notice. What varies is how closely NRIO watches between reports. Ben rates each grantee when its agreement is executed. The band sets the monitoring, and the rating is reviewed at each report and whenever something changes.

## Rating a grantee on NRIO's 5 × 5 matrix

This is an illustration. NRIO's risk framework rates likelihood and consequence from 1 to 5 and multiplies them. Scores of 1 to 6 are Low, 7 to 14 Medium and 15 to 25 High. For grants, the Regional Programs Branch uses its own descriptors. The event it rates is "the project is not delivered as agreed": finished late, short of money, or different from the project approved.

Likelihood:

- 1, rare: an experienced grantee, with funding secured and approvals in hand;
- 2, unlikely: a capable grantee with a firm quote and only routine items open;
- 3, possible: a first project of this size, or a known uncertainty in time, cost or scope;
- 4, likely: a key condition not met, such as no builder, a missing approval or an unfunded gap;
- 5, almost certain: the project is already off track.

Consequence:

- 1, minimal: a short delay within the completion date;
- 2, minor: delay past the completion date, or up to A$25,000 of grant at risk;
- 3, moderate: A$25,000 to A$100,000 at risk, or part of the benefit lost;
- 4, major: more than A$100,000 at risk, or the facility's main benefit lost;
- 5, severe: misuse of grant money, or harm to people.

The [Risk Rating (5 × 5 matrix)](/tools/risk-score/) tool does the multiplication and applies the same three bands. Use it to check the arithmetic or to show a colleague how one factor moves the score, but rate against the branch's descriptors, not the tool's general wording.

This is an illustration. When Brindlemere Football Netball Club Inc.'s agreement was executed in July 2027, Ben rated it as follows.

```figure
type: formula
title: Rating Brindlemere at execution
caption: This is an illustration. The score ranks grantees for attention. Keep both factors on the file, because they say what kind of attention is needed.
formula: Risk score = Likelihood × Consequence
- Likelihood | 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain), on the branch's grant descriptors
- Consequence | 1 (minimal) to 5 (severe), on the branch's grant descriptors
- Bands | Low 1 to 6, Medium 7 to 14, High 15 to 25
step: Likelihood 3, possible: the committee itself had asked for a later completion date
step: Consequence 4, major: the A$310,000 grant puts more than A$100,000 at risk
step: Risk score = 3 × 4 = 12, Medium
```

The likelihood is 3, not 2, because of what happened at signing (Chapter 7). The committee had written in a completion date of 30 June 2029. That was not misconduct, but it was evidence that the club's own plan might not fit 18 months, and ratings should use evidence like that. Keep both numbers on the file, not just the product. The score decides how much attention a grantee gets; the factors decide what kind.

## Progress reports and site visits

This is an illustration. The mid-point progress report is where NRIO decides whether to pay the second 40 per cent, so it has to show enough to decide. RCFP reports cover progress against each deliverable in the schedule, spending to date by the schedule's budget lines, photos, any change to the timetable or the risks and, for councils, the council's own spending.

Ben assesses each report against the schedule, line by line. Sam accepts the milestone under NRIO's instructions, which give the Grants Program Manager that power but not the power to vary an agreement. Nadia Haddad raises the payment and the CFO area releases it. Accepting a report is a decision, so the file records what was checked, what was queried and why the milestone was met.

Three habits make reports useful:

- read spending against the schedule's budget lines, not against the total, because a report that is on budget overall can hide money spent on items the agreement does not cover;
- treat any statement that NRIO "agreed" to something as a question, and find the variation the Branch Manager approved on the file;
- query early, because a gap noticed at the mid-point can be fixed and the same gap at acquittal becomes a dispute.

A site visit tests what a report says. Plan it: what to see, which claims to check and which questions to ask. Take photos, compare the work with the schedule, and write a dated, signed note for the file within five working days. A site visit is also where grantees ask for changes in person. An officer on site can listen and take the request back. Only the Branch Manager can agree to it.

## Variations: who approves and what cannot change

A variation changes the agreement: its dates, deliverables, budget lines, conditions or amount. Section 23 of the PGPA Act gives the power to vary arrangements alongside the power to enter into them. This is an illustration. At NRIO only the Branch Manager may approve a variation to an RCFP agreement, in writing, within the A$500,000 delegation. Nobody else's agreement, however senior or well meant, changes the grant.

The CGRPs set the principles, all as better practice. Paragraph 14.13 says value with relevant money should be considered in recommending and approving a variation, and adds: "Variations should not be used as an alternative to appropriately planning a new grant opportunity." Paragraph 14.14 says that, as a general rule, agreements should be varied only where necessary and should "not significantly change the scope of the initial agreement". Under paragraph 14.16, a pattern of variations should prompt officials to consider longer agreements or a new process.

In a competitive round, that means some changes cannot be made by variation at all. Each grant was won on merit, against 176 other eligible applications, on the project described. A change that would have altered the score, the ranking or the amount is not an administrative matter.

```figure
type: decision
title: Can this RCFP variation be approved, and by whom?
question: Does the change keep the project, the money and the round's rules as they were when the Minister approved the grant?
- Adds money | Not by variation | New spending is the Minister's decision, after written advice, and no grant may exceed A$500,000
- Changes what was assessed | Not by variation | If it would have altered the score, the ranking or the amount, it is a merit question
- Breaks a rule every applicant faced | Not by variation | Completion within 18 months of the agreement; a council's 25 per cent minimum contribution
- Unclear | Hold the answer until it is clear | Ben assesses, legal advises on the agreement, and the grantee hears only that the Branch Manager will decide
- *Within the project, the money and the rules | The Branch Manager approves in writing | Value with relevant money considered (CGRPs 14.13), with the reasons on the file
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's rules are the fictional agency's own. Only the delegate's written approval varies an agreement; an officer's email does not.
```

This is an illustration. Two Round 1 requests show the difference.

On Thursday 2 March 2028, Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. asked to move its mid-point report from 15 March to Friday 12 May 2028, because the asbestos removal took three weeks longer than planned. The completion date of 15 December 2028 and the money were unchanged. Ben checked the request against the schedule, Sam recommended it and the Branch Manager approved it in writing. Ivy Chen moved the A$168,000 mid-point instalment in the forecast from April to June 2028, still in 2027–28. A date change within the same financial year is a minor variation that need not go on GrantConnect, although it still needs the delegate's approval and a record on the file (Chapter 9).

In May 2028 the Shire of Eastbrook's tenders came in A$60,000 above its estimate, and the council asked whether NRIO could add that amount. It could not. The grant was already A$500,000, the maximum in the guidelines and the top of the Branch Manager's delegation. Extra money would also be new spending the Minister had not approved, and Round 1 had only A$10.0 million − A$9.98 million = A$20,000 unallocated. The council funded the gap itself. Its contribution rose from A$300,000 to A$360,000 of an A$860,000 project, or 41.9 per cent, still well above the 25 per cent minimum.

## Underspends

This is an illustration. Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. finished its workshop fit-out for A$79,000, which is A$6,000 less than its A$85,000 grant. The RCFP agreement pays eligible costs actually incurred, up to the grant amount. So the question is not whether the shed may keep the A$6,000. It is whether the money is spent on the project or not paid at all.

There are two proper answers. If, before its completion date, the shed asks to spend the money within the project, on more of the equipment the schedule describes, the Branch Manager can consider that as a variation, weighing value with relevant money as paragraph 14.13 asks. Otherwise NRIO pays only what was spent. The shed had received A$34,000 + A$34,000 = A$68,000, so the final instalment is A$79,000 − A$68,000 = A$11,000 instead of A$17,000.

The wrong answers are to let a grantee spend the remainder on anything it likes, or to discover after the final payment that NRIO has paid more than the project cost. Holding 20 per cent back until the final report is approved is what turns an underspend into a smaller payment rather than a debt.

When an underspend is confirmed, close that part of the commitment in the finance system the same week, so the forecast stops showing money that will never be paid (Chapter 7). What happens to released money is for the Branch Manager to decide with the CFO area, not for the grants team.

## Misuse and recovering money

Not every problem in an acquittal is misconduct. A grantee that buys something the agreement does not cover, in good faith, needs a decision under the agreement. A grantee whose invoices are false, or whose money has gone where it cannot explain, needs NRIO's fraud process. Tell the two apart before you act. When in doubt, treat it as the second.

Section 10 of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text) requires the accountable authority to have mechanisms for detecting fraud and corruption, including ways for people to report suspicions confidentially, and for investigating or otherwise responding to them. Paragraph 15.5 of the CGRPs adds: "Officials should be aware of the procedures to follow when fraud, corruption or misappropriation is suspected." In practice, report the suspicion through NRIO's process, keep the evidence as it is, do not question the grantee or alert anyone involved, and release no further payment without advice.

The CGRPs do not deal with recovering grant money; the duty comes from the PGPA Rule. If money is repayable under the agreement, it is a debt to the Commonwealth. Section 11 says the accountable authority of a non-corporate Commonwealth entity "must pursue recovery of each debt for which the accountable authority is responsible". There are three exceptions: the accountable authority considers recovery uneconomical, it is satisfied the debt is not legally recoverable, or the debt has been written off as authorised by an Act. Not pursuing a debt is therefore a decision with reasons, made by the official NRIO's instructions name, and recorded.

Do not confuse recovery with set-off. Section 64 of the PGPA Act lets the Finance Minister authorise setting off an amount a person owes the Commonwealth against an amount the Commonwealth owes that person. It is a separate power, not the duty to recover, so ask the CFO area before netting one amount against another. Not paying an instalment that the agreement does not make payable is different again. What the agreement allows is a question for NRIO's legal area.

## Acquittal and closing the grant

Acquittal is the grantee's account of how the grant was spent, and NRIO's check of that account. Paragraph 14.12 says adequate, well-documented arrangements for financial accountability are the basis of effective acquittal, and that "Reliable, timely and adequate evidence is required" to show the grant was spent as the agreement requires. It also says officials should balance the stringency of acquittal against the risk and the cost of compliance.

This is an illustration. RCFP final reports and acquittals are due by 31 March 2029. Each one covers what was delivered against the schedule and evidence that the work is finished and any required approvals signed off. It includes a statement of spending by the schedule's budget lines, certified by the grantee's authorised officers, and, for councils, the council's own spending. The evidence rises with the risk band, as the monitoring table shows.

```figure
type: checklist
title: Before the final payment and closure
caption: This is an illustration. NRIO's acquittal check. The question-marked item decides whether the matter goes to the fraud process.
- Deliverables match the schedule | photos, and the site visit note for Medium and High grantees
- Spending statement by budget line | certified by the grantee's authorised officers
- Every line matches the schedule or an approved variation | the Branch Manager's approval on the file, not a mention of one
- Council contribution shown | at least 25 per cent of eligible project costs
- Final instalment = eligible spending less instalments paid | never more than the 20 per cent in the schedule
- Commitment closed and closing letter sent | the forecast stops carrying money that will not be paid
- x Accepting "NRIO agreed" without the approved variation | only the Branch Manager's written approval varies an agreement
- ? Does anything suggest misuse rather than error | if so, NRIO's fraud process comes before any conversation with the grantee
```

Closing is a short list of things that are easy to leave undone. Pay the final instalment, or record why less is paid. Close the commitment. Send the grantee a letter saying the grant is closed. Check that every reportable variation is on GrantConnect (Chapter 9). File the decision, its reasons and the evidence together. A grant file that ends with a payment and no closing note leaves the next reader to guess whether the grant was finished.

## When it goes wrong: the email that varied an agreement

This is an illustration. Brindlemere's change-room tenders came in on Friday 15 October 2027, A$41,000 under the budget in its schedule. On Friday 19 November the club's president emailed Sam asking to use the savings on "related improvements at the ground". Sam had worked closely with the club since the signing problem in July. He replied the same day: fine in principle, keep it related to the facilities and show it in the reports. He held no power to vary agreements; NRIO's instructions give it to the Branch Manager. Sam did not see his reply as deciding anything, so it stayed in his inbox and never reached the grant file.

The mid-point report arrived on Wednesday 19 April 2028. The change rooms were on track, and the report said NRIO had agreed the savings could be used on related improvements. Ben flagged the sentence. Sam, remembering his email, accepted the milestone, and the second instalment of A$124,000 was paid. Between August and October 2028 the club spent the A$41,000 on an electronic scoreboard (A$24,000), a ride-on mower (A$11,500), and branded volunteer shirts and an opening-day event (A$5,500).

On Friday 16 March 2029, Ben's acquittal check matched every line of the club's statement against the schedule. A$24,000 + A$11,500 + A$5,500 = A$41,000 had been spent on items the schedule did not contain.

On legal advice, the Branch Manager considered the items as the variation request they should have been in November 2027. None was part of the change-room upgrade that the panel had assessed and the Minister had approved, and the mower, the shirts and the event were not part of any facility. The Branch Manager declined the request on Friday 13 April 2029. The club's spending on the agreed project was A$310,000 − A$41,000 = A$269,000, and NRIO had paid A$124,000 + A$124,000 = A$248,000. The final instalment was A$269,000 − A$248,000 = A$21,000 instead of A$62,000, and the A$41,000 difference left the program's commitments.

No money had to be recovered, but only because 20 per cent was still held. Everything else went wrong. The club had spent A$41,000 of its own money relying on an NRIO officer's written word. NRIO wrote to the club with its reasons and an apology, and its legal area advised on the club's position. The matter was reported through NRIO's process for possible non-compliance with the finance law. Almost seventeen months passed between the email and the decision, by which time the scoreboard was installed and nothing could be undone.

The controls that would have caught it are ordinary. Any request to change an agreement goes on the grant file the day it arrives, and the only answer an officer gives is that the Branch Manager will decide. The officer who gave an informal answer does not accept the report that relies on it. A mid-point check that looks for the approved variation behind every claim of NRIO's agreement would have stopped this in April 2028, months before the money was spent. Sam had joined from a policy role in mid-2026, and nobody had walked him through the branch's delegations. NRIO now does that for every new starter in their first week.

## Exercise: monitoring four grantees

This is an illustration. On Monday 6 September 2027, Ben rates four grantees and proposes their monitoring to Sam. Use the branch's descriptors and NRIO's bands above.

| Grantee | Grant | What Ben knows |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. | A$420,000 | Fixed-price builder's quote; asbestos found in the roof and its removal costed; the hall is the town's only public venue |
| Shire of Eastbrook | A$500,000 | Council projects team that has delivered similar buildings; building approval granted in August 2027; A$300,000 contribution in the adopted council budget |
| Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. | A$85,000 | Volunteer group; its first grant above A$20,000; licensed electrician not yet engaged; key equipment on a 12-week order |
| Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. | A$140,000 | Its builder withdrew in August 2027; the new quote is A$22,000 above the kitchen budget; the association has not said how it will fund the gap |

Rate each grantee, set its monitoring, and name one trigger that would change its rating.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration.

| Grantee | Likelihood | Consequence | Score and band |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shire of Eastbrook | 1: experienced, funding secured, approval in hand | 4: more than A$100,000 at risk | 1 × 4 = 4, Low |
| Kellerton Hall Committee Inc. | 2: capable, firm quote, the known asbestos issue costed | 4: more than A$100,000 at risk, and the town's only venue | 2 × 4 = 8, Medium |
| Tallowood Ridge Men's Shed Inc. | 3: first project of this size, with the electrician and equipment still open | 3: A$85,000 is between A$25,000 and A$100,000 | 3 × 3 = 9, Medium |
| Corran Bay Progress Association Inc. | 4: a key condition not met, with no builder and an unfunded gap | 4: more than A$100,000 at risk | 4 × 4 = 16, High |

Each score sits inside its band: 4 is within Low (1 to 6), 8 and 9 are within Medium (7 to 14), and 16 is within High (15 to 25).

The monitoring follows the band.

- **Eastbrook (Low).** The reports in the agreement, and a call when each is due. Each report shows the council's own spending, because the 25 per cent minimum applied to every council applicant; Eastbrook's plan is A$300,000 of A$800,000, or 37.5 per cent. Trigger: any change to the council's budget for the project. A Low score with a consequence of 4 is not a reason to stop looking. It is a reason to look at the right thing.
- **Kellerton (Medium).** A call each quarter, photos with each report, and one site visit before the mid-point report is accepted. Trigger: asbestos removal costing more than the quote allows, or the builder's program slipping past the mid-point date.
- **Tallowood Ridge (Medium).** A call each quarter, starting with help on the report template, photos with each report, and a site visit at completion. Trigger: no licensed electrician engaged by the end of October 2027.
- **Corran Bay (High).** A short monthly update, a site visit before works start, and written evidence that a builder is engaged and the A$22,000 gap is funded before the mid-point report can be accepted. The Director hears at once, the Branch Manager within two working days, and the rating is reviewed monthly.

Corran Bay was approved against officials' recommendation, in the Minister's own electorate (Chapter 6). That history does not set its rating, and it must not soften or harden its monitoring. The evidence does: no builder and an unfunded gap. Once the association signs a builder and shows the gap is funded, the likelihood falls to 2 and the score to 2 × 4 = 8, Medium, and its monitoring steps down with it. Applying the same method to every grantee is what makes each rating defensible.
