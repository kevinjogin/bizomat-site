## About this book

Tanks, bins and hoppers are among the most common things a fabrication shop makes, and almost every question about them is a calculation. How much does it hold, and how much when it is filled to the working level? How high must it be to hold 1,500 litres? What does the dipstick read at 1,000 litres? What does it weigh full, and what does each leg carry? How much sheet does it take, and what shape is the flat pattern of its cone? Each answer is geometry that has been known for centuries, and each is easy to get wrong by a unit, a radius used as a diameter, or a formula applied to a shape it does not fit.

This book works through those calculations in the order a tank is designed, drawn and made. It starts with capacity and what "full" means, then covers prisms and cylinders, cones and frustums, the prismoidal formula, partly filled tanks and calibration tables, mass and support loads, sheet area, and the patterns for cones, frustums and pyramid hoppers. It ends with the checks a tank should pass before it is cut.

It suits designers, drafters, fabricators and process engineers. It assumes school geometry and a calculator or spreadsheet. The Library’s collection chapter on areas, volumes and masses for the workshop gives a broad introduction; this book goes deep on one family of products.

## The running case

Every chapter follows one fictional job. This is an illustration. A fabricator builds equipment for a small food processor: a stainless steel holding tank, TK-1, with an internal diameter of 1,200 mm, a straight shell 1,500 mm high and a conical bottom 400 mm deep draining to a 100 mm outlet; and a square powder hopper, HP-400, 1,000 × 1,000 mm at the top, tapering over 900 mm to a 250 × 250 mm outlet, with a 300 mm straight upstand above.

The business, the processor and the equipment are invented; none describes BIZOMAT work or a real product. Prices are illustrations in Australian dollars, excluding GST.

## Source limits and professional responsibility

The geometry in this book is exact, and every calculation was checked by running it. The structural design of a tank or bin, its wall thickness, its supports, its behaviour under pressure, vacuum, wind or the flow of a bulk solid, is a separate engineering task. Tanks for flammable and combustible liquids, pressure vessels and large bins are governed by Australian Standards such as AS 1692 for steel tanks for flammable and combustible liquids and AS 1210 for pressure vessels, and by the regulator in each state. This book is educational. Real tanks and hoppers must be designed and approved by a competent engineer to the standards that apply.
