A supplier emails to say it "cannot continue at the agreed price". A customer announces it is "putting the project on hold indefinitely" and stops answering calls. A subcontractor walks off site, leaving tools behind and a vague message about other commitments. Nothing in the contract has yet fallen due, or perhaps only a minor obligation has been missed, but the message is clear enough: the other side may not be going to perform.

Situations like these are where commercial relationships are most often damaged by a hasty response. Treating the message as the end of the contract and moving on may be exactly right. It may also be a serious mistake, because if the other party's conduct did not legally amount to a refusal to perform, the business that terminates may become the party in breach. Doing nothing can also be costly: losses grow, alternatives disappear and the business may lose the right to terminate later.

This article explains, in general terms, what **repudiation** and **anticipatory breach** mean, the choice the innocent party faces, the risks on both sides of that choice and a practical way to respond when a counterparty wavers. It is general information, not legal advice. Termination decisions on significant contracts should be made with legal advice, because the consequences of getting them wrong can be larger than the original dispute.

## What repudiation means

In general terms, a party **repudiates** a contract when, by words or conduct, it shows that it does not intend, or is unable, to perform the contract, or to perform it in a way that is essential to the bargain. The test is usually objective. It asks how a reasonable person in the position of the other party would understand the conduct, not what the repudiating party privately intended.

Repudiation can take several forms:

- **An express refusal** to perform, such as "we will not deliver" or "we are not proceeding".
- **Insisting on terms the contract does not contain**, as a condition of performing, such as demanding a higher price than agreed, where the insistence is clear and unqualified.
- **Conduct showing inability to perform**, such as abandoning the work, selling the asset that was to be delivered or entering a state where performance is plainly impossible.
- **A series of breaches** that together show the party no longer regards itself as bound.

When repudiation happens before the time for performance has arrived, it is often called **anticipatory breach**. The innocent party does not have to wait for the delivery date to pass to know the contract will not be honoured; it can respond to the repudiation itself.

### What is not repudiation

The threshold is high. Many statements that feel like repudiation do not reach it:

- A genuine request to renegotiate, without a refusal to perform the existing terms.
- A bona fide dispute about what the contract means, where the party says it will perform as it understands the contract, even if that understanding turns out to be wrong. Courts are often reluctant to treat an honest but mistaken interpretation as repudiation, although persisting in a clearly wrong position can cross the line.
- Expressions of difficulty, such as "we are struggling to source materials", without a refusal.
- Minor breaches of obligations that are not essential and do not show an unwillingness to be bound.

This is why the exact words and context matter. "We cannot hold this price" may be a negotiating position. "We will not supply unless you pay the new price" may be repudiation.

## Breach, serious breach and essential terms

Repudiation sits alongside other bases for terminating a contract at common law. Australian law, as summarised by the High Court in *Koompahtoo Local Aboriginal Land Council v Sanpine Pty Ltd* (2007), generally recognises a right to terminate:

- where a party breaches an **essential term** (a condition), meaning a term so important that the other party would not have entered the contract without assurance of strict performance;
- where a party commits a **sufficiently serious breach** of an intermediate term, one that substantially deprives the other party of the benefit of the contract; and
- where a party **repudiates** the contract.

Whether a term is essential depends on the contract and its context. Contracts sometimes say expressly that certain obligations are essential, or that "time is of the essence" for a particular date. Without such words, the classification can be uncertain, which is one reason contracts usually include express termination clauses.

### Contractual termination rights

Most commercial contracts include their own termination procedure. A common pattern is a **notice to remedy** or **show cause** process: the innocent party gives written notice identifying the breach and requiring it to be remedied within a set period, and may terminate if it is not. These clauses often operate alongside common law rights, but not always; some contracts are drafted to limit or replace them.

Contractual procedures must be followed exactly. Notices usually need to be in a particular form, delivered to a particular address, identify the breach precisely and allow the full period. Errors in notices are one of the most common reasons terminations fail.

## The innocent party's election

When a counterparty repudiates, the innocent party generally has a choice, often described as an **election**:

| Option | What it means | Consequences |
|---|---|---|
| Accept the repudiation and terminate | Treat the contract as at an end and stop performing | Future obligations end; accrued rights remain; damages may be claimed for loss of the bargain |
| Affirm the contract | Treat the contract as continuing and insist on performance | Both parties remain bound; the innocent party must keep performing its own obligations; damages for the breach may still be claimed |

Repudiation does not end a contract by itself. The contract ends only if the innocent party accepts the repudiation, usually by clear communication or by unequivocal conduct inconsistent with the contract continuing. Until then, the repudiating party may be able to withdraw its repudiation and perform.

Several features of the election matter in practice.

**Affirmation can happen by accident.** If the innocent party, knowing of the repudiation, continues to demand performance, accepts further performance or behaves as though the contract continues, it may be taken to have affirmed. Once affirmed, the right to terminate for that repudiation may be lost, although a later repudiation or breach can create a new right.

**Silence and delay are risky.** The innocent party is usually allowed a reasonable time to decide, and courts recognise that a business may need to investigate. Prolonged inaction can be read as affirmation, and losses may grow in the meantime.

**Reserving rights helps, but is not magic.** Letters that "reserve all rights" while continuing to deal with the other party can help preserve options, but they do not stop conduct from amounting to affirmation if the conduct is clearly inconsistent with termination.

**Affirming has costs.** A business that affirms must keep performing its own obligations. It cannot insist the other side perform while itself withholding performance without a contractual basis.

## The danger of wrongful termination

The most serious risk in this area is terminating when the law does not support it. If a business treats the other party's conduct as repudiation, stops performing and engages a replacement, and a court later finds that the conduct did not amount to repudiation, the business that terminated may itself have repudiated the contract. The other party may then accept that repudiation and claim damages, potentially including the profit it would have made on the whole contract.

Wrongful termination commonly arises from:

- treating a request to renegotiate as a refusal to perform;
- terminating for a breach that was not of an essential term and not serious enough;
- defective notices under a contractual termination clause;
- terminating before a notice period has expired; or
- relying on a ground that the business had earlier waived by its conduct.

Because the consequences can be severe, termination should be a decision, not a reaction. The [how contracts end](/insights/how-contracts-end/) article sets out the broader ways contracts come to an end and why work stopping is not the same as a contract ending.

## Damages after termination

If the innocent party validly terminates for repudiation or serious breach, it can generally claim damages for **loss of the bargain**, meaning the difference between its position had the contract been performed and its actual position. In a supply contract, this may be the extra cost of buying the goods elsewhere. In a construction or service contract, it may include the extra cost of completing the work with another contractor, less the unpaid contract price.

The innocent party is expected to take reasonable steps to reduce its loss, sometimes called **mitigation**. It cannot recover losses it could reasonably have avoided, such as by declining a reasonable replacement offer. Reasonable costs incurred in mitigating are generally recoverable. The [when a contract is broken](/insights/when-a-contract-is-broken/) article explains how compensation is calculated and the common mistakes in preparing claims.

## A disciplined way to respond

When a counterparty signals it may not perform, a structured response protects the business on both sides of the risk.

1. **Capture the evidence immediately.** Save the email, take notes of the call, record who said what and when. The exact words will matter.
2. **Read the contract.** Identify the obligations at stake, any termination clause, notice requirements, essential-term or time-of-the-essence provisions, and any dispute resolution procedure that must be followed first.
3. **Seek clarification in writing.** A short, neutral letter asking the other party to confirm whether it will perform its obligations on the agreed terms by a stated date can turn an ambiguous message into a clear position. If it confirms it will not perform, the repudiation is clearer. If it confirms it will, the contract continues and the record shows the business acted reasonably.
4. **Keep performing your own obligations, unless advised otherwise.** Withholding performance prematurely can undermine your position.
5. **Assess the commercial picture.** What would termination cost? Can you replace the supplier or contractor, at what price and how quickly? Would a negotiated variation or exit be better than litigation?
6. **Decide and communicate clearly.** If terminating, do so in writing, identify the basis and follow every contractual step. If affirming, say so and set out what performance you require.
7. **Mitigate.** Take reasonable steps to limit the loss, and keep records of what you did and what it cost.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The businesses, figures and events are hypothetical.

A small food manufacturer has a twelve-month contract to buy 40,000 printed cartons a month from a packaging supplier at $0.42 each, with deliveries on the first business day of each month. In month four, the supplier emails: "Due to board price increases we can no longer supply at $0.42. From next month the price will be $0.51." The manufacturer's buyer is tempted to reply "Contract terminated" and place orders with a competitor quoting $0.47.

A more careful response starts by asking whether the email is a refusal to perform at the contract price or an attempt to renegotiate. The manufacturer writes back: "Our contract fixes the price at $0.42 until the end of the term. Please confirm by Friday that next month's delivery will be supplied at the contract price. We reserve our rights." The supplier replies that it will not deliver unless the new price is accepted.

The position is now much clearer. An unqualified refusal to perform the contract at its price, for the rest of the term, points strongly towards repudiation. With advice, the manufacturer accepts the repudiation in writing and contracts with the competitor at $0.47. Its damages claim is based on the difference: $0.05 per carton, multiplied by 40,000 cartons a month over the remaining eight months, which is 320,000 cartons and $16,000, plus any reasonable costs of arranging the replacement supply, less anything it saved. It keeps quotes and correspondence showing that the replacement price was a reasonable market price, which supports its mitigation.

Had the manufacturer terminated on the first email, the supplier might have argued it was only proposing a price change, and that it was the manufacturer that ended the contract.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Pause before reacting.** The first message is rarely the final position. A clarifying letter costs little and often decides the legal outcome.
- **Know which terms are essential.** If certain dates or obligations are critical to your business, say so in the contract, for example by making time of the essence for key deliveries.
- **Use clear termination clauses.** A notice-to-remedy process with defined periods makes termination more predictable for both sides.
- **Keep performing while you decide.** Unless advised otherwise, do not stop paying or supplying because the other side is wavering.
- **Line up alternatives quietly.** Understanding replacement options helps you mitigate and helps you decide whether termination is commercially sensible.
- **Consider a negotiated exit.** Sometimes a supplier genuinely cannot continue. A controlled exit with an agreed transition may be better than a damages claim against a business that may not be able to pay.

## Common mistakes

- **Terminating on the first sign of trouble.** Ambiguous statements are not always repudiation, and wrongful termination can reverse the parties' positions.
- **Affirming by accident.** Continuing to demand and accept performance after a clear repudiation can lose the right to terminate for it.
- **Defective notices.** Wrong address, wrong period or a vague description of the breach can invalidate a contractual termination.
- **Stopping your own performance too early.** Withholding payment or supply without a contractual basis can itself be a breach.
- **Ignoring mitigation.** Losses that could reasonably have been avoided are generally not recoverable.
- **Letting emotion set the timetable.** Termination decisions made in anger are often expensive.

## Questions to ask

- What exactly did the other party say or do, and do we have a record of it?
- Is this a refusal to perform, an inability to perform, or an attempt to renegotiate?
- Which obligations are essential under this contract, and does the contract make time of the essence anywhere?
- What does the termination clause require, and have we followed it exactly?
- Have we done anything that could be seen as affirming the contract?
- If we terminate, what will replacement supply or completion cost, and can we prove it was reasonable?
- Would a negotiated exit serve us better than a claim?

## Bringing it together

Repudiation occurs when a party shows, by words or conduct, that it will not or cannot perform the contract in an essential way. When it happens, the innocent party has a choice: accept the repudiation and terminate, or affirm the contract and insist on performance. Both choices carry risk. Terminating without a sound basis can make the innocent party the one in breach. Delay or inconsistent conduct can affirm the contract and lose the right to terminate.

A disciplined response protects the business on both sides: capture the evidence, read the contract, seek written clarification, keep performing while deciding, assess the commercial picture, decide clearly and mitigate the loss.
