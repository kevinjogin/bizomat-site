Every agency that answers to Parliament will sometimes put something wrong on the record. A witness joins two numbers under pressure, an answer uses last month's figure, a report counts something new without saying so. The error itself is rarely what does the damage. The damage comes from how long it stands, how often it is repeated and who finds it first.

This is an illustration. At the Budget estimates hearing on Wednesday 2 June 2027, NRIO's Chief Executive Officer (CEO) told a Senate legislation committee: "Every one of the 186 applications was assessed." In fact 9 of the 186 applications to Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program (RCFP) were ineligible and were never assessed; 177 were (Chapter 2). Clare Whitfield spotted the error that evening, and the CEO's correcting letter went to the committee on Friday 4 June 2027. This chapter follows those two days and what NRIO did next.

## Why prompt correction matters

Section 13(9) of the [Public Service Act 1999](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00538/latest/text) says an APS employee "must not provide false or misleading information in response to a request for information that is made for official purposes" in connection with their APS employment. Section 14(1) binds agency heads to the Code of Conduct in the same way. Section 13(1) adds a broader duty: an employee "must behave honestly and with integrity in connection with APS employment".

Section 13 does not mention correction in so many words, and whether a particular mistake breaches the Code is a question for the agency's own processes. But an honest slip corrected promptly is a different thing from a wrong statement left standing after it is known to be wrong. The first is a mistake. The second starts to look like a choice. In practice the duty comes down to this: once you know that a committee, an auditor or the Ombudsman is relying on wrong information your agency gave, correct it as soon as you are sure of the right facts.

Speed matters for three more reasons. Committees use evidence to question other witnesses, write reports and test the government, so a wrong fact can shape all three. Each repetition multiplies the work: one wrong number can travel into answers to questions on notice, a submission, an audit response and an annual report. And an agency that corrects itself first is more likely to be believed next time than one corrected by someone else. The APS Values point the same way. The APS is "open and accountable" (s 10(4)) and gives advice that is "frank, honest, timely and based on the best available evidence" (s 10(5)).

## Spotting the error

Errors surface in predictable places: at the hearing, in the transcript, in later questions on notice, in an auditor's draft, in a complaint or in a journalist's call. The earliest is the cheapest, so look there first.

This is an illustration. At NRIO, the unit reviews each hearing the same evening. Ravi Menon's log from the table and Lucy Tran's second log from the broadcast are reconciled (Chapter 2), and every number officials gave is read against the brief and its sources. On 2 June, Clare had watched the RCFP questions and noted the CEO's words. Reading her note against the RCFP brief, she saw the problem at once. The brief gave 186 received on its front page, and 9 ineligible and 177 assessed on page three. The answer had joined "186" to "assessed".

Before treating something as an error, confirm three things:

- **That it was said.** Check the log against the transcript when it is available, and until then against a second person's record.
- **That it is wrong.** Check the right figure against its source, with the person who owns it.
- **That it matters.** A misspoken date that nobody could rely on differs from a wrong fact about whether a grants round was run on its rules. When in doubt, treat it as material: an unnecessary correction costs a letter.

Then tell the person who said it, the same day if you can.

This is an illustration. Lucy, who had watched the broadcast, confirmed the words, and Clare rang the CEO that evening.

A transcript records what was said. If the words were recorded correctly but were wrong, the fix is a correction of the evidence, not of the transcript. Follow the committee's instructions for raising each.

## Who corrects what

The rule is simple: whoever made a statement corrects it, through the channel in which it was made.

A witness who realises during a hearing that an answer was wrong should correct it at the table. After the hearing, the witness writes to the committee.

An answer to a question on notice is corrected by a corrected answer. It should go through the same clearance as the original, including the Minister's office, with a note saying what changed and why (Chapter 3). A submission to an inquiry is corrected by a letter to the committee.

Ministers correct their own statements. If a minister has repeated wrong information from the agency, in the chamber, at a hearing or in a media release, the agency's job is to tell the Minister's office at once, with the right facts and their source. Whether and how to correct the statement is for the minister. An agency never corrects a minister's statement on the minister's behalf.

This is an illustration. At NRIO, the CEO signs any correction of evidence given by NRIO officials. Had the minister at the table repeated the CEO's figure on 2 June, NRIO's part would have been a call to the Minister's office that evening with the right figures, and the correction would have been the minister's to make.

An annual report is corrected in a later annual report. If a previous report contains a significant statement of fact that has "proved to be wrong in a material respect", the next must include "information correcting the record" (section 17AH(1)(e) of the [PGPA Rule](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2014L00911/latest/text); Chapter 9). Information given to the Auditor-General or the Ombudsman is corrected in writing to them as soon as the error is known (Chapters 5 and 8).

The committee decides how it receives corrections and what it publishes, so follow its instructions or ask its secretariat. Before you write, read your agency's own instructions and the current *Government Guidelines for Official Witnesses before Parliamentary Committees and Related Matters*, published by the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet.

```figure

title: Who corrects what
question: Where was the wrong statement made?
- Evidence at a hearing | The witness corrects it | at the table if possible; otherwise by letter to the committee
- An answer to a question on notice | The agency lodges a corrected answer | through the same clearance, including the Minister's office
- A submission to an inquiry | The agency writes to the committee | saying what was wrong and what is right
- * A minister's statement | The minister corrects it | the agency tells the Minister's office at once, with the facts and their source
- An annual report | A later annual report corrects it | "information correcting the record" (PGPA Rule s 17AH(1)(e))
caption: Whoever made the statement corrects it, in the place it was made. The agency's part is to find the error and supply the right facts quickly.
```

## Two days in June

This is an illustration. Clare could have drafted a letter that night. She waited for two things: the figures confirmed from their sources by the person who owns them, and the Minister's office told before the letter went. A correction that itself needs correcting is worse than a day's delay. Waiting longer would have bought nothing. The facts were settled by Thursday morning, and every day the error stood, someone could quote it.

The alert to the Minister's office followed NRIO's usual practice (Chapter 1). The Minister answers to the Parliament for the portfolio and could be asked about the error, so the office needed to hear of it from NRIO before anyone else raised it.

## Writing the correcting letter

A correcting letter has one job: to put the right facts on the record, in the same place as the wrong ones, without creating a new problem. Keep it to one page.

```figure
type: checklist
title: A correcting letter that does its job
caption: The letter puts the right facts beside the wrong ones and adds nothing that will need correcting later.
- Name the hearing, the date and the topic | so the committee can find the evidence
- Quote the words being corrected | from the transcript, or from your own record if the transcript is not yet available, and say which
- State the correct facts in full | each number with its definition, as in the settled facts
- Say whether any other answer changes | and if none does, say that too
- Acknowledge the error in one sentence | then stop
- Sign as the witness | and send it as the committee's instructions require
- x Explain how the error happened inside the agency | the committee needs the facts, not the internal story
- x Add new claims or argue the policy | a correction is not a second chance to persuade
- ? Unsure whether an error is material | ask the parliamentary unit; the default is to correct
```

Two points need judgement. The first is scope. The letter corrects the evidence and nothing else. If the correction affects another answer, say so; if it does not, say that too, so nobody has to guess.

This is an illustration. The Senator had asked whether the 2 projects approved against officials' recommendations were assessed in the same way as the others. They were: both were among the 177. So the answer to the question asked stood, and the CEO's letter says so.

The second is tone. One sentence of regret is right. A paragraph of apology, or a defence of the round, reads as if the agency is managing the error rather than correcting it.

## The correction travels

A correction is not finished when the letter is sent. Every later document must carry the corrected facts, because the wrong version is memorable and tends to come back.

This is an illustration. NRIO's correction travelled through the rest of its year. The settled facts sheet records the CEO's letter of Friday 4 June 2027, so later answers can draw on it (Chapter 3). The answers to Q03 and Q04, which asked how many applications were received, found ineligible and assessed, refer to the letter. NRIO's submission to the Senate inquiry includes a sentence on it (Chapter 4). Months later, a paragraph in NRIO's draft response to the ANAO's proposed report said that all applications had been assessed, and Clare's read-across caught it (Chapter 5). The annual performance statements report 177 of 177 eligible applications assessed, and the annual report notes the correction in its external scrutiny section (Chapter 9).

The same discipline applies to any correction. Record it where the agency keeps its settled facts, with the date and the document that made it. When a later document touches the same subject, check it against that record. A correction made once and then contradicted by the agency's own later document can do more harm than the original error.

## A culture that finds its own errors

Errors are found early only where people feel safe reporting them. If the person who points out a mistake is treated as the problem, the next mistake will be found by someone outside the agency.

Four habits help:

- **Review the cause, not the person.** Ask what in the process let the error through.
- **Thank whoever finds an error,** including one in their own work.
- **Keep a corrections log:** what was wrong, who found it, when it was corrected and what changed as a result.
- **Correct openly from the top.** Staff copy what leaders do when they are wrong.

This is an illustration. On Monday 7 June 2027, Clare ran a short review with Ravi, Lucy, Sam and the Director, Regional Programs. It traced the error to its cause, not its speaker. The RCFP brief had been rewritten late on Tuesday 1 June to cover the Minister's decisions. To fit the front page, the key facts shrank to "186 applications received; 31 grants approved, A$9.98 million", leaving the 9 ineligible and the 177 assessed on page three. Because the hearing was the next morning, the rewritten brief skipped Ravi's number check (Chapter 2). The review blamed nobody and changed the process. No reissued brief now goes forward until Ravi or Lucy has checked its numbers, however late it is, and key facts carry each distinction (received, ineligible, assessed, approved) with its number.

The CEO set the tone by signing the correction within two days and asking for the review. The APS Value of Stewardship asks the APS to build "its capability and institutional knowledge" (s 10(6)). A corrections log, and a process changed by what it records, is that knowledge in working form.

## When it goes wrong: the correction that waited for a journalist

This is an illustration. In 2025, before NRIO kept a corrections log, an NRIO official was asked at an Estimates hearing how a A$350,000 contract to digitise NRIO's paper files had been let. The official said it had gone to open tender. It had not: NRIO had invited three suppliers to quote. A week later, reading the transcript, the official saw the mistake and mentioned it to a manager. Each assumed the other would tell the parliamentary area. Nobody wrote it down.

Five months later, a supplier that had not been invited to quote told a journalist. The journalist compared the transcript with the supplier's account and rang the Minister's office for comment. The office had not heard of the problem. The story said NRIO had misled the Senate, and NRIO's correcting letter went to the committee the following week: after the story, not before it.

The cost was out of proportion to the mistake. How the contract had been let was no longer the question. At the next hearing, Senators asked why NRIO had known for five months and said nothing, and what else in its evidence was wrong. The Minister's office had been left to answer a journalist without the facts. And an official who had made an honest slip, and had noticed it, now faced questions about whether the delay was consistent with the Code of Conduct, which NRIO had to work through.

The control is the one that worked on 2 June 2027. Anyone who thinks NRIO has given wrong information tells the parliamentary unit the same day, in writing, and is thanked for it. The unit logs it with an owner and a target date, confirms the facts with the person who owns them, alerts the Minister's office and sends the correction within days. A known error has an owner from the moment it is known.

## Exercise: draft the correcting letter

This is an illustration. It is Thursday 3 June 2027. Draft the letter the CEO will sign on Friday 4 June. You have:

- **The hearing:** the Senate legislation committee's Budget estimates hearing on Wednesday 2 June 2027.
- **The question:** a Senator asked whether the 2 projects approved against officials' recommendations had been assessed in the same way as the others.
- **The answer,** from Clare's note, confirmed by Lucy: "Every one of the 186 applications was assessed." The transcript is not yet available.
- **The settled facts:** 186 applications received, all checked against the eligibility criteria; 9 ineligible and not assessed; 177 assessed against the published criteria, including both projects approved against officials' recommendations.

Write the letter. Then say what it leaves out, who checks it before it goes and what happens after it is sent.

### Worked answer

This is an illustration. One version follows.

> Friday 4 June 2027
>
> Dear Chair
>
> **Correction of evidence: Budget estimates hearing, Wednesday 2 June 2027**
>
> At the committee's hearing on 2 June 2027, in answer to a question about Round 1 of the Regional Community Facilities Program, I said: "Every one of the 186 applications was assessed." That was wrong, and I am writing to correct it. The words are taken from NRIO's record of the hearing, because the transcript is not yet available.
>
> The correct position is:
>
> - NRIO received 186 applications for Round 1.
> - All 186 were checked against the eligibility criteria in the published guidelines. Nine were ineligible and were not assessed.
> - The other 177 applications were assessed against the published assessment criteria.
>
> The two projects approved against officials' recommendations were among the 177 assessed applications, and were assessed in the same way as the others. My answer to the question I was asked is therefore unchanged.
>
> I regret the error. NRIO will provide any further information the committee needs.
>
> Yours sincerely
>
> Chief Executive Officer, Northfield Regional Infrastructure Office

**The arithmetic.** 186 − 9 = 177 applications assessed. The 9 not assessed are 9 ÷ 186 = 0.048, or 4.8 per cent, of the applications received: a small share, but enough to make "every one" false. Both projects in question are inside the 177. The letter goes two days after the hearing, from Wednesday 2 June to Friday 4 June 2027.

**What it leaves out.**

- How the error happened. The late rewrite of the brief is NRIO's to fix, not the committee's to read (Chapter 2).
- Any claim about the quality of the round. A word such as "rigorous" invites the next question.
- The Minister's reasons for the two decisions, which are the Minister's to give.
- Anything the settled facts do not support.

**Who checks it.** Sam confirms each number from the eligibility record and the assessment report, and the Director, Regional Programs clears them. Ravi checks every number and the quoted words against their sources. The Minister's office receives the alert and the draft on Thursday. The CEO, as the witness, signs.

**After it is sent.** NRIO lodges it as the committee's instructions say, records it on the settled facts sheet and in the corrections log, and flags Q03 and Q04 so their answers refer to it. When the transcript is published, Lucy checks that the quoted words match it.

This book began with one page pinned to a wall: a year of hearings, questions, an inquiry, a complaint, an audit and a report, most of them tracing back to one grants round. Each chapter has dealt with a different body and different rules, but the work underneath has stayed the same. Know who answers for what, and keep the Minister informed. Give every body facts that are defined, sourced and consistent with what the agency has already said. Meet the dates others set and the ones the law sets. Treat audits, complaints and committee reports as evidence about how to do the work better, and close their recommendations on proof. Report results against the measures promised, including the misses. And when the record is wrong, correct it quickly, plainly and in the place it was made. An agency that works this way will still be questioned. It will also be able to answer.
