Look at a typical television remote control. It may have forty or more buttons, and most people use perhaps six. The rest add cost, complexity and confusion, and exist because someone thought they might be useful, not because users needed them. Compare that with the minimal remotes that come with many streaming devices, which have a handful of buttons and are easy for almost anyone to use.

That contrast is the essence of **user experience (UX)**: how easy, pleasant and effective it is for people to use your product, service or process. UX is often discussed in terms of websites and apps, but it applies to everything a customer touches, including physical products, instructions, shop layouts, phone systems, quotes, invoices and ordering processes. In business-to-business markets, it applies to how easy it is to specify, order, receive, install, use and maintain what you supply.

Good UX makes customers comfortable and confident. Over time, they get used to the convenience and become reluctant to go elsewhere. Poor UX frustrates them, costs them time and sends them looking for alternatives. This article explains the principles of good UX and how to apply them to physical products, shops, phone services, online channels and business processes.

## The three fundamentals: seamless, easy, repeatable

Good user experience has three qualities:

- **Seamless**: the steps flow naturally from one to the next without gaps, dead ends or unexpected obstacles.
- **Easy**: each step requires little effort, knowledge or decision-making from the user.
- **Repeatable**: because it is easy, people do it again and form a habit. That habit is a powerful source of loyalty.

Leading online retailers, ride-share apps and home-services platforms are built around these qualities. Searching, choosing, paying and receiving are all designed to be as close to effortless as possible.

## Start by removing what users do not need

Ask about every feature, option, button, form field and step: does the user need this, and does it add value for them? Unnecessary features:

- Add cost to design, build and support.
- Make the product harder to learn and use.
- Hide the features that matter.
- Create more things that can go wrong.

Look at your own product range, documents and processes with the remote-control question in mind. How many features are rarely used? How many fields on your quote request form are actually needed? How many steps in your ordering process exist only because "we have always done it that way"?

## Design from research, not opinions

A common UX failure is designing by committee: every stakeholder adds features they think are important, based on their own opinions rather than evidence about users. The result is a product like a multi-tool with too many attachments, versatile in theory and awkward in practice. A specialist tool designed for a specific job usually does that job far better.

Good UX starts with research into real users:

- **Observe** people using your product or going through your process. Where do they hesitate, make mistakes or get frustrated?
- **Interview** users about their goals, frustrations and workarounds.
- **Test** prototypes with real users before committing to production.
- **Analyse data**: support calls, returns, abandoned online orders, frequently asked questions and complaint themes all reveal UX problems.

Opinions are a starting point for hypotheses. Research decides.

## Let people try

For many products, a **trial** is the most powerful UX tool. Free trials of software, demonstration units of equipment, samples of materials, test drives and fitting rooms all let customers experience the product before committing. Trials reduce perceived risk, show value directly and reveal UX problems early. The confidence a trial creates often converts directly into a sale.

## User experience in a shop or showroom

Physical businesses can design experience just as deliberately as websites:

- **Layout**: entrances, exits, aisles and displays that make it easy to find products and move through the space.
- **Speed**: how quickly a customer can find what they need, get help and pay.
- **Convenience**: services that remove effort. For example, a grocery or hardware store can let regular customers send their list by message, prepare the order in advance and have it ready for collection.
- **Contactless options**: some retailers have experimented with stores where customers pick items and pay automatically without a checkout. Even simple measures, such as tap-to-pay, click-and-collect and pre-ordering, reduce friction.

## User experience on the phone

Phone interactions are a major part of experience for many businesses. Good practice:

- Answer promptly, or call back quickly.
- Keep automated phone menus short and logical, with an easy route to a person.
- Use automation where it genuinely helps. Some utilities let registered customers reorder a regular service with a single keypress, then send a payment link before delivery. The interaction takes seconds.
- Give staff the information and authority to resolve common issues on the first call.

## User experience online

Online customers expect speed: fast pages, fast search, fast checkout, fast responses and fast delivery. Small differences in speed and ease decide which service people use. In food delivery and similar markets, the app that gets customers from opening to ordering in the fewest taps often wins, even when the food on offer is similar.

Key online UX principles:

- **Fast loading** on mobile as well as desktop.
- **Clear navigation and search** so users find what they need in a few clicks.
- **Simple forms** with only necessary fields, sensible defaults and helpful error messages.
- **Transparent information**: prices, lead times, specifications and policies easy to find.
- **Guest checkout** or quick enquiry options rather than forced account creation.
- **Accessibility** for people with disabilities, which benefits everyone.

## Hardware and software together

Many products combine physical and digital elements. A phone, a connected machine or a smart appliance has hardware you hold and software that makes it work. If either is poor, the experience suffers. Excellent hardware with frustrating software is still frustrating. Companies known for excellent UX design hardware and software together, around what the user is trying to achieve.

## User experience in business-to-business

Business customers are people too, and they are usually busy. UX for business customers covers:

- **Specification and selection**: clear product information, drawings, models and data sheets that make it easy to choose the right item.
- **Quoting**: fast, clear quotes with all the information needed for approval.
- **Ordering**: simple reordering, online portals or easy email ordering, with confirmation.
- **Documentation**: delivery notes, certificates, manuals and invoices that are accurate and easy to understand.
- **Installation and use**: clear instructions, quick-start guides and training.
- **Support**: easy access to technical help and spare parts.

A supplier that is easy to deal with saves customers time and reduces errors. That is real value, and it can justify a price premium.

## Small touches that people remember

Some of the most memorable experiences come from small human touches: remembering a customer's name, a follow-up call to check everything works, a helpful suggestion, or staff going beyond their job to help. Some online retailers have become known for customer service staff who help with almost any request, including ones unrelated to a purchase. These gestures cost little and build strong emotional connections.

## Accessibility is good UX for everyone

Designing for people with disability, including vision, hearing, mobility and cognitive impairment, improves experience for all users. Clear contrast, readable text sizes, captions on videos, logical layouts, keyboard-accessible websites, plain language and physical access to premises help everyone, including older customers, people using phones in bright sunlight and workers wearing gloves on a factory floor. In Australia, the *Disability Discrimination Act 1992* also makes accessibility a legal consideration for goods, services and premises, and international web accessibility guidelines (WCAG) provide a practical standard for websites.

## Designing better UX: a practical process

1. **Map the journey.** List every step a customer goes through, from first discovering you to repeat purchase, including physical, phone and online touchpoints.
2. **Find the friction.** At each step, identify delays, confusion, errors and frustration, using observation, feedback and data.
3. **Prioritise.** Focus on the steps that cause the most frustration or lost sales.
4. **Simplify.** Remove unnecessary steps, fields, features and decisions.
5. **Prototype and test** improvements with real users.
6. **Implement, measure and repeat.** Track measures such as task completion, time taken, errors, abandonment, support calls and satisfaction.

## A worked example: making reordering easy

A distributor of industrial fasteners and consumables finds that many trade customers reorder the same items regularly, usually by phoning or emailing lists with incomplete part numbers. Orders are often wrong, and staff spend hours clarifying them.

The business maps the reorder journey and makes several changes:

- Each customer receives a personalised reorder sheet listing their regular items, with correct part numbers and current prices.
- A simple online form lets customers tick items and quantities from their own list.
- Order confirmations are sent automatically, with expected delivery dates.
- Frequently bought items are bundled into standard kits for common jobs.
- Customers can opt in to automatic replenishment for high-use consumables.

Order errors drop substantially, staff time spent clarifying orders falls, and customers report that ordering now takes minutes instead of a phone call and follow-up emails. Several customers consolidate more of their purchasing with the distributor because it is simply easier.

## Instructions and documentation are part of the experience

For physical products and equipment, the experience depends heavily on the documents that come with them. Manuals, installation guides, labels and drawings are often written by engineers for engineers, and can leave users confused. Good documentation UX means:

- **Quick-start guides** that cover the first few tasks a user needs, on one or two pages, with pictures.
- **Task-based structure**, organised around what users want to do ("replace the filter", "change the blade") rather than around the product's components.
- **Visuals over text**: photos, diagrams and short videos.
- **Plain language**, with technical terms explained.
- **Clear safety warnings** placed where they are relevant.
- **Labels on the product itself** for controls, connection points and maintenance items.

For manufacturers, clear documentation reduces support calls, installation errors, returns and warranty claims, so it is also a cost saving.

## Measuring user experience

UX improvements should be measured, not assumed. Useful measures include:

- **Task success rate**: the percentage of users who complete a key task, such as placing an order or installing a product, without help.
- **Time on task**: how long the task takes.
- **Error rate**: mistakes made, such as wrong items ordered or incorrect installations.
- **Abandonment rate**: users who start but do not finish, for example online checkouts or quote forms.
- **Support contacts**: questions and calls about how to do things, a direct signal of UX problems.
- **Customer effort score**: how easy customers say it was to deal with you.
- **Repeat use**: whether customers come back and use the process again, the ultimate sign of a repeatable experience.

Measure before and after each change, so you know what worked.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is UX only relevant to technology businesses?** No. Any business that customers interact with has a user experience, whether it is a hardware store, a machine shop, a clinic or a consultancy. Designing it deliberately is a competitive advantage.

**Do small businesses need UX specialists?** Not necessarily. Watching real customers, reading feedback and simplifying steps can be done by anyone. For complex software or products, specialist UX designers add significant value.

**How do we convince colleagues to remove features?** Show evidence: usage data, support calls and observations of real users struggling. It is easier to remove a feature when everyone can see it gets in the way.

**What is the quickest UX win for most businesses?** Usually the enquiry and ordering process. Shortening forms, clarifying information and responding faster improve the experience at the moment customers decide whether to buy.

**How do we balance simplicity with powerful features for expert users?** Make common tasks simple and put advanced features one step away. This approach, sometimes called progressive disclosure, serves both groups.

## Common UX mistakes

- **Feature creep**: adding features rather than improving core tasks.
- **Designing for the business's convenience** rather than the user's, such as forms that mirror internal systems rather than how customers think.
- **Ignoring mobile users.**
- **Hiding important information**, such as prices, lead times and contact details.
- **Never watching real users** use the product.
- **Treating UX as a one-off project** rather than continuous improvement.

## Summary

User experience is how easy and pleasant it is for people to use your products, services and processes. Make each step clear, remove dead ends and give people a consistent experience when they return. Remove features and steps users do not need, design from research rather than opinion, and offer trials. Apply UX thinking to shops, phone services, online channels and business-to-business processes such as quoting, ordering and support. Map the customer journey, find and remove friction, test with real users and keep improving. Customers who get used to an easy experience rarely want to leave it.
