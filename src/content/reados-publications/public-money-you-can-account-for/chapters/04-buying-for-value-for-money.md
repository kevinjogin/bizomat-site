The condition survey in Chapter 3 told the Asset Programs Branch what its 46 facilities need. The next step is to buy the maintenance that keeps them safe and working. It is the largest commitment in the branch's year, and the one most likely to be examined later.

The cheapest tender is easy to defend in a corridor and hard to defend in an audit. The Commonwealth Procurement Rules (CPRs) ask a different question: which offer gives the best value for public money, judged on the criteria you published, over the whole life of the contract?

## The core rule

The [CPRs](https://www.legislation.gov.au/F2025L01263/latest/text) put it plainly: "Achieving value for money is the core rule of the CPRs. Officials responsible for a procurement must be satisfied, after reasonable enquiries, that the procurement achieves a value for money outcome." Procurements should encourage competition and use public resources in an efficient, effective, economical and ethical way. They should support accountable and transparent decisions, engage appropriately with risk, and be commensurate with the scale and scope of the business requirement.

The CPRs also say that "Price is not the sole factor when assessing value for money." Officials must consider financial and non-financial costs and benefits, including:

- quality and fitness for purpose;
- the supplier's relevant experience, performance history and ethical conduct;
- flexibility, meaning innovation and adaptability over the life of the contract;
- environmental sustainability;
- whole-of-life costs.

For procurements above A$1 million (A$7.5 million for construction services), officials must also consider the economic benefit of the procurement to the Australian economy, with some exceptions.

## Which rules apply

Every procurement by a non-corporate Commonwealth entity follows Division 1 of the CPRs, the rules for all procurements, at any value. The additional rules in Division 2 also apply when the estimated value is at or above the relevant procurement threshold and no Appendix A exemption applies. For non-corporate entities the threshold is A$125,000, except for construction services, where it is A$7.5 million. "Where the CPRs refer to thresholds and values, these are inclusive of the Goods and Services Tax (GST)." Every amount in this chapter includes GST.

Below the threshold, a newer rule applies. For a procurement with an expected value at or above A$10,000 and below the threshold, other than a standing offer, non-corporate entities must invite only Australian businesses to make submissions. An official may decide that is not appropriate, but must document the basis. Separately, non-corporate entities must use the Commonwealth Contracting Suite for contracts under A$200,000.

The CPRs also state the Government's commitment on small and medium enterprises (SMEs). The commitment is for non-corporate entities to procure at least 25 per cent of contracts by value from SMEs for contracts up to A$1 billion, with a target of 40 per cent by value for contracts up to A$20 million. That commitment shapes an agency's buying overall. NRIO's evaluation plans do not turn it into a score in a single tender.

This is an illustration. NRIO estimates the maintenance contract at about A$1,200,000 over the longest possible term: three years plus two one-year options. Central procurement confirms early that the work is not construction services, so the A$125,000 threshold applies. Settle that question first: at this value, the answer decides whether Division 2 applies at all. Division 2 does apply, so NRIO uses an open tender. The value is above A$1 million, so the evaluation must also consider the economic benefit to the Australian economy. The contract is well above A$200,000, so the Contracting Suite rule does not decide its form; central procurement chooses the contract terms with NRIO's legal area.

## Going to market

Each entity keeps an annual procurement plan, and open tenders are published on AusTender. An open tender must stay open "at least 25 days" from publication. That can be cut to no less than 10 days, but only in the circumstances the CPRs list. The CPRs add that time limits "should not be treated as default time limits". This is an illustration. NRIO allows 30 days, because tenderers need to visit remote sites before they can price them honestly.

Request documentation should include the evaluation criteria, so that submissions are assessed "on a fair, common and appropriately transparent basis". The work that makes this possible happens before release. Grace drafts an evaluation plan, and the Branch Manager, who will be the delegate for the final commitment, approves it. The plan fixes:

- the criteria and weights: technical response 40%, capability and past performance 25%, price 35%;
- a scoring scale from 0 to 10, with a description of what earns each band;
- the price basis: the five-year whole-of-life cost to NRIO, with call-outs priced at each tenderer's rates on a planning figure of 160 call-outs a year, taken from NRIO's job records and given to every tenderer;
- the panel: the Facilities Director as chair, a regional site coordinator and Tom. Grace advises on procurement and is the only contact for tenderers. Lena oversees the process and keeps the record.

In the CPRs, "ethical" relates to "honesty, integrity, probity, diligence, fairness and consistency". Officials must act ethically throughout, including "recognising and dealing with actual, potential and perceived conflicts of interest and not accepting inappropriate gifts or hospitality". Every panel member signs a declaration of interests before seeing any tender (Chapter 7 covers what happens when someone has something to declare). Questions from tenderers go to Grace, and her answers go to every tenderer.

"Officials must maintain for each procurement a level of documentation commensurate with the scale, scope and risk of the procurement." For a five-year contract across 46 sites, that means the procurement plan, the evaluation plan, the declarations, each member's scores and the agreed scores with reasons, the whole-of-life calculation, the value-for-money report and the delegate's approval. A A$3,000 purchase needs far less. The CPRs also require processes to identify, analyse, allocate and treat procurement risk, with effort to match. Here the main risk is staffing remote sites in the wet season, which Chapter 6 takes up.

## Whole-of-life cost, not headline price

The CPRs give examples of whole-of-life costs: the initial purchase price, maintenance and operating costs, transition out costs, licensing costs, additional features procured later, consumables, and decommissioning, remediation and disposal costs. For a maintenance services contract, four matter most: the fixed fee for planned maintenance, reactive call-outs, the cost of starting, and the cost of leaving.

This is an illustration. Two compliant tenders reach the final evaluation.

- **Coastline Facility Services** charges a fixed fee of A$3,600 per site per year. Across 46 sites that is A$165,600 a year, or A$828,000 over five years. That is the number on the front of its tender, A$92,000 below Ridgeway's. Its call-out rate is A$480 and its transition-in charge A$40,000. It keeps asset and job records in its own system and hands them over only as a file export. NRIO's asset team estimates A$30,000 to convert that export into NRIO's asset system when the contract ends.
- **Ridgeway Maintenance** charges A$4,000 per site per year: A$184,000 a year, A$920,000 over five years. Its call-out rate is A$375 and its transition-in charge A$20,000. It works directly in NRIO's asset system, so there is nothing to convert at the end.

The planning figure gives 160 × 5 = 800 call-outs over five years.

Coastline's fixed fee saves A$18,400 a year. Its call-out rate is A$105 higher, which at 160 call-outs a year costs A$16,800 a year more. Over five years Coastline saves A$92,000 on the fee and spends A$84,000 more on call-outs, A$20,000 more to start and A$30,000 to leave. Net, it costs A$42,000 more.

An estimate this important deserves a test. Before any call-outs, Coastline costs A$898,000 and Ridgeway A$940,000. The A$42,000 gap divided by the A$105 difference in call-out rates gives 400 call-outs. Ridgeway costs less whenever there are more than 400 call-outs over five years, which is 80 a year, half the planning figure. The panel records the test and notes that the planning figure comes from NRIO's own job records.

## The weighted evaluation

Each panel member scores the tenders alone first. The panel then meets, agrees a score for each criterion and writes down the reasons.

- **Technical response (40%):** Ridgeway 8, Coastline 7. Ridgeway bases crews in two regional towns and commits to response times for remote sites in the wet season. Coastline relies on crews travelling from a coastal base.
- **Capability and past performance (25%):** Coastline 8, Ridgeway 7. Coastline has run more contracts of this size, and its referees reported strong performance. Ridgeway's referees were positive, but about smaller contracts.
- **Price (35%):** scored from whole-of-life cost, as the plan says. The lowest whole-of-life cost scores 10, and the other scores in proportion.

```figure
type: formula
title: Weighted score for each tender
formula: S = 0.40 × T + 0.25 × K + 0.35 × P
formula: P = 10 × L ÷ C
- S | weighted score out of 10
- T | technical response score, 0 to 10
- K | capability and past performance score, 0 to 10
- P | price score, 0 to 10
- L | lowest whole-of-life cost among compliant tenders
- C | this tender's whole-of-life cost
step: Coastline: P = 10 × 1,240,000 ÷ 1,282,000 = 9.67
step: Coastline: S = 2.80 + 2.00 + 3.39 = 8.19
step: Ridgeway: P = 10 × 1,240,000 ÷ 1,240,000 = 10.00
step: Ridgeway: S = 3.20 + 1.75 + 3.50 = 8.45
caption: This is an illustration. Each part is rounded to two decimal places at the end. Ridgeway leads by 0.26.
```

A weighted score informs the judgement. It does not replace it. The margin here is narrow, so the panel tests it. If Ridgeway's technical score were 7 instead of 8, its total would be 2.80 + 1.75 + 3.50 = 8.05, and Coastline would rank first. The reasons for that one point therefore carry the decision, and the report sets them out in full, with page references to each tender.

The report also records two other matters. The first is the panel's consideration of the economic benefit to the Australian economy, which the CPRs require above A$1 million; both tenderers proposed Australian workforces and suppliers. The second is the main risk. Ridgeway's plan still depends on recruiting regional staff before each wet season, and that risk goes on the register with an owner.

The panel recommends Ridgeway Maintenance at a total contract value of A$1,240,000 over five years, if both options are taken. That is above Lena's A$250,000 limit and the Facilities Director's A$500,000, so the Branch Manager (limit A$2,000,000) is the delegate. The Branch Manager reads the report, asks two questions about the call-out estimate, is satisfied that the outcome is value for money, and approves and records the commitment as Chapter 3 describes. Once the contract is signed, Tom reports it on AusTender within 42 days, because it is above the A$10,000 reporting threshold. Coastline is told the outcome and offered a debrief, as NRIO's procedures provide.

The CPRs require complaints to be handled through timely, equitable and non-discriminatory procedures, and covered procurements can be challenged under the Government Procurement (Judicial Review) Act 2018. Following the published criteria exactly is the best protection.

## When it goes wrong: a price-only award

This is an illustration. Imagine the evaluation is running late. The panel finds both tenders compliant, and someone points out that Coastline is A$92,000 cheaper. The panel recommends Coastline on price alone, and the delegate signs.

Three things have gone wrong. First, the decision departs from what tenderers were told. The request documents gave 65% of the weight to technical response and capability, and said price would be judged on whole-of-life cost. Awarding on the headline fee ignores both. That is not a fair, common and transparent basis, and it invites a complaint from Ridgeway.

Second, the cost is wrong. On the planning figure, the A$92,000 saving is consumed by A$84,000 of extra call-outs, A$20,000 of extra transition-in and A$30,000 at exit: 92,000 − 84,000 − 20,000 − 30,000 = −42,000. NRIO pays A$42,000 more over five years for a weaker technical response.

Third, the record cannot support the decision. The CPRs say price is not the sole factor, and the delegate must be satisfied after reasonable enquiries. A one-line recommendation that names only price shows neither.

A quieter version of the same error keeps the weights but scores price on the headline fee. Ridgeway's price score becomes 10 × 828,000 ÷ 920,000 = 9.00. Its total falls to 3.20 + 1.75 + 3.15 = 8.10, while Coastline's rises to 2.80 + 2.00 + 3.50 = 8.30. Coastline "wins" on a price basis the plan never set.

What should have happened: the panel follows the approved evaluation plan, in full, however late it is running. If a panel member believes the plan is wrong after release, the panel stops and asks central procurement before anyone scores anything.

## Exercise: score a third tender correctly

This is an illustration. A third tender, called Tenderer C in the report, arrived on time. It offers a fixed fee of A$3,200 per site per year and a transition-in charge of A$25,000, but it left the call-out rate blank. NRIO's request documents said a tender that did not price every item in the schedule of rates would be excluded from evaluation, and that prices could not be added after closing. One panel member suggests scoring it anyway, because it looks cheapest by far.

Decide how the panel should treat Tenderer C and what it should record. Then show what would happen to the other two tenders' price scores if C's incomplete price were let in as its whole-of-life cost.

### Worked answer

The panel excludes Tenderer C under the published request documents and does not score it. It does not estimate the missing rate, and it does not invite a rate after closing, which would give C a chance the other tenderers did not get. Grace confirms the exclusion with central procurement before the report is finalised. The record shows the date and time of receipt, the missing item, the clause applied, who decided and the advice received. The tender stays on file, and NRIO tells Tenderer C the reason when it announces the outcome.

Letting C in would distort every price score. Its incomplete price is A$3,200 × 46 × 5 = A$736,000, plus A$25,000, which is A$761,000 with no call-outs at all. That figure would become the lowest cost, L.

| Tender | Price score, P | Weighted score, S |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ridgeway | 10 × 761,000 ÷ 1,240,000 = 6.14 | 3.20 + 1.75 + 2.15 = 7.10 |
| Coastline | 10 × 761,000 ÷ 1,282,000 = 5.94 | 2.80 + 2.00 + 2.08 = 6.88 |
| Tenderer C | 10.00 | 3.50 from price before any other score |

A tender that left out every call-out would set the yardstick for two complete ones and collect the full 3.50 price points. Scores are rounded to two decimal places at the end, from unrounded values. The correct result does not change: Ridgeway 8.45, Coastline 8.19, and a recommendation for Ridgeway at A$1,240,000.
