Business owners and managers deal with law every day, usually without thinking of it as law. A purchase order, a lease, an employment arrangement, a product warranty, a complaint from a customer and a letter from a regulator all sit inside a legal system with its own structure and logic. Most of the time that structure stays in the background. When something goes wrong, it suddenly matters a great deal: which rules apply, where they come from, who decides a dispute and how long it will take.

Many expensive misunderstandings come from a confused picture of the system itself. People quote overseas cases or television dramas, assume a regulator's guidance is the law, treat an old court decision as settled when it has been overtaken, or start proceedings in the wrong forum. A basic map of how Australian commercial law works helps a business ask better questions, use advisers more efficiently and avoid several common traps.

This article provides that map in general terms: the sources of law, how court decisions bind later cases, the court and tribunal structure for commercial disputes, the role of regulators and standards, and practical ways to find the rules that apply. It is general information, not legal advice, and it simplifies a system with many exceptions.

## Two layers of government, one Constitution

Australia is a federation. The **Commonwealth Constitution** divides law-making power between the Commonwealth and the six states, with the territories exercising powers granted by the Commonwealth. This matters to business because some areas are largely governed by Commonwealth law and others by state or territory law, and many areas involve both.

| Area | Mainly governed by | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Companies and financial services | Commonwealth | *Corporations Act 2001*, administered by ASIC |
| Competition and consumer protection | Commonwealth law, applied nationally | *Competition and Consumer Act 2010*, including the Australian Consumer Law |
| Taxation | Mostly Commonwealth, some state taxes | Income tax and GST (Commonwealth); payroll tax and stamp duty (states) |
| Employment | Largely Commonwealth for most private employers | *Fair Work Act 2009*, modern awards |
| Contracts and civil wrongs | Mainly the common law, with state statutes | Sale of goods Acts, civil liability Acts, limitation Acts |
| Property, planning and building | States and territories | Land titles, planning approvals, building legislation |
| Work health and safety | States and territories, mostly under harmonised model laws | State WHS Acts and regulations |
| Construction payments | States and territories | Security of payment Acts |

The **Australian Consumer Law** shows how the layers interact. It is set out in a schedule to a Commonwealth Act but also applies as a law of each state and territory, so the same consumer protections apply across the country, enforced by the ACCC and state fair trading agencies.

## Where law comes from

Australian law comes from three main sources, which work together.

```figure
type: tree
title: Three sources of Australian law
caption: They operate together within a federal structure that divides responsibility between the Commonwealth and the states.
- Australian law
  - Legislation | statutes made by parliament
  - Common law | developed by judges through decided cases
  - Equity | principles that soften the common law and prevent unconscionable conduct
```

### Legislation

**Legislation** (statutes or Acts) is law made by parliament. It is the most powerful source of law within constitutional limits: if a valid statute conflicts with a common law rule, the statute generally prevails. Acts often authorise **delegated legislation**, such as regulations, rules and legislative instruments, which carry the detail. Modern awards, building codes adopted by legislation and many technical requirements reach business through this route.

When a court interprets a statute, it looks at the text, its context and the purpose the legislation was meant to serve. The words matter, but they are read in light of what the Act is trying to achieve, not in isolation.

### The common law

The **common law** is law developed by judges through decided cases. Large parts of commercial law, including most of the law of contract and the law of negligence, are common law, although statutes have modified them in many places. Common law develops incrementally. Each decision applies existing principles to new facts and occasionally refines or extends them.

### Equity

**Equity** is a body of principles developed historically by separate courts to soften the rigidity of the common law and prevent unconscionable conduct. Today the same courts apply both, but equitable doctrines remain distinct. Equity is the source of trusts, fiduciary duties, estoppel, relief against unconscionable conduct and remedies such as injunctions, specific performance, rescission and rectification. Equitable remedies are discretionary, which means the court considers the conduct of the party seeking them, including whether it acted promptly and fairly.

## How precedent works

Common law depends on **precedent**: the principle that courts follow earlier decisions so that like cases are decided alike. This gives the law predictability, but it operates with rules that are often misunderstood.

- **Binding precedent.** A court is generally bound by the reasoning of courts above it in the same hierarchy. Every Australian court is bound by decisions of the High Court. A state trial court is bound by its own state's Court of Appeal.
- **Persuasive precedent.** Decisions from courts at the same level, from other states, or from overseas courts can be persuasive but are not binding. English decisions were once highly influential and are still cited, but Australian law has diverged in many areas, so an English or American case is not a reliable guide to the Australian position.
- **The reason for the decision.** What binds is the legal reasoning necessary for the decision, often called the *ratio decidendi*, not every remark the judge made. Observations made in passing (*obiter dicta*) may be persuasive, especially from the High Court, but are not strictly binding.
- **Facts matter.** A precedent applies to similar facts. Much legal argument is about whether an earlier case is relevantly similar or can be distinguished.
- **Law changes.** Statutes may override a line of cases, and higher courts may overrule lower ones. An old case is only useful if it remains good law.

For a business, the practical point is that "a case said" is not the end of an argument. The questions are which court decided it, whether it is binding where your dispute will be heard, whether it is still good law and whether its facts are close to yours.

```figure
type: checklist
title: Before relying on "a case said"
caption: Only binding, current and relevant decisions carry real weight; overseas cases are rarely a reliable guide.
- ? Which court decided it? | higher courts bind lower ones in the same hierarchy
- ? Is it binding where your dispute will be heard? | or only persuasive

```

### Reading a case citation

Court decisions are usually cited in a standard form. A citation such as *Mann v Paterson Constructions Pty Ltd* [2019] HCA 32 tells you the parties, the year in square brackets, the court (HCA is the High Court of Australia) and the decision number for that year. Reported decisions may also carry references to law report series. Common court abbreviations include FCA (Federal Court), FCAFC (Full Federal Court), NSWSC (Supreme Court of New South Wales), NSWCA (New South Wales Court of Appeal) and VSC (Supreme Court of Victoria).

## The courts that hear commercial disputes

Each state and territory has its own court hierarchy, and there is a separate federal system. The broad structure is similar everywhere.

| Level | State and territory courts | Federal courts | Typical commercial work |
|---|---|---|---|
| Final appeal | High Court of Australia | High Court of Australia | Appeals only by special leave, on questions of public importance |
| Intermediate appeal | Court of Appeal (in most states) | Full Court of the Federal Court | Appeals from trial decisions |
| Superior trial courts | Supreme Courts | Federal Court of Australia | Large or complex commercial disputes, corporations matters, urgent injunctions |
| Intermediate trial courts | District or County Courts (in larger states) | Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia | Mid-sized claims; some federal matters such as certain bankruptcy and employment claims |
| Lower courts | Local or Magistrates Courts | | Smaller debt and civil claims, usually with simpler procedures |

Monetary limits and the precise allocation of work differ between jurisdictions and change from time to time, so check the current position for the court you are considering.

```figure

shape: pyramid
title: The court hierarchy for commercial disputes
caption: State and territory courts on the left of each pair, federal courts on the right; limits and allocations vary by jurisdiction.
- High Court of Australia | final appeal, only by special leave
- Court of Appeal · Full Federal Court | appeals from trial decisions
- *Supreme Courts · Federal Court | large or complex commercial disputes, urgent injunctions
- District or County Courts · Federal Circuit and Family Court | mid-sized claims, some federal matters
- Local or Magistrates Courts | smaller debt and civil claims
```

**Federal jurisdiction** is broader than many people expect. The Federal Court hears matters arising under Commonwealth laws such as the Corporations Act and the Australian Consumer Law, and commercial disputes often include such claims alongside contract claims. A cross-vesting scheme allows matters to be transferred between federal and state courts, so a dispute is less likely to fail simply because it started in the wrong place, but forum choice still affects cost, speed and procedure.

## Tribunals

Alongside the courts, each state and territory has civil and administrative tribunals, such as the NSW Civil and Administrative Tribunal (NCAT), the Victorian Civil and Administrative Tribunal (VCAT) and the Queensland Civil and Administrative Tribunal (QCAT). Tribunals often deal with consumer claims, residential building disputes, small claims, tenancy and many administrative decisions. Their procedures are usually simpler and cheaper than court, and legal representation may be limited.

Federal bodies also review government decisions, and employment disputes may go to the Fair Work Commission, which deals with matters such as unfair dismissal applications and enterprise agreements.

For a small business, a tribunal is often the realistic forum for a consumer or building dispute, and it may be the forum a customer uses against the business.

## Regulators, guidance and standards

Several bodies shape commercial behaviour without being courts:

- **Regulators** such as the ACCC, ASIC, the Australian Taxation Office, the Fair Work Ombudsman, the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner and state fair trading, building and safety regulators administer and enforce legislation. They can investigate, issue notices and penalties within their powers and bring proceedings in court.
- **Regulatory guidance** explains how a regulator interprets the law and intends to act. It is valuable and often decisive in practice, but it is not itself the law. A court may interpret the legislation differently.
- **Technical standards**, such as Australian Standards, are documents prepared by standards bodies. A standard becomes legally mandatory only when legislation or a contract calls it up, for example through the National Construction Code. Otherwise it is evidence of good practice, which can still matter in negligence and contract disputes. The [which edition of a standard applies](/reados/collections/risk-controls-and-assurance/which-edition-of-a-standard-applies/) article explains why the referenced edition matters.

## Time limits

Legal rights do not last forever. Each state and territory has **limitation legislation** setting periods within which claims must be started. For many contract and negligence claims the period is six years, but it varies by jurisdiction and type of claim, there are different rules for personal injury, and some statutes set much shorter periods for particular claims or notices. Building legislation, security of payment schemes and consumer claims all have their own time requirements. Missing a limitation period can extinguish an otherwise strong claim, so ask about time limits early.

## Finding the rules that apply

A business does not need to research law itself, but knowing where the rules live helps it brief advisers well and check what it is told.

- **Commonwealth legislation** is published on the [Federal Register of Legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/), including current versions and historical compilations.
- **State and territory legislation** is published on each jurisdiction's official legislation website.
- **Court decisions** from Australian courts are freely available through [AustLII](https://www.austlii.edu.au/), and many courts publish their own judgments.
- **Regulator websites** publish guidance, fact sheets and enforcement updates.

Three habits prevent many errors. Always check that you are reading the **current version** of an Act, as amendments are frequent. Check **which jurisdiction** governs your issue. And treat **summaries**, including articles like this one, as a starting point for questions, not as the answer.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The business and events are hypothetical.

A small Queensland business that manufactures commercial shelving sells a system to a retailer in New South Wales for $38,000. After installation, several shelves deflect under load, and the retailer claims the product is not fit for purpose. The retailer's email cites an American case about product liability and threatens to "go to the ACCC".

Using the map, the business can frame better questions before it calls its lawyer. Which contract terms apply, and which state's law governs the contract? Is the retailer a "consumer" under the Australian Consumer Law for this purchase, given that the price is below the $100,000 threshold, and if so, which guarantees apply and what remedies follow? Does an Australian Standard for storage racking or shelving apply because the contract or a regulation calls it up, or is it only evidence of good practice? Is the American case relevant? Almost certainly not as precedent. The ACCC can investigate systemic issues but does not usually resolve individual disputes. Where would a claim be heard? Possibly a tribunal or a lower court, depending on the jurisdiction and amount. And what time limits apply?

With these questions answered, the business can respond to the retailer accurately, focus its technical investigation on the load assumptions and installation, and use legal advice efficiently.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Identify the governing law of each important contract.** Contracts often state which state's law applies and which courts have jurisdiction. Check yours.
- **Know your regulators.** Understand which regulators oversee your activities and read their guidance for your industry.
- **Distinguish guidance from law.** Treat regulator guidance and industry codes seriously, but recognise what is legally binding and what is not.
- **Ignore overseas law unless advised otherwise.** Foreign cases and statutes are rarely a reliable guide to Australian obligations.
- **Ask about forum and time limits early.** Where a dispute would be heard and how long you have to act can shape strategy from the first day.
- **Keep a register of key legal requirements.** A simple register of the Acts, regulations, standards and licences that apply to your business, with review dates, makes compliance less dependent on memory.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a single case as settled law.** A case may be from a lower court, from another jurisdiction, distinguishable on its facts or overruled.
- **Quoting American or English law.** Australian law differs in many important respects.
- **Confusing a regulator with a court.** Regulators enforce the law; courts and tribunals decide disputes.
- **Assuming standards are always mandatory.** They are mandatory only when called up by law or contract, although they can still be relevant evidence.
- **Reading an out-of-date Act.** Legislation changes often, and the version in force at the relevant time is what matters.
- **Missing time limits.** Some periods are short, and missing them can end a claim.

## Questions to ask

- Is this issue mainly governed by Commonwealth law, state law or both?
- Which state's law governs our contract, and which courts or tribunals could hear a dispute?
- Is the rule we are relying on in legislation, a court decision, equity or regulator guidance?
- If we are relying on a case, is it binding here and still good law?
- Which regulators oversee this activity, and what have they said publicly about it?
- Is the standard we are applying legally mandatory, contractually required or simply good practice?
- What time limits apply to any claim we might make or face?

## Bringing it together

Australian commercial law comes from legislation, the common law and equity, operating within a federal structure that divides responsibility between the Commonwealth and the states. Precedent gives the system predictability, but only binding, current and relevant decisions carry real weight. Courts and tribunals at different levels hear different kinds of disputes, regulators enforce the law without being courts, and standards become mandatory only when law or contract makes them so.

A business does not need to master this system, but a clear map helps it ask the right questions, brief advisers efficiently and avoid relying on the wrong sources.
