The *Managing Your Opportunities* workbook gives rapport a page of its own, with two columns: **"I am good at"** and **"I need to practise"**. There are no prescribed techniques on the page, just space for honest self-assessment. That design is quietly wise. Rapport is not a trick to perform on customers. It is a set of habits that some people have naturally and others develop through practice.

This article explains what rapport is, why it matters so much in business relationships, practical ways to build it, and the techniques that tend to backfire.

## What rapport is

Rapport is the sense of mutual understanding and ease between people. When it is present, conversations flow, people say what they really think, disagreements are handled without defensiveness, and both sides give each other the benefit of the doubt. When it is absent, conversations are guarded, information is withheld, and small problems become large ones.

Rapport is not the same as friendship, and it does not require agreement. You can have strong rapport with a customer who drives a hard bargain. What rapport provides is **trust that the other person is acting in good faith and genuinely trying to understand**.

## Why rapport matters in business

### It unlocks honest information

Customers share their real problems, budgets, doubts and internal politics with people they trust. Without that information, it is almost impossible to qualify an opportunity properly or propose the right solution. Much of what passes for sales skill is really the ability to create conditions in which customers tell you the truth.

### It reduces perceived risk

Every purchase involves some risk for the buyer: that the product will not work, that the supplier will not deliver, that they will look foolish for choosing it. Trust in the people involved reduces that risk. Customers often choose the supplier they trust over the one with a slightly better offer.

### It survives problems

Every business makes mistakes eventually. A late delivery or a fault is far easier to resolve with a customer who trusts you than with one who does not. Rapport built before a problem is what carries a relationship through it.

### It makes work better

Relationships with mutual respect and understanding are simply more pleasant and productive. That matters for the people on both sides.

## How rapport is built

Rapport is not built by any single action. It accumulates from many small signals that you are interested, reliable and on the other person's side. The following practices are among the most reliable.

### Genuine interest

The foundation of rapport is real curiosity about the other person: their work, their challenges, their goals. People can tell the difference between someone asking questions to get to their pitch and someone who actually wants to understand. Ask about their situation, and listen to the answers with attention.

### Listening, and showing that you listened

Few things build rapport faster than being genuinely heard. Listening well, covered in detail in its own article, includes small visible signals: summarising what someone said, referring back to it later, and following up on it after the meeting. When a customer hears their own words reflected accurately, they feel understood.

### Finding real common ground

Shared experiences, interests or perspectives create connection. The key word is *real*. Noticing that you both started businesses in the same industry, or both dealt with the same supplier problem, creates genuine common ground. Inventing an interest in their football team does not.

### Reliability in small things

Rapport is built as much through actions as conversation. Being on time, sending what you promised when you promised, returning calls and remembering details all signal that you can be trusted with bigger things.

### Honesty, including about limits

Telling a customer "we're not the best fit for that part of the project" or "I don't know, but I'll find out" builds more trust than claiming to do everything. Customers are generally good at spotting overpromising, and they remember who was straight with them.

### Respect for their time and constraints

Keeping meetings to the agreed length, being concise in writing and acknowledging that they have other priorities all show respect.

### Appropriate warmth

Warmth (friendliness, good humour, kindness) makes interactions easier. It should be appropriate to the person and the context. Some customers welcome personal conversation; others prefer to stay focused on business. Matching their preference is part of rapport.

### Remembering what matters to them

Noting personal and professional details (a project they mentioned, a family event, a concern they raised) and following up later shows genuine attention. A simple "How did the audit go?" at the start of the next call can do more for rapport than an hour of presentation.

## Techniques that tend to backfire

Sales training sometimes teaches rapport as a set of techniques. Some are harmless; others can damage trust when used mechanically.

**Mirroring.** People who get on well often unconsciously adopt similar postures and speech patterns. Some training encourages deliberately copying a customer's body language to build rapport. Done subtly and naturally, matching someone's pace and energy can help. Done deliberately and obviously, it feels manipulative, and people often notice.

**Excessive name use.** Using someone's name is friendly. Using it in every sentence sounds like a script.

**Forced small talk.** Small talk can ease the start of a conversation, but extended, scripted chat about weather and weekends before getting to the point can feel like a delay tactic, especially to busy people.

**Flattery.** Sincere appreciation builds rapport. Exaggerated praise undermines it.

**Pretending to agree.** Agreeing with everything a customer says to seem likeable erodes credibility. Respectful disagreement, when warranted, is a sign of honesty.

**Fake familiarity.** Treating a new contact as an old friend can feel presumptuous.

The common thread is authenticity. Techniques that support genuine interest and respect help; techniques that substitute for them tend to fail.

## Rapport with different kinds of people

People differ in what makes them comfortable. A few broad patterns are worth noticing, without treating them as rigid categories:

- **Some people value directness and efficiency.** Rapport with them comes from being concise, prepared and focused on outcomes.
- **Some people value relationships and warmth.** Rapport comes from personal connection and time to talk.
- **Some people value detail and accuracy.** Rapport comes from thoroughness, evidence and careful answers.
- **Some people value ideas and energy.** Rapport comes from enthusiasm, possibilities and big-picture conversation.

Many personality and communication frameworks describe variations on these themes. Their value is not in labelling people but in reminding you to notice how the other person prefers to communicate, and to adjust. Rapport is easier when you meet people where they are.

## Rapport under pressure

Rapport is easy when everything is going well. It is tested when there is tension: a price negotiation, a complaint, a delayed project or bad news to deliver. How you behave in those moments does more to build or destroy trust than any number of pleasant meetings.

A few principles help:

- **Raise problems early.** Customers forgive problems more readily than surprises. Telling a customer about a likely delay as soon as you know about it builds trust; letting them discover it destroys it.
- **Acknowledge their perspective first.** Before explaining your position, show that you understand theirs: "I can see why this is frustrating, especially with your launch date."
- **Separate the person from the problem.** Disagree about the issue, not about the person's competence or intentions.
- **Stay calm.** If a customer is angry, matching their emotion escalates the situation. Steady, respectful responses help it settle.
- **Focus on what happens next.** Once the problem is understood, move to options and a plan. People feel better when they can see a way forward.

Handled well, difficult moments often strengthen relationships. Customers learn that you can be trusted not just when things are easy but when they are hard.

## A worked example

A small software consultancy wins a project with a regional healthcare provider. The project lead on the customer's side is cautious and reserved, and early meetings feel stiff. This is an illustration.

The consultancy's lead resists the temptation to fill the silence with enthusiasm. Instead, she notices that the client lead asks detailed questions and prefers written summaries. She starts sending a clear, structured summary after each meeting, with decisions, risks and open questions. She answers technical questions carefully, and when she does not know something, she says so and comes back with a precise answer the next day.

Two months in, a supplier delay threatens a milestone. She tells the client lead the same day, explains the cause and presents two options. The client lead chooses one and thanks her for the early warning.

By the end of the project, the client lead is the consultancy's strongest advocate within the organisation. Rapport was built not through charm but through attention to how the client preferred to work, reliability in small things and honesty when it mattered.

## The two-column self-assessment

The workbook's "I am good at / I need to practise" format is a useful tool for developing rapport, because rapport skills vary so much between people.

Here is an example of how someone might complete it. It is illustrative only.

| I am good at | I need to practise |
| --- | --- |
| Being friendly and putting people at ease | Listening without planning my reply |
| Following up reliably after meetings | Remembering personal details between conversations |
| Explaining technical things clearly | Adjusting my pace for people who want to get straight to business |
| Admitting when I don't know something | Asking about concerns rather than waiting for them to be raised |

The value lies in the second column. Choosing one or two items and building them into a personal action plan (covered elsewhere in this series) turns self-awareness into improvement.

A useful extension is to ask a trusted colleague or manager to complete the same two columns about you. Their view often differs from your own in revealing ways.

## Building rapport over time

First meetings matter, but rapport is mostly built over repeated interactions. A few practices help sustain it:

- **Regular, low-pressure contact** that is not always about selling: sharing a useful article, checking in on a project, congratulating them on news.
- **Consistency** between what you say and what you do, across every interaction.
- **Owning mistakes** quickly and honestly when they happen.
- **Introducing them to useful people,** even when there is no direct benefit to you.
- **Remembering the relationship when there is no sale in sight.** Customers notice who stays in touch only when they want something.

## Rapport across a team

In larger relationships, rapport exists between many people on both sides. A customer may trust your salesperson but not your delivery team, or the other way around. Building rapport at several levels makes the relationship more resilient and less dependent on any one person.

This connects to a theme from the Buffett articles on this site: businesses whose value depends on a few individuals are fragile. Shared notes, introductions to colleagues and consistent standards help rapport belong to the business, not just to individuals.

## Rapport and ethics

Because rapport creates trust, it carries responsibility. Trust makes people more willing to share information and more open to recommendations. Using that openness to push a customer towards something that is not in their interest is a betrayal, and usually a short-sighted one: the customer eventually notices, and the relationship (and often the reputation) is lost.

A simple test helps. Would you be comfortable if the customer could hear your internal reasoning for every recommendation you make? If the answer is yes, rapport is working as it should: as the foundation of an honest relationship in which both sides benefit. If the answer is no, the problem is not your rapport skills but your intentions.

The most durable business relationships are built by people who use trust carefully, recommend what genuinely fits, and are willing to say "you don't need this" when it is true.

## Common mistakes

**Treating rapport as a stage to get through.** Rapport is not a preamble before the "real" conversation. It runs through the whole relationship.

**Confusing rapport with being liked.** Customers do not need to like you as a friend. They need to trust you.

**Neglecting reliability.** Warmth without follow-through destroys rapport faster than coolness with reliability.

**Using the same approach with everyone.** People differ; rapport requires attention to the individual.

**Forgetting existing customers.** Rapport fades without contact.

## Bringing it together

Rapport is the trust and ease that make honest conversation possible. It is built through genuine interest, attentive listening, real common ground, reliability, honesty and respect, and it is weakened by techniques that substitute performance for those qualities.

The workbook's two-column approach is a good model: know what you already do well, choose what to practise, and improve deliberately. Over time, rapport becomes less something you do and more a description of how you work with people.
