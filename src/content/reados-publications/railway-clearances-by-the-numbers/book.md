## About this book

Every railway runs through a tunnel of empty space. A train needs room for its own outline, room to sway, lean and bounce as it moves, room for the track beneath it to be imperfect, and room on top of all that so that a small error does not become a collision. Structures beside and over the track, from bridge piers and signal masts to platforms, walls and loading chutes, must stay outside that space, and so must trains on the next track. Getting it wrong is expensive at best: a new overbridge that has to be rebuilt, a platform that strikes doors, a transformer that cannot pass a cutting. At worst it kills.

This book is for designers, project engineers and maintainers who place things near railway track, and for planners who move unusual loads along it. It assumes basic geometry and some engineering background. It explains, with numbers, how a clearance is built up from the vehicle outward: the vehicle's outline, the extra width a curve produces at its middle and ends, the lean that cant gives it, the dynamic movements and track errors that make up the kinematic envelope, and the safety margin and service space beyond. It then covers track centres, refuges for people, infringements and how they are approved and recorded, out-of-gauge loads, and the clearance record.

## The sources and their limits

The book draws on a set of Australian Rail Track Corporation engineering standards for New South Wales, carried over from the former Rail Infrastructure Corporation standards and issued between 2004 and 2007, and on Section 7 of ARTC's Track and Civil Code of Practice for South Australia, Western Australia and Victoria, issued in 2007. Where a value from one of those documents is quoted, it is cited, and it is quoted for teaching: those issues may since have been revised or replaced. Real design must use the standard and corridor requirements that the rail infrastructure manager currently specifies for the line, and its approval.

## The running case

This is an illustration. A civil engineering consultancy is designing a new road overbridge for a regional council where the road crosses a single standard gauge main line, on a 400 m radius curve with 60 mm of cant, close to a passing loop and a short industrial siding. Later the same corridor must carry an out-of-gauge transformer on a well wagon. The consultancy's engineers must work out the clearances the bridge needs, check the track centres at the loop, provide for people at a long retaining wall, and plan the transformer move.

The consultancy, council, bridge, rolling stock and numbers are invented for teaching. They are not KEVOS® work.

## How to use the book

Chapter 1 sets out the outlines and the vocabulary. Chapter 2 deals with rolling stock and load outlines, Chapter 3 with curve throw, and Chapter 4 with cant. Chapter 5 builds the kinematic envelope, Chapter 6 adds the margins and service space to reach the structure gauge, and Chapter 7 covers track centres. Chapter 8 deals with platforms, refuges and people, Chapter 9 with infringements and out-of-gauge loads, and Chapter 10 with surveys and the clearance record. Every chapter ends with an exercise and its worked answer.

## Professional responsibility

This book is educational. Railway clearances are safety-critical. Design, construction near track, infringements and out-of-gauge moves are governed by the rail infrastructure manager's current standards, corridor strategies and approvals, and by rail safety law, and they need qualified review. Nothing here replaces those.
