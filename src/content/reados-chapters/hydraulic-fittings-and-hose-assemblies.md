Hydraulic systems are found throughout industry: presses, mobile plant, agricultural machinery, lifting equipment, injection moulding machines and countless special-purpose machines. They transmit large forces through fluid at high pressure, and their reliability depends heavily on the humble connections between pumps, valves, cylinders, pipes and hoses. A poorly chosen or badly installed fitting leaks, contaminates the system, wastes oil and creates slip hazards. A failed hose can release fluid at pressures high enough to cause serious injury.

Hydraulic connections are also a source of confusion. Several fitting families look similar but seal in completely different ways. Threads from different standards can appear to engage and then leak or fail. Hose assemblies are often replaced with whatever is available, without checking pressure rating, bend radius or compatibility. Many leaks blamed on "bad seals" are actually caused by mismatched fittings, wrong torque or poor routing.

This article explains, in general terms, how the main hydraulic connection types seal, how to identify them and avoid mixing them, how to specify hose assemblies, how to route and install them, and the cleanliness and safety practices that keep systems reliable. It is general information. Component manufacturers' specifications and applicable safety requirements must always be followed, and work on pressurised systems should only be carried out by competent people.

## How hydraulic connections seal

Every hydraulic connection has two separate jobs: **holding** the parts together against pressure and **sealing** the fluid in. In most modern fittings, the thread does the holding and a separate feature does the sealing. Understanding which feature seals is the key to understanding the fitting.

The main sealing methods are:

- **Metal-to-metal cones or flares**, where two precisely angled surfaces are drawn together by the thread.
- **Elastomeric seals**, such as O-rings in a face, groove or port, which seal as they are compressed.
- **Bonded or composite washers**, which seal between a flat face and a port.
- **Tapered threads**, where the thread itself seals through interference, usually with a sealant.

Elastomeric seals are generally more tolerant of vibration and less dependent on precise assembly torque than metal-to-metal or tapered thread seals, which is why they are widely used in high-pressure and high-vibration equipment.

## The main fitting families

| Fitting family | Sealing method | Typical features | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| JIC 37° flare | Metal-to-metal 37° cone | Unified threads; very common on mobile and industrial equipment | Can leak if over-tightened or under-tightened; looks similar to 45° flare fittings |
| O-ring face seal (ORFS) | O-ring in a flat face | Unified threads; good resistance to vibration and high pressure | Excellent leak resistance; O-ring must be present and undamaged |
| Metric 24° cone (cutting ring or O-ring cone) | Cutting ring bites into tube, or O-ring in the cone | Metric threads; light and heavy series | Common on European equipment; assembly procedure matters |
| BSPP ports and fittings | Bonded washer, O-ring or 60° cone | Parallel British pipe threads | Common in Australia, the UK and Europe |
| SAE straight thread O-ring boss | O-ring in the port | Unified threads in the port | Common on North American equipment |
| Metric ports with O-ring | O-ring in the port | Metric threads, often with an identification marking | Common on newer international equipment |
| NPTF (dryseal) taper | Thread interference | Tapered threads | Sensitive to over-tightening and vibration; often avoided in new high-pressure designs |

### Look-alike fittings

Several families can look almost identical to an untrained eye:

- **JIC 37° and SAE 45° flare** fittings have similar threads in some sizes but different cone angles. Combined, they seal only on a line and leak.
- **BSP and NPT** threads have different thread angles and pitches in most sizes. They may engage for a few turns before binding, giving a false impression of a sound connection.
- **Metric and unified threads** of similar diameter can be confused.

Identifying fittings correctly requires measuring the thread diameter and pitch with a gauge, checking the seat angle and noting any O-ring groove or identification marking. Supplier identification guides and thread gauges are inexpensive tools that prevent expensive mistakes. The [choosing fasteners and threads](/reados/collections/machine-elements-and-mechanical-design/choosing-fasteners-and-threads/) article explains thread systems and why mixing 55° and 60° threads causes problems.

## Specifying hose assemblies

A hose assembly is a hose with fittings permanently crimped or swaged on each end. Specifying it properly involves several factors, sometimes summarised by suppliers with mnemonics such as STAMPED: size, temperature, application, media, pressure, ends and delivery. In practical terms:

- **Size**: the inside diameter must suit the flow without excessive velocity or pressure drop.
- **Temperature**: both fluid and ambient temperatures, including heat from nearby components.
- **Application**: movement, flexing, abrasion, vibration and the environment around the hose.
- **Media**: compatibility of the hose and seals with the fluid, especially for fire-resistant fluids and biodegradable oils.
- **Pressure**: the working pressure, including pressure spikes, must not exceed the hose and fitting ratings.
- **Ends**: the correct fitting families and sizes at both ends, and their orientation.
- **Delivery and documentation**: length, assembly identification and any testing requirements.

Hydraulic hoses are commonly made to recognised international standards for wire-braided and spiral-reinforced types. Hose and fitting combinations should come from the same manufacturer's system, or be approved by the manufacturer, because crimp dimensions and fitting designs are matched to particular hoses. Mixing brands without approval can produce assemblies that fail at pressure.

## Rigid tube or hose

Designers often have a choice between rigid tube and flexible hose. Each has its place:

- **Rigid tube** is compact, neat, resistant to abrasion and long-lived, with lower expansion under pressure and often lower cost over long runs. It suits fixed connections between components that do not move relative to each other.
- **Hose** accommodates movement, vibration and misalignment, and simplifies installation in tight or irregular spaces. It suits connections to moving cylinders, articulated parts and components mounted on vibration isolators.

A common good practice is to use rigid tube for fixed runs and short hose sections only where movement or vibration requires them. Long hose runs used purely for convenience are harder to protect, more vulnerable to damage and more expensive to maintain over the life of the machine. Tube needs proper support, using clamps at suitable intervals to control vibration, and correct bending so that the tube's wall is not thinned or kinked.

## Routing and installation

Many hose failures result from installation rather than manufacture. Good practice includes:

- **Respect the minimum bend radius.** Bending a hose more tightly than its rating, particularly close to the fitting, damages the reinforcement and shortens life.
- **Allow for length change.** Hoses can change length slightly under pressure; leave a little slack.
- **Avoid twisting.** A twisted hose under pressure can fail early. Align the hose's lay line and use swivel fittings or adapters where needed.
- **Prevent abrasion.** Clamp hoses so they do not rub against each other or structures, and use protective sleeves where contact is unavoidable.
- **Keep away from heat.** Route hoses away from exhausts and hot surfaces, or shield them.
- **Use adapters to improve routing** rather than forcing hoses into tight bends.
- **Tighten correctly.** Use the manufacturer's torque values or turn-based methods for the fitting type. Over-tightening damages flares and threads; under-tightening leaks.

For rigid tube, correct bending, cutting square, deburring and following the fitting manufacturer's assembly procedure for cutting rings or flares are essential to a leak-free joint.

## Cleanliness

Contamination is a leading cause of hydraulic component failure. Particles from cutting tube, crimping hose, dirty fittings or careless maintenance can damage pumps, valves and seals. Good practice includes:

- capping hoses and ports during storage and installation;
- cleaning hose assemblies after cutting and crimping, for example with cleaning projectiles;
- flushing new or repaired systems according to the manufacturer's guidance;
- maintaining filtration and monitoring oil cleanliness, often expressed using the ISO 4406 cleanliness code; and
- keeping fluid storage and handling clean.

## Documenting hydraulic connections

Many fitting mistakes happen during repairs, months or years after a machine was built, when the person replacing a hose has to guess what was originally fitted. A simple record prevents that. For each machine, record the hose assemblies by identifier, with hose type, size, length, pressure rating, fitting types at each end and their orientation, along with port types on major components. Tagging hoses with an identifier that matches the record, and keeping a hydraulic circuit diagram with the machine's documentation, makes correct replacement straightforward and supports scheduled hose replacement.

## Inspection and replacement

Hoses age even when they look sound. Regular inspection should look for:

- leaks at fittings or along the hose;
- cracked, hardened or blistered cover;
- exposed or damaged reinforcement;
- kinks, flattening or twisting;
- fittings that are corroded, damaged or have moved on the hose; and
- clamps that are missing or loose.

Many organisations set replacement intervals for hoses in critical or high-risk applications, based on manufacturers' recommendations and service experience, rather than waiting for failure. Labelling hoses with installation dates helps. The [maintenance that prevents breakdowns](/reados/collections/lean-and-continuous-improvement/maintenance-that-prevents-breakdowns/) article explains how to decide which components deserve scheduled replacement.

## Safety

Hydraulic systems store energy, and a failure can release it suddenly. Key hazards include:

- **Fluid injection injuries.** A pinhole leak can inject fluid through the skin. Such injuries can appear minor at first but are medical emergencies requiring urgent specialist treatment. Never search for leaks with a hand; use cardboard or similar material.
- **Hose whip.** A hose that separates under pressure can whip violently. Restraints and shields reduce the risk in exposed locations.
- **Stored pressure.** Accumulators, trapped pressure and raised loads can release energy even when pumps are off. Isolation procedures must release or block stored energy before work begins.
- **Hot fluid and slip hazards.**

Work on hydraulic systems falls within an employer's duties under work health and safety legislation, including safe systems of work, competent workers and appropriate isolation procedures.

## A worked example

This is an illustrative example. The equipment and figures are hypothetical.

A small manufacturer operates a hydraulic press that has developed persistent leaks at three connections on the valve manifold, losing about 20 litres of oil a month and creating a slip hazard. Seals have been replaced twice without lasting improvement.

An investigation finds that during an earlier repair, one replacement hose was fitted with a 45° flare fitting on a 37° JIC adapter, which seals only on a line. A second connection has an NPT adapter forced into a BSPP port, engaging only a few threads. The third is a correct ORFS fitting, but its O-ring has been cut during installation.

The business replaces the mismatched fittings with correctly identified parts, fits new O-rings, tightens each connection to the manufacturer's values and labels the hoses with fitting types and installation dates. It buys thread and seat angle gauges and a supplier identification chart for the maintenance team, and standardises new equipment on O-ring face seal and metric O-ring port connections where practical. The leaks stop, and oil consumption returns to normal.

## How this applies to a small Australian business

- **Identify before replacing.** Measure threads and seat angles rather than guessing.
- **Standardise connection types** on new equipment where possible, preferring elastomeric seals for high pressure and vibration.
- **Buy matched hose systems.** Use hose and fittings from one approved system.
- **Route hoses properly.** Bend radius, twisting, abrasion and heat cause many failures.
- **Keep systems clean.** Cap, clean and flush.
- **Inspect and label hoses.** Replace critical hoses before they fail.
- **Train people on hazards.** Injection injuries and stored energy are serious risks.

## Common mistakes

- **Mixing 37° and 45° flares, or BSP and NPT threads.**
- **Reusing damaged O-rings** or omitting them.
- **Over-tightening flared fittings**, cracking or deforming the flare.
- **Hoses bent tightly at the fitting** or twisted during installation.
- **Mixing hose and fitting brands** without manufacturer approval.
- **Searching for leaks by hand.**
- **Ignoring contamination** during repairs.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can thread sealant fix a leaking parallel-thread fitting?** No. Parallel threads seal at a face, O-ring or washer. Sealant on the thread does not fix a damaged or missing seal and can contaminate the system.

**How often should hydraulic hoses be replaced?** It depends on the application, environment, pressure cycles and the manufacturer's guidance. Critical hoses are often replaced on a schedule; others are replaced based on inspection findings.

**Why do new machines increasingly use O-ring connections?** Because elastomeric seals tolerate vibration, pressure spikes and minor assembly variation better than metal-to-metal or tapered thread seals, which reduces leaks over the life of the equipment.

**Is it safe to make our own hose assemblies?** It can be, with the correct crimping equipment, matched hose and fittings, trained people and appropriate testing. Otherwise, use a competent hose supplier.

## Questions to ask

- Which connection types do our machines use, and do our maintenance staff know how to identify them?
- Are replacement hoses and fittings checked for type, pressure rating and compatibility?
- Do we have thread and seat angle gauges available?
- Are hoses routed within their bend radius and protected from abrasion and heat?
- How do we control contamination during repairs?
- Do our isolation procedures release stored hydraulic energy?

## Bringing it together

Hydraulic connections hold and seal fluid at high pressure, and the sealing feature varies by fitting family: metal cones and flares, O-rings in faces and ports, bonded washers or tapered threads. Look-alike fittings from different standards cause many leaks, so correct identification is the first discipline. Properly specified, matched and routed hose assemblies, correct tightening, cleanliness, regular inspection and a clear understanding of stored-energy hazards keep hydraulic systems reliable and the people working on them safe.
